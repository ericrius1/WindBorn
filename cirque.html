Carving the Cirque

Fish Hawk finally gets its valley. One pure function of (x, z) carves a glacial bowl, rings it with ridged mountains, and drops the shoreline exactly where the terrain crosses zero — no heightmap, no sculpting, the same mountain every time anyone asks.

Scroll down to read ↓

We have a lake that looks like water, a bird with working wings, and a flight model that can ride invisible rivers of lift — over an infinite, featureless sheet of water. Time to fix that. In the finished game, the valley is the level design: the cirque walls are the boundary of the playable world (mountains, not invisible walls), windward slopes are the elevators, the treeline organizes navigation, and the bowl under the water decides where fish hold. This series builds all of it, and today's post lays the one foundation everything else stands on: a terrain height function.

Two decisions before any noise gets made. First, the lake's mean surface stays at world y = 0 — every series since Glass Dawn has assumed it, and the floating bird already queries it. That makes the shoreline a definition rather than an asset: it falls wherever terrain height crosses zero. Second, the terrain is a pure function h(x, z) — no stored heightmap, no files to page in. Ask twice, get the same answer; ask on the CPU for physics or on the GPU for vertices, same answer. That property does a shocking amount of work in the posts ahead, so we guard it jealously from the first line.

Step 1 · Ridged noiseFolding randomness into crests

Everything begins with repeatable randomness. We hash each integer lattice point through a few rounds of multiply-and-xor (the murmur3 finalizer), so hash(ix, iy, seed) is statistically random but eternally deterministic. The hash picks a random unit gradient at each corner of the containing cell; dot each gradient with the offset to the sample point; and blend the four results with Perlin's quintic fade t³(6t² − 15t + 10), chosen because its first and second derivatives vanish at the lattice, so neither value nor curvature betrays where the grid lines are. That's gradient noise: smooth hills in roughly [−1, 1].

Smooth hills aren't mountains. Two more moves get us there. First the fractal sum — fBm — stacks octaves, each at double frequency and half amplitude, with the lattice rotated half a radian between octaves so no two share a grid orientation. Then the ridge fold: instead of using each octave's value n directly, bend it toward 

n ← 1 − 2·|n|        // fold: zero crossings become sharp crests

The absolute value creases the smooth function along its zero contours, and flipping it puts those creases on top. Since the zero set of gradient noise is a network of meandering lines, the fold turns rolling hills into connected, branching ridgelines — exactly the skeleton real erosion leaves behind. Our ridge parameter blends between plain fBm (0) and a fully folded field (1); the basin uses 0.85. Aside · One hash, two processors

Every demo on this page displaces its vertices on the GPU, but physics will ask the CPU. The trick that keeps them identical: JavaScript's Math.imul wraps multiplication modulo 2³² exactly like WGSL's u32 arithmetic, so the murmur3 hash produces bit-identical output on both processors — same gradient at every lattice point, same mountain to within f32 rounding. The terrain library maintains the height function twice, operation for operation, and the pair must change together. It's the least glamorous code in the project and some of the most important.

Step 2 · Domain warpNo circle survives

Ridged fBm has a tell: faint axis-aligned regularity inherited from the lattice, and a sameness everywhere. The cure is to bend the coordinates before the noise ever sees them. Sample two independent fBm channels u and v at low frequency, and evaluate the mountains at a displaced position: 

h(x, z) = ridged( x + A·u(x, z),  z + A·v(x, z) )

This is domain warping, and it's the difference between procedural-looking and geological-looking. Straight ridges shear into curves, valleys swerve, and — crucially for us — every radial shape we build in the next step gets warped by the same displacement, so the lake won't be a circle and the rim won't be a ring. The basin uses A = 110 m of warp at a 640 m wavelength. Slide it up from zero and watch the terrain stop remembering its lattice.

Step 3 · The skeletonA valley is a radial idea

Noise gives texture, not place. The shape of the world — a lake in a bowl, ringed by a crest, draining through a notch — is authored as a radial skeleton built from a handful of smoothstep terms, all functions of the warped distance from the origin r. From the inside out: 

bowl   = −26 m · (1 − smoothstep(0.45, 1.0, r_bowl))   // the tarn, deepest off-center
shore  = clamp(r − 320, −90, 140) · 0.065              // a beach ramp through y = 0
rise   = 360 m · smoothstep(320, 1150, r)              // climb to the crest ring
settle = −170 m · smoothstep(1150, 2700, r)            // ease down beyond, stay mountainous

The beach term deserves a sentence: it's a clamped linear ramp crossing zero at the shore radius with a definite 6.5% grade, so the zero crossing is a crisp line instead of a marshy ambiguity. On top of the skeleton goes the warped ridged noise from Steps 1–2, scaled by 290 m and masked by an envelope that is zero near the lake and full beyond 700 m — mountains grow with distance from the water, never under it. Last, an angular wedge clamps the rim down to a low lip 4 m above the lake, with a valley floor descending away downstream: every tarn drains somewhere, and that outlet notch is a landmark you'll navigate by from the air. Aside · Cirques and tarns

The shape we're carving is real. A cirque is the amphitheater a glacier excavates at its head: ice plucks rock from the wall it leans against (the headwall) and grinds an overdeepened floor below. When the ice retreats, the bowl fills with meltwater — a tarn — often dammed by the moraine ridge the glacier left at its toe. That's why our deepest sounding sits off-center near the steepest wall, and why the lake drains over a low lip rather than through a gorge: we put the bowl's asymmetry and the outlet exactly where the ice would have.

Step 4 · The shorelineWhere the function crosses zero

Now the payoff for keeping water at y = 0: we never author a shoreline. The demo below paints every point where |h| ≈ 0 gold — that meandering line is the coast, wobbling between roughly 230 and 420 m from the origin because the domain warp bent the shore radius before the beach ramp saw it. A gaussian band of fine noise, 110 m wide and centered on the shore, roughens the coast at the meter scale so beaches read as gravel rather than CAD. Toggle the water plane on and off: the gold line and the water's edge agree everywhere, because they are the same equation solved two ways.

One function, fourteen-odd noise evaluations deep, and we have a world: a 0.3 km² tarn held 4 m above its outlet, ringed by crests that top out around 600 m. But look at what the hero is actually drawing — a 3 km square at 384² resolution, about 8 m between vertices. Fine for a turntable; hopeless for a game where the bird can dive to the deck and skim wingtip-close over gravel it needs to see. Uniform grids can't serve both the beach under your talons and the far shore in the same frame. The fix is to spend vertices where the camera is — rings of detail that follow you, kilometers on a fixed budget. That's The Far Shore.
