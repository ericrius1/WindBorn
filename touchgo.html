Touch and Go

The first contact between series: the bird lands on the water, rides the same waves the renderer draws, announces every touch on the disturbance bus, and beats her way back into the air.

Scroll down to read ↓

Everything in this series so far happened in empty air. The mesh, the rig, the wingbeat, the poses — a complete bird, hovering over nothing. Meanwhile The Mirror has been building a lake. Today the two series shake hands, and the handshake is the most important architectural moment in Fish Hawk so far: the bird and the water will meet only through two shared contracts. One function, waterHeightAt(x, z, t), answers "where is the surface?" One event channel, disturb(), carries "I touched it." Nothing about feathers leaks into the water code; nothing about waves leaks into the bird.

Step 1 · The flareLanding is a scheduled stall

Watch any big bird land on water and the choreography is fixed: a shallow glide, then at some height — committed, irreversible — the whole body converts into an air brake. Wings forward and high with leading edges pitched up, tail fanned and pressed down, feet reaching. Lift spikes, speed collapses, and the bird arrives at the surface moving barely faster than the ripples. We already own that shape: it is the brake pose, so landing is a trigger, not an animation. The controller glides while height-above-water exceeds a flare height, then hands the pose springs a brake request and lets physics bleed the speed. Forward velocity decays exponentially, sink rate eases toward half a meter per second, and the geometry of a glide slope does the rest: flare too high and she stalls and drops in gracelessly; too low and she hits fast. Step 2 · The floatBuoyancy from the same water you can see

The moment of contact swaps the controller from aerodynamics to Archimedes. The honest law — upthrust equals the weight of displaced water — would need the submerged volume of a bird-shaped hull. But notice what that force looks like near equilibrium: floating a little too deep displaces a little more water and pushes up, a little too high pushes down. Linearize it and buoyancy is the damped spring from last post, pulling the body toward (surface − immersion): 

ay = k·(waterHeightAt(x, z, t) − immersion − y) − c·vy

The crucial detail is which surface. The spine's waterHeightAt is the same five-wave sum, with the same wind steering, that the GPU evaluates per-vertex to displace the surface you're looking at — CPU and shader are twin transcriptions of one table (Wind over Water built it). So the float doesn't approximately ride the waves, it rides them exactly. The four orange probes below are pinned to the CPU function with no rendering tricks: they stay glued to the GPU surface at any wind. Aside · A raptor that swims

Ospreys are the only raptor that routinely goes into the water — often a meter under on a committed strike. The equipment list reads like marine hardware: dense, oily plumage that sheds water, nostril valves that close on impact, and that famous two-and-two toe grip. Even so, a soaked osprey is a heavy osprey; they float well but linger badly, and a bird that grabs a fish too big to lift will sometimes row ashore with its wings rather than let go. The takeoff run in step four is genuinely hard work.

Step 3 · The busAnnouncing the touch

Here is the part that outlives this post. When the bird hits the water, who needs to know? This season: a ripple-ring effect. Next season: a real fluid simulation that wants momentum injected, a foam layer, a splash synthesizer in your headphones. The wrong answer is for the bird to call each of those systems; the right answer is for the bird to shout into a room. disturb({kind, x, z, energy, radius, vx, vz}) publishes the event; anyone can subscribe. The energy scale is the contract's most useful clause — a raindrop is ~0.001, a fish rise ~0.05, a full talons-first strike ~1.0 — so every listener, forever, can scale its response from one number. Our landing emits a splash with energy from impact speed; the float emits soft wake events as it drifts. Step 4 · The takeoff runLeaving is harder than arriving

A floating osprey can't jump into the air — there's nothing solid to jump from. The escape is a run: near-vertical hover-gait strokes for maximum thrust, body hauled forward, feet slapping the surface stride after stride while speed builds. In the controller that's three lines layered on systems we already have: gait pinned near hover with the flap rate commanded high, forward thrust integrated each frame, and lift that grows with airspeed until it finally exceeds the weight. While the bird stays low, every footfall publishes a skim event with the bird's velocity attached — next season's simulation will stretch those into a dotted wake line, and the audio series will make them patter. And with that, New Feathers closes its loop. Five posts ago the osprey was a table of numbers; now she folds, flaps, poses, flares, floats on the shared lake, and climbs back out of it, narrating every contact to whoever subscribes. What she still lacks is freedom — every flight in this series ran on rails I scripted. Giving her real ones — lift and drag and stall, a stick to fly her with, wind you can ride — is a whole series of its own, and it starts at Four Forces. The bird is ready. Let's teach her physics.
