The Color of Air

Fish Hawk's world runs on one clock, and the sky is its face. This post derives a sun that knows the hour and an atmosphere that knows what to do with it — blue noons, red sunsets, and the slow grade between. Press play and watch a whole day pass over the lake.

Scroll down to read ↓

Of Fish Hawk's five verbs, the quietest one is witness: the world runs on a clock, and the player gets to be there for it. Dawn mist burning off glass water, the dusk hatch boiling at golden hour, a storm front shouldering over the rim — every one of those moments is, underneath, the same variable: clock.hour. The spine has carried that number since the project began, and back in Glass Dawn we promised that its hand-keyed five-stop sunrise was a placeholder — one hour value in, sun vector and colors out, fidelity to follow. This series is the follow. Today we build the real thing: where the sun is at any hour, and why the air between us and it has a color at all.

Step 1 · The sunOne axis, two angles

Forget the solar system; stand on the lake. From here the entire sky is a rigid dome that turns once a day around a single fixed axis — the celestial pole, which at our latitude φ = 46.6° stands exactly 46.6° above the northern horizon. The sun is glued to that dome on a circle offset from its equator by the declination δ — how far north of the equator the season has pushed it. Stillwater fixes the date in mid-April, when ospreys return to northern lakes, so δ = +5° forever: one season, honestly declared. The only thing that moves is the hour angle H: fifteen degrees per hour, zero at solar noon.

Spin a vector around a tilted axis and read off its height — that is the whole derivation. Express the sun in the rotating frame, tip the frame down by the latitude, and the vertical component falls out as 

sin e = sin φ · sin δ + cos φ · cos δ · cos H

where e is the sun's elevation above the horizon. The same rotation gives the full 3D direction, which is what the code actually returns: sunDirection(hour), a unit vector in world space. Scrub through the geometry below, then drag the live arc to see the formula's day: sunrise just north of east, a 48° noon, a long northwestern sunset.

Step 2 · The blueAir is not transparent

Now point that sun at a sky and ask the real question: why is the air visible? Because nitrogen and oxygen molecules scatter sunlight, and they scatter it unfairly. Rayleigh's law says the scattering strength goes as 1/λ⁴ — the fourth power of inverse wavelength — so 440 nm blue light scatters about 5.7× more strongly than 680 nm red. We carry that law as three numbers, the scattering coefficients per kilometer of air at red, green and blue wavelengths: βR = (0.0058, 0.0135, 0.0331).

The other ingredient is how much air a ray crosses. Treat the atmosphere as a flat slab about 8 km thick (its real density falls exponentially; 8 km is the equivalent uniform column). A ray leaving your eye at elevation angle e crosses an air mass m ≈ 1/sin e times the vertical column — 1 looking straight up, ten or twenty out near the horizon. Multiply: the optical depth of the view path is τ = βR · 8 km · m, per channel, and Beer–Lambert says the fraction of light that survives it is T = e^(−τ) — the same law that tinted the lake's depths, wearing thinner clothes.

The sky's radiance is then one honest sentence: what the sun delivers, times the chance it scatters toward you, times the chance the scattered light survives the rest of the trip. A real renderer integrates that along the ray; we take a single sample that stands for the whole path: 

L(view) = E_sun · T_sun · phase(θ) · (1 − T_view)

(1 − T_view) is the slab's total chance of redirecting light into your eye — small and blue-dominated overhead (thin path, blue scatters most: blue sky), saturating toward 1 in every channel at the horizon (long path: the pale horizon band you have squinted at all your life). T_sun is the sun's own transmittance through its slant path, and it is doing quiet, important work — hold that thought one step.

Step 3 · The hazeMie, the disc, and sunset for free

Molecules alone give a sky that is blue but strangely clean — no glow around the sun, no warmth in the valley air. The missing population is aerosols: dust, pollen, water droplets, all far bigger than a wavelength. Their scattering (Mie scattering) is nearly colorblind — one coefficient, not three — but fiercely directional: most light continues almost straight ahead. We model that lobe with the Henyey–Greenstein phase function with anisotropy g = 0.76, against Rayleigh's nearly-uniform (3/16π)(1 + cos²θ). The forward lobe is the white glare around the sun; turn the haze forwardness slider below and watch it tighten.

And the sunset? Already paid for. At e = 2° the sun's own light crosses roughly twenty atmospheres of slant path, and Beer–Lambert strips the blue out of it: T_sun comes back red. Every term lit by the sun — the in-scatter, the haze glow, the visible disc itself — inherits that color with no sunset keyframe anywhere in the code. Sunset is not a color; it is white light minus its blue, and the model knows it before we do.

Aside · Two declared cheats

First: a flat slab has no dawn. Real twilight is sunlight grazing the curved shell of air above your head while you sit in shadow — geometry our slab cannot express — so we soften the air-mass clamp and let the sun keep feeding the sky for a few degrees below the horizon. Second: the single sample under-saturates the daytime horizon, which real skies whiten by light scattering twice. Our (1 − T) term saturates in the same direction, which is why the cheat holds up. Multiple scattering returns in the clouds post, where it cannot be dodged.

Step 4 · The hoursGolden, blue, and the grade between

Put the two steps together and key the result to the spine clock, and the photographer's vocabulary falls out as regimes of one variable, the sun's elevation. Above ~7°: full day, white key light, blue fill. Between 0° and 7°: golden hour — the air-mass curve goes steep, every degree of elevation doubles down on the reddening, and the light goes long and warm. From 0° to about −4°: blue hour — the disc is gone, direct light is gone, and all that remains is sky-scattered blue arriving from everywhere at once. Below −12°: night, which gets its own post. Scrub the evening below and watch the regimes hand off; the readout names them as you go.

Aside · Why the lake never reads the clock

The water in these demos is the same LakeSurface built in The Mirror's finale, completely unchanged. It asks its environment for a sun vector, a sun color, and a skyColor(direction) — and our atmosphere exports exactly that shape. Light the world and the water lights itself; the contract did its job, and the placeholder dawn retires with a clean conscience.

The APIWhat the sky now exports

Everything above ships in src/lib/sky/ as the series' contract: sunDirection(hour) and moonDirection(hour) on the CPU; skyColors(hour) returning the palette other systems grade by (fog color, ambient, sun tint, intensities keyed to elevation); and createAtmosphere() on the GPU — uniforms plus a skyColor(dir) node, with overcast and flash knobs already wired for the weather post to pull. The terrain standing in these scenes is the real basin, lit by the same sun.
