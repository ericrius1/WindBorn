The Mirror World

Until now the lake has been reflecting a painted sky. Time to make it reflect the world — mountains, the snag, the buoys — by rendering the whole scene a second time, upside down, from an eye that lives underwater.

Scroll down to read ↓

The lake we built in part one and rippled in part two has been getting away with a trick: when a pixel needs to know what reflects, it asks an analytic sky-dome function and nobody checks its work. That stops mattering the moment anything stands at the shoreline. Fish Hawk's lake sits in a glacial bowl — ridgelines all around, a dead snag where the nest will go, and eventually a bird skimming two meters off the surface. Every one of those things needs a double in the water, or the scene splits into "lake" and "stuff", two layers that don't believe in each other. Today we earn the double: true planar reflections, derived from a geometric fact so clean it feels like cheating.

Step 1 · The mirrored eyeA camera under the lake

Here's the fact. Stand at the shore and look at a mountain's reflection. The light reaching you left the mountain, hit the water at some point, and bounced up to your eye — equal angles in, out. Now reflect yourself across the water plane instead: put a phantom eye at your position with its height negated, (x, y, z) → (x, −y, z). The bounced path from mountain to surface to you and the straight path from mountain to the phantom have identical geometry — same intersection point, same lengths, mirrored. Which means: everything a flat mirror shows you is an ordinary photograph taken by your mirrored twin. No ray tracing required; a second rasterized render from a second camera position is exact for a flat mirror.

Aside · Why not just ray trace, or screen-space hacks?

Screen-space reflection reuses the main image, so anything not on screen — the underside of a branch, terrain behind the camera — simply vanishes from the water, and the artifacts crawl as you move. Real ray tracing solves everything and costs everything. The planar mirror is the one special case where plain rasterization is exactly right, because all reflected rays share a single virtual viewpoint. A lake at y = 0 is the best reflection deal in graphics; we'd be fools not to take it.

Step 2 · The double as a textureRender, then look up

Each frame now runs twice: first the twin renders the scene (with the water itself hidden — it shouldn't reflect its own surface) into an offscreen render target; then the main camera renders normally, and the water material samples that texture. The lookup is almost embarrassingly simple: because the twin shares the main camera's projection, the reflected image lines up with the screen pixel for pixel, so a fragment at screen position (u, v) reads the texture at (1 − u, v). The horizontal flip is the fingerprint of a mirror: a reflection across a plane reverses handedness, but a camera's lookAt always rebuilds a proper right-handed frame, and the difference between those two — one flip — lands in the image. Sampling x-backwards puts it back.

Two practical details. The twin can see underwater geometry that no reflection should contain, so we shear its projection matrix to make the near plane coincide with the water itself — an oblique near plane, clipping everything below y = 0 for free. And because the reflection is just a texture, its resolution is a budget knob independent of the main image. Crank it down in the demo and watch the mirror — and only the mirror — go soft:

Aside · The oblique clip, briefly

A projection matrix's third row computes clip-space depth. The trick is to replace that row so the equation "depth = near plane" becomes the water plane's equation in camera space — the far plane gets skewed in the process, which costs depth precision but never correctness for our shallow scenes. It's a four-line matrix surgery in the library, performed on the twin every frame after copying the main projection.

Step 3 · The ripples take the mirrorPerturbing the lookup

A perfect mirror is a dead lake. On real water, every ripple facet tilts the reflected ray: where the surface pitches by a small angle α, the reflection swings by 2α, and the mirror image wobbles, stretches, and tears along the wave crests. The exact answer would re-intersect every tilted ray with the scene — ray tracing through the back door. The honest cheap answer: nudge the texture lookup sideways by the normal's tilt, 

uv = (1 − u, v) + normal.xz × strength

which is the first-order version of that ray swing, projected to the screen. It inherits everything we built in part two for free — wind speed, choppiness, gusts — because it reads the same analytic normal the glints use. Step 4 · Weighted homeFresnel takes the wheel again

Raw reflection everywhere makes liquid chrome — gorgeous, and wrong. The Fresnel ramp from part one already told us the law: 2% mirror at your feet, ~100% at the horizon. So the planar reflection simply replaces the painted sky in the same one-line skin, mix(body, reflection, F) + glint, and the mirror world fades in exactly where physics wants it. OnwardWhat this unlocks

The lake now carries the world on its back: geometry above the waterline gets an honest, rippled, Fresnel-weighted double, at the cost of one extra render into a half-resolution texture. What it still fakes is everything below the line — the body color is a flat deep-water constant, no matter how shallow the shore. Next we open the water up: Beer–Lambert absorption to tint the depths channel by channel, Snell refraction to bend the view rays, and a lakebed you can finally see at your feet — right where Fresnel always promised the window would be.
