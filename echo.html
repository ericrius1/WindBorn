The Far Wall

Put the bird anywhere on the basin, press call, and count the seconds until the rock answers. The delays aren't designed — they're measured, by rays marched across the same terrain function that built the mountains.

So far this season the world makes sound at the bird — wind from its own speed, water from the bus. This post the bird speaks, and the world speaks back. In Fish Hawk the call has jobs to do: it's the player's presence in the valley, it will mark the nest, greet the dawn, scold the eagle. But the real reason it's a whole post is the answer. When we carved the cirque, we ringed the lake with rock walls hundreds of meters high. Walls like that have a voice of their own, and it arrives on a schedule set by nothing but distance and the speed of sound — which means we can compute it, exactly, from terrain we already have.

Step 1 · The instrumentA throat with two voices

A bird's voice box is not like ours. Where the trachea forks into the lungs sits the syrinx — and because it sits at the fork, it has two independent sound sources, one per bronchus, each with its own vibrating membranes and its own muscle control. Songbirds can sing two unrelated notes at once. Even when both sides voice the same note, they're never perfectly tuned to each other, and the slight mistuning beats — a shimmer no single oscillator can fake.

So the model is honest about the anatomy: two parallel chains, each a sine oscillator pushed through a soft-clip waveshaper — membranes driven hard don't move sinusoidally, and that clipping adds the harmonics that read as animal rather than test tone. Voice two sits about 30 cents sharp and its pitch contour lags by twelve milliseconds, because it's an independent source, not a chorus effect. Both sum into one bandpass formant near 2.4 kHz — the resonance of the trachea and open beak. The chirp itself is a frequency envelope: from f₀ up to 1.32·f₀ in the first third, then falling past the start to 0.82·f₀ — the rise-and-fall arc of the osprey's familiar cheep, around 1.6–2.3 kHz, repeated three to four times a second, creeping upward as urgency rises. Compare one voice against two below — the difference is the bird.

Aside · The whistle mismatch

For a raptor that looks like it should scream, the osprey sounds almost comically gentle — field guides reach for "whistled", "plaintive", even "chirping kettle". Hollywood agrees: when a bald eagle appears on screen, the cry you hear is usually a red-tailed hawk, because the eagle's real voice is a giggle. We get to keep the osprey's true voice — at game scale, a clear quiet whistle over still water carries more atmosphere than any stock screech.

Step 2 · One wallThe 2d/343 tap

Now the answer. Sound crosses valley air at 343 m/s. A call that reaches a rock face at distance d and bounces straight back arrives 

delay = 2·d / 343 seconds

after it left — the entire mechanism of an echo, one division. Loudness falls with the round trip: pressure spreads spherically as 1/r over 2d, times a reflectivity for the rock (we use 0.75 for steep faces). At lake scale that honest law gives −50 dB echoes — physically right, mix-wise useless — so we compress it to √(1/2d), which keeps the ordering (near walls loud, far walls faint) at audible levels, and we say so instead of hiding it. Distance also dulls: air absorbs high frequencies first, so each tap gets a lowpass whose cutoff falls with d. Step 3 · The ringMarching the basin

The cirque isn't one wall — it's a ring of them, every bearing a different distance. Finding them is a raymarch over terrainHeightAt, the same canonical height function every mesh on this site displaces by. From the bird we walk ten compass rays outward in 12 m steps until the terrain rises above flight height. Not every hit deserves an echo: a beach that drifts up through our altitude is a grazing surface, not a reflector, so we check the local slope along the ray and give reflectivity only to steep rock — gentle ground lets the ray keep marching until it finds a face worth bouncing off. Each surviving hit becomes a tap: {delay, gain, cutoff, pan}, the pan set by the wall's bearing so the answer comes from the right side of your head.

The hero is this code running live. Click around the basin: near the headwall the first echo returns inside a second; over the lake's center the whole ring answers between two and five seconds in a spread chord; drop the altitude slider low over the outlet valley and watch taps vanish — the notch we carved for the lake to drain through is also a gap in the sound. And because the taps refresh as the bird moves, echo timing becomes navigation: short delay means wall coming, symmetric delays mean center of the lake. File that thought — the finale will fly on it.

Aside · First order only

Real valleys echo echoes: wavefronts bounce wall to wall, smearing into seconds of reverberant tail. We stop at first-order reflections — ten taps, no feedback — because the legible, countable answer cheep… cheep is the version that carries information a player can use. A feedback delay network could fake the tail cheaply, and the score post's pads will supply that sense of wash instead. A design choice, recorded here so future-us knows it was on purpose.

The bird has a voice; the valley has a shape you can hear. With wind, water, and now the call, every diegetic sound in Fish Hawk exists — sound with a physical cause inside the world. What's missing is the layer with no source at all: music. Next post builds the adaptive score — layers keyed to altitude, the world clock, and the weather, crossfaded with the equal-power math that keeps a mix steady while everything in it moves.
