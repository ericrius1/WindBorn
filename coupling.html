Two-way Coupling

Click the water and watch the floats answer: the splash pushes them, the waves carry them, and everything they do pushes back. From today, the water and the world have a two-way conversation.

Scroll down to read ↓

So far our water listens but doesn't speak. The sim bubble hears every event on the disturbance bus and ripples obligingly — but a float sitting next to a splash just bobs in place, riding the height like a sticker. Real water grabs things. A wake shoves a drifting feather; a splash scatters everything around it; and when Fish Hawk's osprey belly-lands a catch, the lake has to push back hard enough to matter (in Touch and Go we faked that with a hand-tuned spring — today the spring gets replaced by physics). This post closes the loop in both directions: events become momentum in the velocity field, and the surface state flows back out to drive buoyancy and drag on floating bodies.

Step 1 · Momentum inWhat an impact actually is

The shallow-water state gives us two things to poke: the surface η and the velocity field v. The lazy injection — subtract a Gaussian dent from η and let it relax — makes passable rings, but compare it side by side with reality and something's missing: the rebound. A body hitting water transfers momentum; the displaced water rushes outward and the cavity collapses back with a jet. So our impulse writes both channels: a modest dent in η (the displaced volume) plus an outward radial kick in v, each weighted by a Gaussian footprint and scaled by √energy — kinetic energy goes like v², so the velocity we inject goes like the square root of the event's energy budget. Directional events (a wake, a wingtip skim) additionally inject the disturber's own velocity, which is what will make wakes trail behind movers in part six. Aside · One bus, many listeners

Nothing in the game ever calls the sim directly. An impact calls disturb({kind, x, z, energy, radius}) on the spine's bus — the same call the bird's feet have been making since season one — and the sim is just one subscriber that converts kinds into impulse recipes. The expanding analytic rings from Glass Dawn subscribe to the identical events, which is why nothing needed rewriting when the real physics arrived: we swapped a listener, not a protocol. The splash synthesizer in the audio series will be subscriber number three.

Step 2 · State outThe mirror

Direction two is harder for an annoying architectural reason: the water lives on the GPU, and the bodies — gameplay, AI, audio — live on the CPU. waterHeightAt must return a number now, synchronously; the GPU answers questions by mail. Stalling the pipeline to ask (a synchronous readback) can cost more than the entire frame. The compromise is a mirror: every frame we kick off an asynchronous copy of the state buffer; it arrives a frame or two later and becomes the CPU's picture of the surface. Queries bilinearly sample the mirror — fast, allocation free, and slightly stale.

Is stale acceptable? Quantify it: at two frames behind (33 ms), a wave front moving at the capped 3.8 m/s has advanced 13 cm. The bodies that read the mirror are floats with inertia — a 13 cm phase error in the force that pushes a relaxing log is invisible. (It would not be invisible for rendering, which is why the renderer never touches the mirror — it samples the live buffer in the vertex shader.) Step 3 · ArchimedesBuoyancy as a spring you don't tune

With heights flowing back, floating is one theorem old enough to have a bath story. A body displaces water; the water pushes back with the weight of what was displaced: F = ρ·g·V. For a sphere of radius R with its bottom a depth e under the surface, the submerged cap has volume V(e) = π·e²·(3R − e)/3, so the force is zero when dry, the full displaced sphere when buried, and smoothly nonlinear in between. The beautiful part: equilibrium, bobbing frequency, and splash response all fall out with no tuning constants. A body of relative density 0.42 floats 42% submerged because that's where the cap volume balances its weight. Push it under and the surplus shoves it up; the restoring force makes it a natural oscillator, damped by drag. Drag is the second half of the story — and the reason the mirror also carries the velocity field. Step 4 · Full circleBodies that write and read

Now run both directions at once. A tugged float announces "wake" events as it moves (momentum in, energy scaled by v²); the sim integrates them into a trailing wave pattern; free-floating bodies read heights and velocities back from the mirror and get carried, shoved, and rocked by the wake of a thing they never touched. OnwardWhat this unlocks

The loop is closed: bus → sim → surface → mirror → bodies → bus. This is the machine the game's centerpiece runs on — when the osprey strikes, the impact event, the rebound, the bobbing recovery, and the fish's escape rings are all this page's physics. But watch any real impact and the water doesn't stay a surface: it leaves it. Droplets, sheets, the white crown that gives a splash its silhouette. The height field can't represent water in the air — so next post we spend the impact's energy budget on a few thousand ballistic particles, born from the surface velocity field and rejoining the sim as rain. The splash finally gets its crown.
