Everything pointed here. Fifty-some posts, four seasons, eleven series, and a single goal underneath all of them: be an osprey on a mountain lake for an evening. The window above is that goal, finished. It is not a mockup or a sizzle reel — it is the same code every earlier post built, mounted whole and wired together: the title screen, the play loop, pause and photo mode and settings, and the credits that close the project. The "Play Fish Hawk" button on the front page lands right here, on a bird you can fly.

What follows isn't really an essay; the teaching is done. It's a walk through the assembly — how the five verbs each became a series, and how the controller snaps them into one game — and then the credits, because a thing this long owes a roll.

Step 1 · Five verbs, eleven seriesHow the game is assembled

Fish Hawk is five verbs, and each is the payoff of the seasons that came before. Soar is the flight model riding on the Basin: a point-mass bird where lift lives in the landscape, thermals off sun-warmed scree and ridge lift on windward slopes, all read from the one wind field. Spot is the hunt drawing on Alive: no markers, ever — you read glints, shadows, and the rings of a rise, and the game's only assist is to lean the camera a few degrees toward a good chance. Dive is the centerpiece, scored on flight and dropped into the Splash's real fluid window, with Under's submarine instant in the middle.

Feed and build is the loop and the nest: hunger that creeps, daylight you spend, and a home that grows a few sticks at a time on the Basin's north-shore snag. Witness is Air & Light and photo mode: a world that keeps time — dawn mist burning off glass water, the dusk hatch, weather fronts, moonrise — and a journal that fills itself with the moments you fly through. Holding all of it together is the Mirror's water, the Ear's procedural soundscape synthesized from your own airspeed, and the Glass & Grain post stack grading every frame to the hour.

The controller itself is small — almost boring, which is the point. It's a state machine with three phases (title, playing, paused) and a few overlay panels, wrapped around the pieces every post already built. It owns the world clock while it runs, advances the game state each frame, grades every dive, leans the camera toward fish, grows the nest when you feed there, freezes time for a photo, and rolls the day over when you roost after dark. Roosting at the nest as the light goes advances the day; a few days and the brood is in and the credits are yours. Aside · The whole budget, one more time

The hero runs the medium preset from the reckoning: clouds present but lean, the ecosystem full enough to telegraph fish, the fluid window at full resolution, and the complete post stack — grade, bloom, speed, wet lens, photo overlays. That's all of it, sharing one sixteen-millisecond frame, holding sixty on an M-series laptop. Drop to low in Settings for a slower machine; nothing breaks, the sky just goes flat and the school thins. Every gram of detail is somewhere you can turn it down.

Step 2 · The rollCredits

A game made of fifty-some essays owes every one of them a line. The credits below are built from the same registry the site navigation reads, grouped by season exactly as the front page is, so they can never drift out of sync with what actually shipped. Every series is here, and every post within it is a link — the whole project, from the first Fresnel term on the water to this game, in one scroll. In the playable hero, the same roll lives behind the Credits button on the title and pause screens.

That's Stillwater: part blog, part build log, part game, converging on one osprey over one lake. The bird at the top of this page is the argument that the whole thing was worth making — that a year of essays about Fresnel and flight models and shallow-water equations and procedural wind was, all along, about this: a quiet evening, a glass dawn, and the small clean shock of dropping out of the sky into real water and coming up with a fish. Thanks for flying it. The lake is yours to wander.
