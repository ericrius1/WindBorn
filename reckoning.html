The Reckoning

Each system was profiled alone and ran fine. The reckoning is what happens when you turn them all on at once — sky, water, terrain, ecosystem, post — and ask the only question a game cares about: does it hold sixty? Here is the full world, the budget broken down, and the knobs that buy back headroom. Click to fly it and watch the fps.

Every post in this project has ended with a demo that ran smoothly, and every one of them was lying a little — because each was alone in the frame. The clouds raymarched against an empty sky. The fluid window stepped with nothing else competing for the GPU. The fish schooled over flat water. A game is the moment they all share a sixteen-millisecond budget at once, and that is a different, harsher question. This post is the reckoning: the whole world assembled, and an honest look at whether it fits.

The target is plain. Sixty frames a second on an M-series laptop means every frame, start to finish, must complete in 16.7 milliseconds. Into that window we have to fit a sky that scatters light and marches volumetric cloud, a lake whose surface is a shaded sheet over a moving fluid sim, kilometers of clipmap terrain, a few hundred animated fish and insects and other birds, the flight integration, and a cinematic post stack of grade, bloom, and speed effects. The good news is that almost none of it is fixed: nearly every system has a knob, and the art of shipping is knowing which knob to turn first.

Step 1 · Where the milliseconds goReading the budget

You cannot optimize what you cannot see, so the first job is attribution: of those 16.7 milliseconds, where do they go? The rough shape, on this world, is that the sky is the heaviest tenant — the cloud raymarch alone can eat half the budget at full quality — followed by the water, then the terrain mesh, then the ecosystem, with the post stack a roughly fixed couple of milliseconds on top. The figure below stacks those classes into one bar against the sixty-fps line, and lets you flip between the low, medium, and high presets to watch the budget redistribute — clouds and water collapsing first, because that's where the money is.

An honest caveat, stated plainly: that breakdown is an estimate. We can measure the whole frame from JavaScript, but a browser doesn't hand out reliable per-pass GPU timings, so the per-system split is modeled from each preset's known weights rather than read off a profiler. The number that matters — the total, and whether it's under the line — is real, measured on your machine in the hero above. The breakdown is there to show the shape: which systems dominate, and therefore which knob to reach for when a frame runs long.

Aside · A fixed window beats a bigger world

The single best performance decision in the whole project was made back in the sim bubble: the fluid simulation is a fixed-size grid that follows the bird, not a lake-sized one. Doubling the lake costs the fluid sim nothing, because the sim never sees the lake — only its own twenty-eight-meter window. The same instinct runs through the terrain clipmap and the focus-following weather: spend detail where the player is looking, fake the rest, and the cost stays flat as the world grows. Most of holding sixty is refusing to pay for what isn't on screen.

Step 2 · The heaviest knobScaling, in one slider

When a frame runs long, the first knob is always the most expensive system, and here that is unambiguous: the volumetric cloud march. Every cloud pixel walks a ray through the sky volume in a fixed number of steps, and that step count is a straight multiplier on the sky's cost — and the sky is already the heaviest tenant. The figure below puts that one slider on an orbiting world and shows the measured fps move with it: drop the steps toward zero for a flat, cheap sky, push them toward twenty for a lush and dear one. Nothing else changes; this is the purest example of a scaling knob earning its keep.

The presets in the hero are just bundles of these knobs chosen to land on a frame budget. High spends freely — sixteen cloud steps, two hundred water cells, ninety fish, geese and an eagle aloft. Low flattens the sky entirely, thins the school, and grounds the extra birds, trading richness for a frame that holds on a modest machine. Medium is the default the game ships with: clouds present but lean, the ecosystem full enough to telegraph fish, the post stack intact. Flip between them in the hero and the fps readout is the verdict.

So the budget holds, with the knobs to defend it when a machine is slower than ours. That is the last piece of engineering the project needs: every system proven alone, then proven together, with a frame that fits and a way to keep it fitting. Everything from here is assembly. The next and final post turns all of this — the loop, the hunt, the scored dive, the growing nest, this whole budgeted world — into the thing it was always for: Fish Hawk, the game.
