The Hunt

A real osprey has no minimap. It finds fish by sight, from sixty meters up, reading the surface for the few things that betray a fish: a glint, a shadow, a rising ring. Fish Hawk refuses markers for the same reason — the lake already tells you everything, if you learn to look. Click to take the stick and read the water.

The most tempting thing to build, the moment you have a reason to fish, is a marker. A little diamond over each fish, a distance number, a "press to dive" prompt. It would work, and it would quietly ruin the game — because the instant the screen tells you where the fish are, you stop looking at the lake, and the whole reason to render a living surface evaporates. So Fish Hawk has a hard rule that shapes this entire post: no UI markers, ever. The hunt happens entirely in the world. And it can, because three seasons of work already made the surface honest. The ecosystem doesn't need a HUD layered on top of it. It is the HUD.

That sounds like a renunciation, but it's really an inheritance. Every cue a real fisher reads is something the simulation already produces, for free, because the physics that make it produce them are the same physics the rest of the game runs on. We just have to teach the game to read its own water the way the player will — so it can lean the camera a hair, warm the music a touch, never more — and otherwise get out of the way.

Step 1 · Glint, shadow, ringThe three tells

There are exactly three ways a fish gives itself away from above, and each is a real optical or behavioral fact the world already simulates. A glint: a fish cruising in the top meter or two of clear water catches the sun and throws a bright specular flash off its flank — the schooling fish already emit these. A shadow: a fish too deep to glint still drags a dark, moving shape across the lit lakebed, and a fast cruiser's shadow is readable even when the fish itself is not. And a rise: a feeding fish breaks the surface to take a mayfly and leaves an expanding ring — the loudest tell of all, because it says this fish is here, near the top, feeding, right now. Rises stamp themselves onto the same disturbance bus the splash and the wake use, so they're already first-class events.

The catch — the thing that makes spotting a skill — is that the surface itself decides which tells are legible. On glass-calm water a glint is a beacon. Put a breeze on the lake and the chop scatters the specular highlight first: glints frost over, and you're left reading the slower shadows and the rings, which a little wind can't hide. The figure below is exactly that. Orbit a school in clear water, then crank the wind: watch the readout's glint count collapse as the chop comes up, while the rises keep ringing through. Learning when the water will and won't show you a fish is the hunting skill, and it's all physics, no rules.

Aside · How ospreys actually hunt

Ospreys hover or quarter slowly at twenty to forty meters, head angled straight down, and they really do read the surface for exactly these cues — which is why they fish best in calm conditions and clear water and go hungry in a chop. Their eyes carry a deep central fovea and oil droplets that cut surface glare, the biological version of a polarizing filter. We can't give the player a fovea, so we give them the next best thing: a lake whose tells obey the same rules a real osprey's prey obeys, and the patience the bird's own anatomy enforces.

Step 2 · Attention, not a markerWhat the game is allowed to do

If the game can't draw on the fish, what can it do to keep the hunt fair? It can have an opinion, and let that opinion gently bias the feel without ever surfacing as a label. The hunt logic scores every visible fish on two axes — how strongly the surface is telegraphing it right now (glint, shadow, or rise, scaled by the chop) and how good a dive it would be (close enough to reach, shallow enough to strike) — and combines them into a single worth. The best chance, if there is one worth taking, is allowed to do precisely two things: lean the chase camera a few degrees toward it, the way your own head would turn, and warm the music. That's the entire "targeting system."

The figure below makes that invisible opinion visible, just so you can confirm it's honest. A soft halo drifts to whatever the scorer thinks is the best chance — and then you can toggle it off. With the assist gone, the fish are exactly as findable, because the assist was never information; it was only a lean, the same lean the hero up top applies to the camera. The scoring is also what the dive will read to decide a strike's odds, and what the audio reads to know when to lift. One small function, no pixels spent on the fish.

So the hunt is reading, not aiming: learn the water, wait for a tell you can trust, and commit. The hero lets you do exactly that — fly the bay at the dusk hatch, when the rings are everywhere and the light is going, and find a fish with nothing but your eyes and a camera that turns its head when you do. What you do next — the tuck, the fall, the talons-first hit timed to where the fish will be — is the dive, and it is where the whole project has been pointing.
