The Sim Bubble

The osprey tows a 26-meter patch of real physics around a kilometer-wide lake. Press "show window" to see the trick; release it and try to find the seam again.

Scroll down to read ↓

Here's the budget problem that decides Fish Hawk's architecture. The shallow-water sim needs cells about 14 cm wide to carry a crisp splash ring. Our lake is roughly 600 m across. Cover it all and that's nearly twenty million cells stepped multiple times a frame — not a chance, and almost all of it would simulate flat calm nobody is looking at. Meanwhile the ambient wave table already renders endless convincing water for free, and it's analytic — evaluable anywhere, instantly. So the design writes itself: keep one fixed grid, a couple hundred cells on a side, parked wherever the action is, and let the analytic lake own everything else. The bird is the action. The window follows the bird. This post builds that window — the series' namesake trick — in three moves: the shift, the fade, and the swap.

Step 1 · The traveling gridSnap, shift, refill

The grid never changes size; only its anchor in the world moves. When the bird drifts, we re-center — but never by fractional cells. Moving by an arbitrary offset would mean resampling: every new cell a blend of four old ones, and a little blur leaks in every frame the window moves. Death by a thousand interpolations. Snapping the center to whole lattice steps makes the move an exact memory copy instead: cell (i, j) takes the value of cell (i+di, j+dj), bit for bit. That's one ping-pong compute pass — read A shifted, write B — plus a depth-buffer refill at the new position, since the bathymetry under the window changed too. The snap offset is at most half a cell (7 cm); nothing at gameplay distance can see it. Scroll the mechanism:

Two details earn their keep. Cells entering at the leading edge initialize to zero — flat water — which sounds like a visible pop until you remember the rim is also the sponge: anything near the edge has already been damped toward zero, so "new cell = flat" matches what was there anyway. And cells falling off the trailing edge are simply forgotten — their waves were leaving the simulation's jurisdiction regardless. Aside · Why not just scroll the texture origin?

A classic alternative wraps the grid toroidally and moves a "origin" marker instead of copying memory — clipmap style, like the terrain rings in The Far Shore. It avoids the copy entirely, but every neighbor lookup in every kernel then needs wrap-around index math, and the stencil reads of a wave sim touch five cells per cell per substep — that's a lot of modulo. The dumb copy is one extra pass only on frames the window actually crosses a cell line. We measured our priorities and kept the kernels simple.

Step 2 · The invisible seamFade at the rim

Inside the window, real physics; outside, the wind-driven wave table. At the boundary they disagree — the sim carries a splash ring the ambient lake has never heard of, and cutting from one to the other mid-wave leaves a visible cliff. The fix is the oldest trick in graphics, applied with one insight: the sim doesn't store the water's height. It stores the deviation from the ambient surface. The rendered height everywhere is 

height(x, z) = ambient(x, z, t) + fade(x, z) · η_sim(x, z)

where fade is 1 through the window's core and slides to 0 across the rim band. Because the sim is a delta, fading it out doesn't fade the water out — it just returns the surface to the ambient waves, which were underneath all along. The wind ripples blow straight through the window unmolested; only the splash physics fades. And the fade band is the same rim where the sponge eats outgoing waves, so by the time a ring reaches the place it would be clipped, there's almost nothing left to clip. Step 3 · The swapOne question, any answer

The visual seam was half the job. The other half is everything that asks about the water: the bird floating in Touch and Go, fish deciding when to rise, the journal camera — all of them call one site-wide function, waterHeightAt(x, z, t), against a swappable provider. The Mirror installed the ambient provider back in Wind over Water; the spine's contract says callers may never know which implementation answers. So The Splash registers its own: 

heightAt(x, z, t) = ambient.heightAt(x, z, t) + fade(x, z) · sim η(x, z)

— the exact formula the renderer draws, answered on the CPU from a mirror of the sim buffer that reads back asynchronously each frame (about which much more in part four). One call to setWaterProvider and every consumer on the site is splash-aware, having changed zero lines of code. Aside · How big should the bubble be?

The window must hold the splash's interesting lifetime. A strike ring at the capped wave speed (≈3.8 m/s) crosses a 26 m window in about three and a half seconds — and after three seconds of damping, a ring has spent most of its energy anyway. Bigger windows buy little: at fixed cell count the cells coarsen and the crisp short-wavelength detail near the impact — the part you actually look at — gets worse. 192² cells over 24–30 m is the sweet spot on this hardware, and it's why the budget never moves even when the whole screen is water.

OnwardWhat this unlocks

The lake now has a body double that goes wherever the camera cares. But so far information flows one way: the bus pokes the sim, the sim moves the surface. A floating body still rides the water like a sticker — glued to the height, never pushed by it. Next post closes the loop: disturbance events become real momentum in the velocity field, the CPU mirror lets us read the surface back, and Archimedes takes over from there — buoyancy, drag, and bodies that get shoved sideways by the very waves they made.
