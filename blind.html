Blind Flight

The exam at the end of the season. Click in, put headphones on, drag the sight slider to zero, and fly. Everything you need to know is in your ears — that's not a boast, it's this post's claim to defend.

Scroll down to read ↓

Four posts built four instruments: wind from airspeed, water from the bus, a voice and its echoes, and a score the world conducts. The honest test of a soundscape isn't whether it's pleasant — it's whether it's informative. Game audio at its best is a second display, and there's a brutal way to measure that: turn the first display off. If the player can still fly — hold a speed, find the water, avoid the walls — then every synthesizer is carrying real signal. If they can't, the season was decoration. Hence the hero above: the complete soundscape over the real basin, with a sight slider standing between you and the dark.

Step 1 · AssemblyFour mounts, one loop

First, the engineering claim from the start of the season, now cashed in: the entire soundscape mounts with one call per system.

const water = attachWaterAudio();
const calls = attachCallAudio();

// per frame:  wind.update(dt); water.update(dt); …

Nothing else. No scene wiring, no event plumbing — each mount reads the spine singletons everything already publishes: flightState for airspeed, wingbeat, and position; the disturbance bus for impacts; the world clock and the wind field for the score's mood. The demo below is the proof by absurdity: it runs the four mounts against a headless flight model — a FlightBody stepping its physics with no renderer at all — and the soundscape neither knows nor cares that nobody drew the bird. Mute stems one at a time and listen to what each carries; the meters show every layer breathing with the dive cycle.

Step 2 · LocalizationWhat two ears can know

Flying blind leans hard on knowing where a sound is, so it's worth being precise about what our spatialization gives you. Every bus event gets three distance-and-direction cues: a gain that falls as 1/(1+(d/18)²), a lowpass that dulls far sounds the way air absorption really does, and a stereo pan from the bearing's left-right component. That's intensity panning — the cheapest of the localization cues real ears use — and the game it supports is below: a fish rises somewhere in the dark; click where you heard it. With headphones most people land within a few meters laterally on the first try.

You'll also discover the model's honest blind spots, because your own auditory system has them too: front-back confusion (a pan of zero is ambiguous between ahead and behind) and no elevation cue at all. Real ears solve those with spectral filtering by the outer ear — what head-related transfer functions model — and with head motion. We get the motion fix for free in flight: bank ten degrees and the ambiguity resolves, which is exactly what owls and pilots both do. The HRTF upgrade is a clean future slot behind the same bus interface; noted and deferred.

Aside · Hunting by ear, for real

Sound-only hunting isn't a gimmick — it's a working sensory strategy. Barn owls strike mice in total darkness, localizing by microsecond arrival differences between asymmetrically placed ears. Ospreys are eye hunters, but anglers will tell you the ear matters on still water: you hear a rise behind you before you ever see the rings. Our fish telegraph themselves on the bus with a 60 Hz-bloop rise — by the time Fish Hawk's hunt ships, that sound will be a gameplay channel, not garnish.

Step 3 · The protocolReading the dark

Back to the hero, sight at zero. Here is the full instrument panel your ears are getting, one signal per system this season built:

Speed is the wind's brightness and loudness — hold the hiss steady and you're trimmed; hear the scream resonators open and you're past 16 m/s and committed. Effort is the whump rate, the wingbeat metronome. Stall announces itself as the 10 Hz buffet flutter — ease the nose down until it stops. Water is double-keyed: the lapping bed swells as you descend toward the surface, and touching down speaks for itself — your own splash arrives on the same bus as everything else's. Walls are the call: press C, count the echoes — a short answer means rock coming, a long symmetric chord means open water. Altitude is the score's aloft layer: bells thin out as you sink through the 65–18 m band. Even weather reports in, through the storm layer's pulse. None of these were designed as accessibility features; they're just what falls out when every sound is derived from state instead of triggered by script.

If you want the full exam, here's the training flight we use. First, hold a speed: pick the hiss pitch of a comfortable glide and keep it constant for thirty seconds — pitch creeping up means the nose is dropping. Second, find the water: descend until the lapping bed swells, then flare by ear and listen for the skim — a light slap, no bloop — that says you kissed the surface instead of burying yourself in it. Third, find a wall: call, turn toward the shortest echo, and call again every few seconds; the delay shrinking from two seconds toward a half is the rock face growing in the dark, and the moment to break off is when the first tap drops under about 0.4 s — 70 m of clearance, four wingbeats. It takes most people two or three attempts. The fact that it's possible at all is the season's real test result.

That closes The Ear — and with it, the season. The valley now has a sky that keeps time, an ecosystem that telegraphs itself, and a soundscape synthesized from the same spine state as everything else: nothing recorded, nothing looped, nothing that can drift out of sync with the world, because it is the world, rendered as pressure. What it unlocks is the third playable milestone — a full day at the lake, dawn mist to moonrise, with the hatch overhead and all of this in your ears — and beyond that, the final season, where polish and play turn these systems into Fish Hawk itself. The game will open on a black screen, one beat before the first light. Now you know that even there, the lake is already speaking.
