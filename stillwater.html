Glass Dawn

Four essays of optics converge on one shot: first light over black glass, mountains doubled to the pixel, and a lake that answers when you touch it. This is the scene Fish Hawk opens on. Tap the water.

Every game I love opens with a shot that teaches you what the game is. Fish Hawk's is this one: the lake at about 6 a.m., wind dead, surface gone to glass, the cirque hanging upside down in the water, and somewhere out in the dark a fish ring spreading — the first thing the osprey, and the player, learns to read. This post builds that shot, end to end, out of the four systems this series derived: the Fresnel skin, the wind-driven ripples, the planar mirror, and the absorbing depths. Nothing new gets invented today except the connections — which, it turns out, is where scenes come from.

Step 1 · The stackOne material, four layers

Here is the entire surface material, top to bottom. Vertices lift by the wave-table height (plus any ring packets — more on those below). The fragment rebuilds the analytic normal, asks Fresnel for the mirror weight, samples the planar reflection nudged by that normal, settles the body color from the depths or the deep-water scatter constant, and adds the sun glint: 

color = mix(body, reflection, F) + glint

Same one-liner as part one — every essay since just upgraded one of its inputs. The demo below starts at zero and lets you stack the series back up one layer at a time: flat paint, then the Fresnel ramp, then ripples, then the mirrored world, then the living touches. Step 2 · The hourDawn on the world clock

Stillwater has one clock — a site-wide clock.hour that everything subscribes to, from fish behavior to music, eventually. The water itself never reads it. Instead, the sky does: sun direction and a five-key dawn palette (night, first light, sunrise, morning, day) interpolate from the hour, and the lake inherits all of it through the mirror and the glint, for free. Aside · Why a shared clock is a contract

The dawn palette here is a hand-keyed placeholder — a real sky model is a whole series of its own. But the interface is permanent: one hour value in, sun vector and colors out. When the full atmosphere lands, this scene re-keys itself without touching a line of water code. Contracts first, fidelity later — it's the only way a one-person project survives its own ambitions.

Step 3 · The stillnessGlass is not an asset

The defining property of the dawn lake is what's missing: wind. Back in part two we made every wave amplitude scale linearly with wind speed — 3.5 cm per m/s — which looked like a modeling detail at the time. It was actually this scene. As wind.speed → 0 the entire spectrum dies together, normals flatten, the reflection distortion (which reads those normals) sharpens to perfect, and the glitter path collapses toward a single solar point. Glass dawn isn't a special state with its own shader; it's the zero of the function we already had. Run the wind down yourself and watch the mirror focus:

Step 4 · The touchRings on the disturbance bus

A perfect mirror is a stage with no actors. The world needs to touch the water — fish rising now; later a landing osprey, raindrops, a wingtip. So Stillwater routes every touch through one channel: the disturbance bus. Anything can call disturb({kind, x, z, energy, radius}); anything can listen. The Mirror's listener turns each event into an expanding ring packet, added analytically to the surface like a sixth wave: a narrow Gaussian envelope riding at capillary speed, carrying a short sine, fading by time decay and by 1/(1+r) spreading as its energy stretches around a growing circle. Amplitude scales with √energy — energy goes with amplitude squared in a wave, so a 20× bigger splash rings only ~4.5× taller. Ten rings live in a small rotating pool of uniforms; the oldest quietly yields its slot, usually long after it has faded below a pixel.

The demo has two writers — your taps (a fish-rise's worth of energy) and an optional rain shower of tiny ones — and two readers: the surface, and an event counter in the readout. That second reader is the point. Next season, a fluid simulation replaces the rings and a splash synthesizer joins in your headphones, all without the writers changing a single call:

Aside · Real rise rings

Watch a trout sip a mayfly at dusk and you'll see exactly this structure: a ring a few millimeters tall, crawling outward at well under a meter per second (capillary-gravity waves this short are slow), gone in seconds. For the osprey — and the player — the ring outlives the glimpse of the fish itself. It's a sighting that persists, which is what makes it a game mechanic and not just a graphic.

The exportOne lake, many futures

With this post, The Mirror ships its real deliverable: not a picture but a library. The surface you've been poking lives in one place — src/lib/water/ — as a LakeSurface built from the wave-table nodes, the optics nodes, an optional planar reflection, an optional analytic bed, and an optional ring pool, with uniforms for clarity, roughness, and distortion. Its CPU twin remains the spine's waterHeightAt(x, z, t), glued to the rendered vertices by construction. This scene also takes over the site's front door: the landing page hero is now this exact glass dawn, drifting.

OnwardWhat this unlocks

The lake is done waiting. It can be looked at, looked into, lit by the hour, stilled by the weather, and — through the bus — touched. What it's missing is the thing that touches it. Next series we build the bird: an osprey modeled feather by feather as honest mesh geometry, rigged to fold and flap, posed to tuck and strike. When it finally lands on this water, waterHeightAt will hold it up, and the bus will catch the moment its talons break the glass. The mirror is ready for its first crack.
