The Speed of Sight

An osprey hits thirty meters per second in a dive, and a fixed, perfectly sharp camera makes it look like ten. This post teaches the frame what a real camera knows about speed: blur it, streak it, narrow its focus, and widen its eye — all read live from the flight telemetry.

The dive is Fish Hawk's centerpiece, and we spent a whole season making it physically true — the flight envelope gives the acceleration, the breach gives the splash. But truth isn't the same as sensation. When the joy pass tuned game feel, we discovered the renderer's dirty secret: every frame is infinitely sharp and the lens is infinitely fast, so speed only exists as parallax. A real camera — and a real eye — degrades under speed, and that degradation IS the sensation. Last post built a chain that recolors the frame. This one builds the stages that move pixels, and wires every one of them to the FlightState telemetry the flight model publishes each frame: airspeed in, sensation out.

Step 1 · Motion blurIntegrating over the shutter

A film camera's shutter stays open for a slice of each frame — at 24 fps, classically half the frame, the "180° shutter". Anything that moves while it's open paints a smear across the film. Mathematically the photograph is the integral of the image over the shutter interval, and our discrete version is an average: sample the frame at several points along each pixel's motion and sum. The only hard part is knowing the motion. So we ask the renderer: alongside color, each object also writes its screen-space displacement since the previous frame into a second render target — the velocity buffer. (The vertex shader simply projects each vertex with both this frame's and last frame's matrices and subtracts.) The blur node then walks ±shutter/2 along that vector. Freeze the world and the blur vanishes instantly — there is no motion between identical frames, which tells you precisely where this effect's information comes from:

Step 2 · Radial streaksOptical flow of pure forward motion

Velocity blur is honest but it has a blind spot: when the camera flies forward, most per-pixel motion is small near the center of the frame and large at the edges. Look at where things flow when you move straight ahead — every point streams radially outward from the point you're aiming at. (Geometry: a point at angle θ off the flight axis slides across your visual field at a rate proportional to sin θ; at the aim point, θ = 0, nothing moves.) So the dive look is a radial blur we can compute without any velocity buffer at all: for each pixel, pull samples along the ray from the screen center through the pixel, with a pull strength that grows from zero at the center. The fovea stays sharp, the periphery tears — which happens to be exactly how your own vision degrades when you sprint. Aside · Two blurs, one budget

Why keep both? Velocity blur is per-object — the bird's wings smear while the mountains hold still — and it costs an extra render target. Radial streaks are camera-global and nearly free, but they know nothing about individual objects. In the game the streaks carry the dive (camera motion dominates) and the velocity buffer is a quality toggle for the wingbeats. Each stage hands the next its color as the center tap, so stacking them stays seamless.

Step 3 · Depth of fieldThe circle of confusion

A pinhole camera renders everything sharp; every real lens focuses exactly one distance. Light from a point at any other distance converges in front of or behind the sensor, so it lands as a disc — the circle of confusion (CoC). Thin-lens optics says the disc's diameter grows with how far the point sits from the focal plane, scaled by aperture. We keep the model and shed the constants: 

coc(z) = maxCoC · clamp(|z − focus| / range, 0, 1)

Implementing it is where renderers usually cheat backwards. The honest simulation scatters: each source pixel splats a disc of its own CoC size onto the image. GPUs hate scatter, so we gather: each destination pixel samples a disc of neighbors (sixteen taps on a golden-angle spiral, radius √i for even coverage) scaled by its own CoC. Done naively that's wrong in one telling way — a sharp, in-focus bird would "gather" smeared color from the blurry background behind it. The fix is the scatter-as-gather weight: a neighbor only contributes if its own CoC is large enough to reach us. Each tap is weighted by tapCoC / gatherRadius, and the bleed disappears. Step 4 · The bridgeTelemetry in, sensation out

Now the wiring, which is where this post earns its place in the game. The flight model publishes a FlightState every frame — airspeed, vertical speed, tuck, altitude — and a small bridge object reads it and settles the uniforms. Airspeed maps through a smoothstep window (nothing below 13 m/s, saturating by 28) to the streak amount, so cruising stays clean and the dive tears. The dive signal — tuck × falling — drives the DOF: focal range collapses from sixty meters to seven, focus pinned to the water you're aimed at, because a predator in a stoop sees its fish and nothing else. And the FOV law from the joy pass lives here too: 0.9° of extra width per m/s above cruise, eased, so the world physically widens as it rushes in. Every input is smoothed with the same exponential lag (about a sixth of a second) — effects that snap on feel like a bug; effects that bloom in feel like adrenaline. Aside · The osprey's actual eyes

The real bird does the opposite of our camera. An osprey's eyes have two foveae per eye and roughly twice the human density of photoreceptors; in the dive it tracks the fish through head stabilization so precise the image barely moves on its retina. The blur is for us — a translation of what 30 m/s feels like into a language human players already speak from car windows and bicycle descents. Honest physics, cinematic delivery.
