The Lakebed

The basin series ends where the dive will: under the surface. The same height function that raised the mountains keeps going below zero — and one query, terrainHeightAt(x, z), becomes the question every future system asks the world.

Scroll down to read ↓

Fish Hawk's centerpiece is a dive that doesn't stop at the surface — and nothing below the surface works without a bottom. Fish need depths to hold in and shallows to cruise; the depth-tinted water needs a distance for light to die over; the fluid simulation coming next series needs a depth term in its wave speed; and a bird that misjudges a dive should meet gravel, not the void. The good news: we already built all of it. The basin function was designed from day one to be negative under the lake — the bowl term is just another part of the composition. This post is about taking that seriously: what the bathymetry actually looks like, why continuity at the shoreline matters more than it sounds, and how one function becomes the canonical world query that every later series — fish, splash, sound — will call without ever importing a mesh.

Step 1 · SoundingsThe shape of the bottom

Surveyors map a lake by running a boat along a bearing and recording depth as they go — a sounding line. We can do the same with a for-loop. The demo below slices the valley through the lake center at any bearing you choose, sampling terrainHeightAt seven hundred times per redraw and shading everything below y = 0 as water. The shape it reveals is deliberate: a flat-floored bowl whose main term digs 26 m, deepened to its true maximum — around 30 m — off-center, southwest of the middle, under the steepest wall. The floor carries a few meters of gentle noise relief (masked to fade before it reaches the shore band) so the bottom reads as silt and drift, not glass. Swing the bearing and watch the profile change while the deepest sounding marker chases the hole.

Aside · Overdeepening

A river can't dig below its own outlet — water has to flow downhill to leave. Glaciers can. Ice hundreds of meters thick plucks and grinds the bed wherever it flows fastest, which is under the thickest ice near the headwall, not at the snout. The result is a basin scooped deeper than its lip: an overdeepened floor that becomes a lake the moment the ice is gone. That's why our deepest sounding hugs the headwall side while the lake spills over a 4 m outlet sill — a shape no river could have carved, and the signature of every cirque tarn in every mountain range on Earth.

Step 2 · Shore continuityOne surface, no seam

The most load-bearing property of the bathymetry is the least dramatic: the function doesn't know where the waterline is. The beach ramp from part one crosses zero at 6.5% grade and simply keeps going — the gravel you'd land on continues underwater as the gravel a fish cruises over, with the same slope, the same noise, the same normals. Compare that with the common alternative: authoring land and lakebed as separate meshes and hiding the seam under the waterline. Then every system that crosses the boundary — a wading bird, a wave running up the beach, the camera following a dive — has to negotiate two surfaces that almost agree. Ours cross without noticing. The demo below is a close-up of the east shore with the water sheet at 42% opacity; toggle it off and try to find where “beach” becomes “bed.” There is nothing to find.

Step 3 · The queryterrainHeightAt, canonically

With the surface continuous above and below water, we can promote it to a contract. The terrain library's entire public story is one signature: 

terrainHeightAt(x, z) → y       // meters; negative under the lake
waterDepthAt(x, z) = max(0, −terrainHeightAt(x, z))

and a promise: this function is the world. The exact version costs a dozen-plus gradient-noise evaluations, fine for dozens of queries a frame; swarms — wind influences, fish schools, scatter filters — go through a prebuilt bilinear lattice cache that answers in about 50 ns with the same shape. (The GPU twin from the clipmap post makes the same promise to vertices.) The probe below is the contract made tangible: drag anywhere and a plumb line drops from the spot, its readout quoting terrainHeightAt directly. Over land it reports ground height; over water, bed and depth — and the buoy at the top of the line rides waterHeightAt(x, z, t) from the shared water API, so you're watching the two canonical world queries, terrain and water, measure the gap the fish will live in.

Step 4 · Ridge liftThe terrain joins the weather

One more system was waiting on a real terrain function. Invisible Rivers derived ridge lift from the kinematic boundary condition — air can't flow into a hillside, so wind V meeting ground with gradient ∇h is forced upward at 

w = V · ∇h        // positive windward, negative on the lee

— but it demonstrated the idea over a stand-in slope. Now the influence reads the basin itself: the gradient comes from the terrain cache by central differences, the lift fades exponentially over a 90 m e-folding height above ground, lee-side sink is softened to 45% (no fail states extends to the weather), and the whole thing is clamped at ±7 m/s so cliff edges don't become catapults. Registered as an influence on the one shared wind field, it means every wind.sample() in the world now knows about the mountains — the flight model, the swaying trees from last post, eventually the clouds and the sound of the air itself. In the demo, nine hundred tracer particles ride the live field across the valley: warm streaks climb the windward faces, cool ones sink behind the crests. Swing the wind bearing and watch entire mountainsides switch from elevator to trap — that's the soaring economy of the finished game, emerging from a dot product.

The Basin is complete. Five posts ago the lake floated in a void; now one deterministic function holds a warped ridged cirque, a clipmap that serves it at every distance, painted altitude bands, a hundred thousand trees with published perches, and a lakebed that the wind, the water, and everything that swims will query by name. Which sets up the flagship. The next series builds the splash — a real fluid simulation living in a moving window around the action — and its very first equations need what we just shipped: waves slow and steepen as √(g·depth), so the sim's depth term reads waterDepthAt straight from this bathymetry. The water is about to find out about the bottom. That story starts at Waves as Arithmetic.
