Four Forces

Fish Hawk is a game about flying, which means the flying has to be the best thing in it. This post builds the physical core: four forces on a point-mass bird, integrated every frame, with every constant traceable to a real osprey.

Scroll down to read ↓

Everything in Fish Hawk happens between takeoff and touchdown. You read the water for fish, you climb for a better view, you trade height for speed and speed for height — the whole game is a conversation with the air. So before we tune a single camera or play a single sound, the air has to push back honestly. We already have a bird — a mesh with working wings that knows how to beat them — and a lake for it to hunt. What we don't have is a reason for any of it to stay up.

Here's the central decision of this series: the flight model is a point mass. One position, one velocity, 1.5 kg. No rigid body, no torque, no inertia tensor. The bird's orientation is reconstructed afterward from the velocity and the player's bank command — it's presentation, not simulation. This sounds like cheating until you notice what the player actually feels: the trajectory. If the path through the air is right, the bird feels real; if the path is wrong, no amount of rigid-body fidelity will save it. A point mass also can't spin, tumble, or wedge itself into unrecoverable states — which matters in a game with no fail states.

Four forces act on that point: weight pulls down, lift pushes perpendicular to the airflow, drag pulls backward along it, and flap thrust pushes forward when the bird works. Sum them, divide by mass, integrate. Everything else in this series — stalls, dives, thermals, the dive scream we'll synthesize someday — falls out of these four arrows.

Step 1 · LiftAir pushes back

Start with what moving air is worth. A parcel of air with density ρ ≈ 1.2 kg/m³ moving at speed V carries kinetic energy ½ρV² per unit volume. That quantity — the dynamic pressure q — is the budget a wing gets to spend, and it grows with the square of speed. Fly twice as fast and the air is worth four times as much. This single quadratic is the engine of almost every flight sensation in the game.

A wing of area S converts that budget into a force at some efficiency C_L, the lift coefficient: 

L = ½ · ρ · V² · S · C_L(α)

C_L depends on the angle of attack α — the angle between the wing's chord and the oncoming air. Tilt the wing into the flow and it deflects more air downward; Newton's third law sends the reaction up. For small angles the relationship is beautifully linear. Thin airfoil theory gives a slope of 2π per radian for an infinite wing; a real, finite wing leaks pressure around its tips, and the correction for a wing of aspect ratio AR is a = 2π / (1 + 2/(e·AR)). Our osprey spans 1.6 m with about 0.30 m² of wing, so AR = b²/S ≈ 8.5, and with span efficiency e ≈ 0.9 the slope comes out almost exactly 5.0 per radian. That one number is the spine of the model.

Sanity check before we trust it. Level flight needs L = W = mg ≈ 14.7 N. Solve the lift equation for speed at a comfortable C_L of 0.9: V = √(2W / (ρ·S·C_L)) ≈ 9.5 m/s. Real ospreys cruise at almost exactly that. The constants aren't tuned to taste — they're tuned to a bird.

Aside · The bird in the numbers

Every constant here is a field measurement. An osprey (Pandion haliaetus) masses about 1.5 kg on a 1.6 m wingspan — an absurdly large wing for the weight, because it has to climb out of the water carrying a fish. It cruises near 10 m/s, dives at 15–30 m/s, and hits feet-first with a reversible outer toe that swings backward so two talons grip each side of the fish. The wing area of 0.3 m² gives a wing loading around 50 N/m² — closer to a glider than to a falcon, which is why the soaring in this game will feel so generous.

Step 2 · DragThe price of everything

Drag uses the same currency: D = ½ρV²S·C_D. The interesting part is what's inside C_D, because it has two terms with opposite personalities: 

C_D = C_D0  +  C_L² / (π · e · AR)

C_D0 ≈ 0.033 is profile drag — the cost of shoving a feathered body through air. It's constant in coefficient terms, so as a force it grows with V²: the tax on speed. The second term is induced drag, the cost of making lift itself. It scales with C_L², and since slow flight demands high C_L, induced drag is the tax on flying slowly. One tax rises with speed, the other falls — so somewhere in the middle is a best speed where the ratio L/D peaks. For our numbers that's C_L ≈ 0.89, around 9.6 m/s, giving a best glide ratio of about 13: every meter of altitude buys thirteen meters of distance, at a sink rate near 0.7 m/s. Those two numbers — thirteen-to-one, 0.7 down — are the resting heartbeat of the whole game. They're what the bird does when you do nothing.

In the demo below, the only control is the angle of attack. Pitch for too shallow an angle and the bird is fast but falls steeply; too steep and it wallows. Find the angle where the glide ratio readout peaks — you're feeling out the bottom of the drag bucket.

Step 3 · ThrustFlapping is an engine with a power curve

A flapping wing is doing something genuinely complicated — the downstroke tilts the lift vector forward so a slice of it becomes propulsion. For a point mass we keep the net effect and skip the blade physics: flapping applies a thrust force along the flight path. But we don't get to pick a free number, because muscles deliver power, not force, and power is force times speed: 

T = effort · min(T_max, P_max / V)

With P_max ≈ 95 W of burst output and a low-speed cap of T_max = 11 N, the same effort that blasts the bird off the water barely nudges it in a fast glide — thrust fades as 1/V, exactly the breathlessness you want when chasing speed by flapping. Cruising at moderate effort yields a climb rate around 1.5–2 m/s, which matches a real bird working its way up; full burst is for escapes and takeoffs, and the stamina system the game adds later will make you pay for it.

Aside · Why not simulate the wingbeat?

We could compute lift per wing segment per stroke phase — and the wingbeat rig could drive it. But a force that oscillates at 3 Hz mostly averages out in the trajectory, while costing stability headaches at every frame-rate hiccup. The model applies the average thrust; the visible wingbeat stays in the animation layer, synced through the same wingbeat clock. If they ever feel out of step, that's a bug in presentation, not physics.

Step 4 · IntegrationSum, divide, step

Now the loop. Each frame we sample the bird's airspeed vector — velocity relative to the air, which today equals velocity because the air is still (the wind field joins the party two posts from now, and the plumbing is already in place for it). From airspeed we get the forward direction; lift is built perpendicular to it; the four forces sum to an acceleration: 

F = L·l̂ − D·f̂ + T·f̂ + (0, −mg, 0)
v ← v + (F/m)·dt        // velocity first…
x ← x + v·dt            // …then position: semi-implicit Euler

Updating velocity before position — semi-implicit Euler — costs nothing and is dramatically more stable than the naive order for oscillating systems like ours. We also clamp dt and split big frames into substeps of at most 1/90 s, so a stuttering tab can't slingshot the bird. The scrolly below assembles the whole force diagram in one picture.

One more honest detail: the water at y = 0 is a floor, not a wall. Sink into it and the bird skims — vertical speed zeroes out, belly drag scrubs the rest, and the contact announces itself on the world's disturbance bus so that anything watching the surface (ripples now, a full splash simulation in a later season) knows exactly where and how hard we touched. A missed approach in Fish Hawk costs you speed and dignity, never progress.

What's missing is the third dimension of intent. You can climb and dive, but you can't go anywhere — there's no way to turn, and the camera is a disinterested bystander. Both of those are one idea in disguise: tilting the lift vector. The Stick takes the bank command from your keyboard or gamepad, leans the lift over, and lets the physics turn the bird — then bolts on a chase camera that makes speed legible.
