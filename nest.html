The Nest

A game needs somewhere for the days to add up. Fish Hawk's answer is a dead pine on a north-shore point, and a nest that fills in a few sticks at a time as you carry catches home. No quota, no timer, nothing to top up — just a place that slowly becomes a home. Click to fish the bay and fly the catch back.

By now the verbs are all here: you can spend a day, read the water for a fish, and dive a scored strike that lands a catch with real weight. What's missing is a reason to do it more than once — a thread that runs from one day to the next, so a handful of evenings on the lake feel like they were about something. That thread is the nest, and the hardest part of designing it was deciding how little it should ask.

The temptation, the moment you have a save file and a catch, is to bolt on a progression system: experience bars, upgrade trees, a resource you grind to refill. Every one of those would fight the tone. Fish Hawk is A Short Hike, an evening you sink into, not a treadmill you climb. So the nest is the smallest progression that still feels like growth: five stages, each a few meals apart, and once it's done it stays done. You can fish forever after, for the joy of it, and the game will never ask you for anything again.

Step 1 · Five states of homeThe cup, built across days

The nest lives at one fixed place: NEST_SNAG, a grand dead pine the Basin places on the north-shore promontory, chosen by scanning the shoreline for low flat ground with open water on most sides — exactly where a real osprey builds. Its flat crown is home, claimed the moment you arrive. From there it grows in five states: the bare spar, then first sticks wedged into the crown, then a real cup deep enough to disappear into, then a lined bowl of moss and grass, and finally the brood — three pale eggs, and a reason to be a fish hawk that goes past hunger. Each stage carries a journal line, because building the nest is itself a thing witnessed.

The geometry is deliberately humble and built by hand, no fancy growth simulation: a tapered spar, and rings of short twigs laid around the rim, faded in stage by stage so each meal visibly adds sticks. The state — which stage, how close to the next — lives in the same save file the rest of the game uses; the sticks are just that number, drawn. Aside · Ospreys and their nests

Real ospreys are famously loyal to a nest site, returning to the same platform year after year and adding to it each season until some grow to the size of a small car. They favor dead snags and the tops of broken pines for the clear sightlines and the open approach. Pairs build together — the male brings most of the sticks, the female arranges them — over a couple of weeks in spring. Fish Hawk compresses a season's building into a handful of days, but the shape of it, a bare crown becoming a deep lined cup, is true to the bird.

Step 2 · A nudge, not a quotaWhy there's no grind

Here is the entire progression curve: it takes three meals to build out of the first stage, four out of the next, then five, then six. That's it — small numbers that grow only gently, capped at a finished nest. There is no decay, so a nest never falls back; no timer, so you are never racing; no bar to keep topped up. A meal eaten at the nest counts toward the home; a meal eaten on the wing just answers your hunger. You choose, every catch, whether dinner goes down your own throat or toward the cup, and either choice is fine.

The only thing the game ever says about it is a single line of encouragement — nestGoal — phrased as a suggestion you can ignore. Hungry and the nest unbuilt: eat first, then bring one home. Comfortable: carry a catch to the snag to build toward the cup. Finished: the nest is done; the lake is yours to wander. The figure below shows that whole table — every stage crossed with every hunger word — so you can see there is nothing hiding behind it. It is one sentence, and it never scolds.

In the hero, that's the loop closed: fish the bay, carry the catch the long way home to the point, skim the spar, and feel the nest take one more stick. Sleep when the light goes and the day rolls over; a few days of that and the brood is in. Every system the game owns is now in place and proven on its own. What's left is the honest accounting — turning all of it on at once and asking whether it holds sixty frames a second — and then the game itself. Next: the reckoning.
