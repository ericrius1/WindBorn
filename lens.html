Crossing the Lens

Most games cut away the instant a camera touches water. Fish Hawk's camera goes through — and the half-second where the lens belongs to both worlds at once is what this post builds.

Scroll down to read ↓

Everything in Fish Hawk funnels toward one second of gameplay: the dive. The osprey tucks, hits the lake talons-first, and for a breath it is a submarine — then it breaches and climbs out with a fish. If the camera cuts to black at the surface, that second is gone; the payoff moment of the whole game would happen off-screen. So the camera has to follow the bird through the skin of the lake. We already built that skin looking down at it — the Fresnel mix, the water you can see into, the assembled dawn surface — and we taught a bird to float on it. What we have never done is put the eye on the wrong side. Crossing turns out to be three separate problems: knowing which world you're in, drawing the instant where you're in both, and cleaning up afterwards.

Step 1 · The state machineTwo worlds, one comparison

Which side of the surface is the camera on? The honest answer is one line: 

submerged = camera.y < waterHeightAt(camera.x, camera.z, t)

The function in that comparison matters more than the comparison. It's the spine's water query — the exact CPU function the floating osprey reads in Touch and Go, and the exact wave table the GPU displaces the rendered surface with. Because all three agree to the millimeter, the state flips at the precise frame the lens visually meets a wave crest — not at y = 0 while a swell is passing 4 cm above it. One source of truth, zero seams.

The state drives two color worlds. Air is the scene as we've always rendered it. Water is the same scene with three amendments, all of them physics we already own. First, a grade: a full-screen quad parented to the camera that multiplies the frame by the water's filter — the same Beer–Lambert per-channel exponentials, applied to the camera instead of a view ray, deepening as the camera sinks (red goes first, as always). Second, fog: every underwater material lerps toward the medium's own glow color with distance, because water is a participating medium and air, at lake scale, is not. Third, a different void: above water the sky dome backs every pixel; below, a camera-following dome takes over — bright toward the surface, blue-black below, with a warm lobe toward the sun's azimuth. Aside · Why a multiply, not a repaint

The underwater grade is multiplicative on purpose. Water doesn't add color to a scene — it subtracts, channel by channel, which is exactly what a multiply does. A bright sand patch stays brighter than a dark stone through any amount of grading because the filter scales both; an additive tint would crush that relationship and read instantly as "blue overlay" instead of "underwater." Cheap rule, big believability dividend.

Step 2 · The boundaryThe meniscus band

A real camera isn't a point. The housing has height, and for a moment during any crossing the waterline sits on the glass: water across the bottom of the frame, air across the top, and between them a dark, wobbling stripe where the water climbs the lens by surface tension. Photographers call shots like this over-unders, and that stripe — the meniscus — is the thing that makes them read as one continuous world instead of two stacked photos.

To draw it we need to know where the waterline crosses the frame. We project one point per frame on the CPU: take a spot a meter and a half ahead of the camera, snap its height to waterHeightAt, and run it through the camera's projection. Near the crossing — the only time the band is visible — that approximation lands within a couple of pixels of the truth, and it costs one matrix multiply. The shader turns that screen height into a line, wobbles it with two sine terms scaled by wind speed (waves slosh across the glass), and draws a Gaussian band around it: a dark body where the climbing film blocks light, a thin bright core where it lenses light instead. Aside · The band has a gate

The band's strength is gated by proximity: it fades in only when the lens sits within half a meter of the surface. Without the gate, the projected line would still exist when the camera is ten meters under — mathematically fine, visually nonsense. Most of the polish in this post is gates like this one: knowing when an effect must not appear is half of making it believable when it does.

Step 3 · The exitDroplets on the glass

Coming out is messier than going in. A wet lens keeps a film of water that beads up and runs, and those streaks are the single strongest "we were just underwater" cue available — so strong that we get it almost for free. The droplets are fully procedural: when the state machine reports an exit, we bump a counter and stamp the time. Each of a dozen streaks hashes its index against that counter for a position, a delay, and a lifetime, then slides down the frame on an accelerating curve (gravity feeding a streak through a film) while it fades. No particles, no allocations, no cleanup — a fresh dunk reseeds the hash and every streak finds a new home. Aside · The osprey's own wet lens

The bird solved this problem millions of years before housings existed. Ospreys carry a third eyelid — the nictitating membrane — a translucent screen that sweeps across the eye the instant before impact, so the strike is aimed with clear eyes and flown blind for the last meter. They also exit with a signature move we'll steal for the breach post: a full-body mid-air shake, like a dog, that sheds most of a soaked plumage's water in half a second. Our droplet decay constant is tuned to that rhythm.

OnwardWhat this unlocks

The camera can now do the one thing the dive demands: stay with the bird. State, grade, fog, void, band, droplets — the crossing machinery is built once, in one place, and every later scene gets it by parenting two quads to a camera. But the demos above quietly dodged a question. When the camera is down there looking back up, what exactly should the underside of the surface show? It is not a mirror, and it is not a window — it is both at once, split by a single critical angle, with the entire sky folded into a circle straight overhead. That circle has a name, an exact size, and a one-line derivation, and it's where we go next.
