The Cloud Layer

A sky without clouds is a screensaver. This post marches rays through a slab of noise and gets real cumulus back — self-shadowed, silver-lined, and drifting on the exact wind field that ripples the lake below. Turn the wind up and watch both answer.

Scroll down to read ↓

An osprey's working day is spent between two surfaces: the lake below and the cloud base above. For Fish Hawk the clouds are not wallpaper — they are the weather's face (next post's fronts arrive as this layer thickening), the light's moderator, and an honesty check on the wind field: if the clouds drift one way while the ripples run another, the world is lying, and players feel it before they can say it. So today we build cumulus the volumetric way — by marching view rays through a density field — and we wire its drift to wind.sample(), the same function the bird's wings read.

Step 1 · The fieldA cloud is a density function

Stop thinking of a cloud as an object. It is a region of air where water droplet density is nonzero — a scalar field ρ(p) — and our whole job is writing that function. We confine it to a slab between base and top (650 to 1500 meters here): cumulus bases are flat because that is the condensation level, the altitude where rising air saturates, so the field gets a hard floor and a soft dome via a vertical profile term. Inside the slab, shape comes from fractal value noise — the 3D cousin of the noise that built the terrain — squashed slightly so puffs sit wider than tall.

Two controls turn noise into weather. Coverage is a threshold: density is whatever pokes above 1 − coverage, so one slider walks the sky from a few fair-weather puffs to a closed deck — that is the knob the weather machine will own next post. Erosion subtracts a second, finer noise, weighted toward the edges, which carves the cauliflower detail without disturbing the solid cores. Step 2 · The marchBeer–Lambert, one step at a time

The fog post could integrate its layer in closed form because the density was a clean exponential. This field has no closed form — so we do it numerically. For each pixel whose ray enters the slab, take N steps. At each step, Beer–Lambert says the step transmits e^(−σ·ρ·Δt); whatever was absorbed, 1 − e^(−σ·ρ·Δt), is the chance this step scattered light toward you, so the step contributes that fraction of its local light, weighted by how much transmittance is left in front of it. Front to back: 

for each step:  L += T · (1 − e^(−σρΔt)) · light(p)
                T *= e^(−σρΔt)        (stop when T ≈ 0)

The only open question is light(p) — and it is the question. A cloud is lit by the sun through itself: bottoms are dark because the light crossed the whole cloud to reach them. So at each sample we run a second, shorter march toward the sun — four or five taps of cheap density — giving the self-shadowing that makes a cumulus look like it weighs something. Two corrections finish the look. The powder term re-brightens thin edges (in dense media, light that scattered several times still escapes near the rim; one Beer term alone over-darkens it). And the phase function is two Henyey–Greenstein lobes plus a third, very narrow forward spike — stand the sun behind a cloud below and that spike becomes the silver lining, the rim fire on the edge where the path through cloud is short and the forward-scattered sun barrels through nearly undeflected.

Aside · Sunset clouds are lit from below

Drag the hour slider to evening and the deck catches fire from underneath — no special case anywhere. The cloud's sun color is the atmosphere's transmitted sun from part one, already reddened by its long path, and at low elevations the light march approaches the slab nearly horizontally, so undersides stop being self-shadowed. Physics keyframes the sunset for us.

Step 3 · The budgetSixty frames, honestly bought

Raymarching is the most expensive thing this site has done: every cloud pixel costs N · (one detailed density + a few cheap ones), and every density is a stack of hashed noise octaves. The budget is bought three ways, all declared. Fewer steps, jittered: 16–20 steps would band visibly, so each pixel offsets its first step by a hash — banding becomes grain, and grain reads as texture. Cheap noise: value noise costs 8 hashes a sample against Perlin's 8 gradients; at cloud scale the quality difference is invisible. Lower internal resolution: the hero up top renders at 62% and lets the browser stretch — clouds are soft; nobody can tell. The readouts own up to all of it. Budgets are design decisions, not apologies.

Step 4 · The driftThe same wind the bird rides

Finally, motion. Every frame we sample the spine wind at mid-slab altitude — wind.sample(0, 1075, 0, t) — and advect the density field's lookup offset by it. That one line is the post's thesis: ripples (which read the wind at the surface), the gusts the bird will lean on, and the clouds overhead all consult the same vector field, so when you swing the wind direction below, the whole world agrees at once. The gusts even give the layer a believable wobble, because the sample point rides through the same noise the wings feel.

Aside · Real cumulus move surprisingly fast

Fair-weather cumulus typically ride winds of 5–10 m/s at base height — watch a cloud shadow cross a valley and you can clock it. Players consistently underestimate this and read true-speed clouds as "too fast", which is why we sample the wind where the clouds actually live instead of applying a fudge factor: if it ever needs slowing down for feel, that will be a declared game decision, not a hidden one.
