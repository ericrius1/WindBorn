The Folding Wing

Three joints, one triangle, and an ancient theorem. We rig the osprey's arm — shoulder, elbow, wrist — and solve it in closed form, so the whole journey from sleeping bundle to 1.6-meter span is a single number.

Scroll down to read ↓

The osprey we lofted last time is frozen at full span. That was deliberate — flat, extended wings are the honest shape to model feathered surfaces in — but Fish Hawk needs the other shapes constantly. Perched on the snag, the wings disappear into the body. In a dive they sweep back to slivers. In a glide they crank into the M. Between any two of those, the player is watching the wing travel, and the travel has to look like an arm folding, not like a morph between meshes. So today the bird gets joints, and the joints get a solver.

The skeleton is free, because the parts table from part one already is one: every row has a pivot, a far end, and a parent. Each row becomes a bone — a joint holding one rotation — and the skin we lofted comes along because every ring wrote down, at construction time, which joints it belongs to. Body rings follow the body, neck rings blend into the head, and each wing ring splits its loyalty between the two nearest arm segments, so the membrane creases smoothly at the elbow instead of cracking.

Step 1 · ForwardThe chain rule of poses

Before solving anything backward, feel the forward direction. A joint's world transform is its parent's world transform times its own local rotation — that's the whole theory. Rotate the shoulder and the forearm, hand, and every feather riding them swing along; rotate the wrist and only the hand moves. One small craft decision pays off everywhere: in rest pose our bones are pure translations, world-axis aligned, which means a joint's local x, y, z rotations read as world pitch, yaw, roll. Forward kinematics is honest but backwards from what animation wants. Nobody thinks "set the shoulder to −38°, the elbow to 64°". They think: the wingtip should be there. Going from a desired tip position to the angles that put it there is inverse kinematics, and for a limb with two main segments it needs no iteration at all.

Step 2 · BackwardThe triangle that solves itself

Fix the shoulder S, pick a target T, and call the upper and lower segment lengths a and b. The moment those are chosen, the elbow has almost nowhere left to be: it must sit at distance a from the shoulder and distance b from the target. Three side lengths — a, b, and d = |T−S| — determine a triangle completely, and the law of cosines hands over its angles: 

cos A = (a² + d² − b²) / (2·a·d)

A is the angle at the shoulder between the line to the target and the upper segment. Rotate the shoulder→target direction by A, walk distance a, and you're standing on the elbow. Two details make it robust. First, clamp d into [|a−b|, a+b]: a target beyond reach straightens the chain toward it and waits, instead of producing a NaN. Second, notice the ambiguity — in 2D the two circles intersect twice, and in 3D the elbow can sit anywhere on a whole circle around the shoulder→target axis. Which point you pick is not a math question, it's a style question, and the conventional answer is a pole vector: a direction the elbow would like to point. Birds fold their elbows back and slightly up, so that's our default pole.

Step 3 · On the birdThree bones, two passes, one roll

Our wing has three segments, not two, but the hand doesn't deserve a general solver. Treat it by successive approximation: guess the hand's direction (shoulder toward target), pull the two-bone target back by one hand-length, solve shoulder and elbow, then re-aim the hand from wherever the wrist actually landed and solve once more. Two passes land the tip within a feather's width, every frame, for the price of a few subtractions.

Position is only half a wing, though. A wing segment is a blade — it has a flat side that must face somewhere — and a direction alone leaves that roll undefined. So each bone's orientation is built from two vectors: its solved direction plus a normal hint saying where "up" is, orthonormalized into a frame; the rotation between the rest frame and the desired frame is the bone's quaternion, with zero twist left to chance. Spread wide, the hint is straight up; folded against the body it rotates sideways so the wing stacks flat against the flank. On top of that, a twist parameter pitches the leading edge — shared out a quarter to the humerus, more to the forearm, all of it to the hand, because primaries are the propeller blades. Aside · The wing that folds itself

A real bird doesn't manage three joints independently either. The elbow and wrist are coupled by tendons and a sheet of skin called the propatagium along the leading edge, so extending the elbow automatically extends the wrist — the wing unfurls as one motion, like a self-opening umbrella. Our IK gets the same economy a different way: because the solver owns all three joints, one moving target produces exactly that coordinated unfurl.

Step 4 · The foldOne scalar, the whole journey

Now compress the whole behavior into one knob. The folded tip rests against the flank, primaries crossed over the tail base — a point we keep as a constant. The extended tip reaches out at 97% of the chain's total length, level with the shoulder. In between, the tip shouldn't travel a straight line (a chord would drag it through the body), so it rides the sphere around the shoulder: direction slerps from folded to extended while the radius lerps. The elbow, biased backward by the pole, passes through every intermediate Z of a real fold without being told to. f = 0 is a sleeping bird; f = 1 is the full 1.6-meter span; the arc below is drawn in blue.

The wing now goes anywhere we point it, smoothly, for nanoseconds of math per frame. What it lacks is a reason to move: nothing yet says when the tip should be high or low, swept forward or folded home. That's a clock's job — a strange, lopsided clock that spends more time pushing down than recovering up. Next post we build the wingbeat, and the targets start dancing.
