The Skin of the Lake

Fish Hawk begins where the osprey's eyes do: at the surface of the water. Before a single wave moves, the lake has to read as water — and that turns out to be one ratio, evaluated per pixel. Drag the hero to skim it.

Scroll down to read ↓

I'm building a game called Fish Hawk. You play an osprey on a mountain lake: you soar, you spot fish, you fold your wings and hit the water talons-first. Every system in the game hangs off that lake — the fish telegraph themselves through its surface, the dive ends inside it, the dawn light lives on it. So the lake surface is the first thing I'm building, and this series builds it piece by piece: today the optics of a still surface, then wind ripples, then true reflections, then the view down to the lakebed, and finally the glass-dawn scene the game opens on.

Here's the problem with water: it has almost no appearance of its own. A bucket of lake water is faintly tinted nothing. Yet you have never once mistaken a lake for a sheet of blue plastic. Whatever your eye is keying on, it isn't color — and if we can find it, we can put it on a flat polygon plane and your brain will say water before anything in the scene moves.

Step 1 · Two fates for a photonThe Fresnel ratio

Light arriving at the surface has exactly two options: bounce off, or bend in. Water is a dielectric — an insulator — so there's no third "absorbed at the surface" option the way there is for metals; the skin itself eats nothing. The split between the two fates is the Fresnel reflectance F, and it depends on just two things: the angle you're looking at, and the index of refraction n — how much slower light travels in the medium. For fresh water, n = 1.333.

Looking straight down along the surface normal, the reflectance has a closed form you can do in your head: 

F₀ = ((n − 1) / (n + 1))²  =  (0.333 / 2.333)²  ≈  0.02

Two percent. Stare straight down into a calm lake and the surface is barely there — 98% of the light crosses it, which is why you see stones, not sky, at your feet. But tilt the geometry toward a grazing angle and F climbs — slowly at first, then violently — until at the horizon essentially everything reflects. The full angle dependence is given by the Fresnel equations, which fall out of asking that the light's wave fields stay continuous across the boundary. Scroll the diagram and watch the split change:

In the shader we don't pay for the exact equations. Schlick noticed the curve is basically a quintic ramp between F₀ and 1: 

F(θ) ≈ F₀ + (1 − F₀) · (1 − cos θ)⁵

where θ is the angle between the surface normal and the direction to your eye. One dot product, one pow. The demo below paints F itself as brightness — white means mirror — on a plane and a sphere. Aside · Why the approximation is safe

For water's n = 1.333, Schlick stays within about one percentage point of the exact unpolarized reflectance across the whole angle range — far below anything your eye can register in a moving scene. The exact form needs a Snell solve and two squared ratios per pixel; the quintic needs almost nothing. We keep the exact version around in the library purely for demos like the one above, as a referee.

Step 2 · A million tiny mirrorsRoughness and the glitter path

Fresnel says how much light reflects. Roughness says where it goes. A perfectly flat surface reflects the sun to exactly one point — to see the glint you'd have to stand in precisely the mirror spot. Real water is a carpet of tiny tilted facets, each one a flat mirror at its own angle. A facet tilted by α redirects the sun's image by 2α, so the spread of facet slopes turns the single sun-point into a smeared lobe of glints.

We model the lobe the cheap, classical way: raise the alignment between the mirror direction and the sun to a power, glint = (R · L)ˢ, where R is the eye ray reflected about the normal and L points at the sun. The exponent s plays the role of inverse roughness; the mapping s = 2/r² − 2 sends a roughness r → 0 to a near-delta spike and r = 0.5 to a wide soft smear. One detail matters for the look: the smear isn't round. Facet tilts move the reflected sun mostly vertically in your view when the sun is low, so the glints stretch into a long road of light running from the sun straight toward your feet — the glitter path. Aside · The osprey's stake in this

The glitter path isn't decoration — in Fish Hawk it's gameplay. A cruising fish tilts the facets above it, and for a frame or two the glint pattern breaks: a flash where no flash belongs. Real ospreys hunt exactly this signal, patrolling with the sun behind them so the glints betray the fish. The game's "spot" verb is, at bottom, this demo with a fish under it.

Step 3 · Assembling the skinWhy it reads as water

Now stack the two ideas on a plane. Each pixel of the surface gets three terms:

Body. What comes up from below. For now — deep water, no lakebed yet — that's a dark blue-green constant: the color scattering hands back when light wanders a long path through the water column and crawls back out. It's the closest thing water has to "its" color, and it's nearly black.

Reflection. What bounces. Today the mirror image is just an analytic sky — a graded dome with a sun in it. (Reflecting actual geometry needs a second render of the scene, and that's its own essay.)

Glint. The roughness lobe from Step 2, pointed at the sun.

The skin is one line:

color = mix(body, reflection, F) + glint

— with F the Schlick ramp doing all the real work. Near your feet, F ≈ 2% and you see the dark body; toward the horizon, F → 1 and the lake becomes pure sky. That brightness gradient — dark below you, mirror far away, with the curve's particular knee around 60–70° — is the signature your visual system has logged from every shoreline it has ever seen. Aside · The two percent that builds the world

It's worth pausing on how strange this is: the difference between "plastic" and "lake" is mostly a 2%-to-100% ramp on borrowed light. Water barely shows you itself — it shows you everything else, weighted by angle. That's also why the rest of this series exists: a material that's 98% "everything else" is only as good as the everything else we give it to reflect and transmit.

OnwardWhat this unlocks

We now have a surface that reads as water while standing perfectly still — a skin with honest optics and placeholder surroundings. Everything after this is upgrades to its inputs. Next we give the normals something real to do: wind. The ripples that break the glitter path and texture the reflection are a sum of traveling waves steered by the same wind field that will someday hold the osprey's wings up — one weather system for the whole world. After that, the sky dome gives way to true mirrored geometry, the black body color gives way to a sunlit lakebed, and the pieces close into the dawn scene the game opens on. One ratio down; a lake to go.
