The Wet Lens

The most violent thing that happens to Fish Hawk's camera is the moment it goes through the lake. This post rebuilds that crossing as a function of the rendered frame: droplets that refract, a filter that deepens with every metre, and a waterline that bends the light as it sweeps past.

Scroll down to read ↓

The dive is the whole game in one second, and we have built it twice now. The breach assembled the physics — the plunge, the submarine beat, the burst back into air — and Crossing the Lens built the optics the first way anyone builds them: two quads parented to the camera, one tinted for the air side and one for the water side, alpha-blended over whatever was behind them. That works, and it shipped. But a quad can only ever paint over the frame. It can darken a pixel, tint it, or hide it — it cannot move it. And water on glass does exactly one thing a quad can't: it refracts. A droplet is a tiny lens; the meniscus where the waterline meets the housing is a bigger one. Now that the speed pass has taught us to treat the rendered frame as a texture we can resample, the wet lens stops being a sticker and becomes what it always physically was: a per-pixel function of the image, sitting in the chain right where the grade already lives.

Step 1 · DropletsThe frame is a texture we can bend

Start with the cheapest, most convincing piece: the beads of water left clinging to the lens after a breach. A real droplet is a lens because it is a bump — a little dome of water whose curved surface bends rays passing through it. So we model each droplet as a height field: a Gaussian bulb, h = exp(−d²/r²), with a thin trailing ridge where the bead has begun to slide. The optics then fall straight out of the height. A surface tilted by a height field h deflects a ray by an amount proportional to that surface's slope, the gradient ∇h. For our Gaussian that gradient is analytic — ∂h/∂x = −2x/r² · h — so each droplet contributes a small uv offset ∇h · refraction, and we sample the frame at uv + offset instead of uv. Fourteen droplets, three hashes each for position and size, reseeded the instant the camera breaches. Where their slopes face the key light we add a glint; where they're steep we bend the frame hardest. Push the refraction up and watch the lake warp behind each bead:

Aside · Slope is the whole trick

It's tempting to think a droplet refracts because it's bright, or because it's round. Neither is the cause — a flat film of water on glass barely distorts anything, and a perfectly hemispherical droplet seen dead-centre doesn't either. What bends light is the change in surface height across a pixel, the gradient. That's why our droplets distort most around their edges, where the dome falls away steepest, and why the centre of a big bead stays almost clear. Model the height, take the derivative, and the optics come for free.

Step 2 · Two color worldsA filter that deepens with depth

Above the surface the frame is graded by the hour. Below it, the water itself is the colorist. We met the physics back in Seeing the Bottom: water absorbs light exponentially with the distance it travels, and it does so unevenly — long wavelengths first. Red is gone within a couple of metres, then orange and yellow; blue-green outlasts everything, which is why depth reads as that particular drained teal. As a post effect this is one multiply per channel, keyed to how deep the camera sits: 

filter = tint · exp(−depth · [k_r, k_g, k_b]),   k_r ≫ k_g > k_b

with red's absorption coefficient several times the blue's. We layer a second, gentler move on top — as colors drain, the survivors converge slightly toward the medium's own teal, the scattering that fills deep water with an ambient glow rather than pure darkness. The blend between the air grade and the water grade rides the crossing's submergence, a smoothed 0-to-1 that the state machine raises the moment the lens dips under. Step 3 · The meniscusMagnifying the half-world below the line

The hardest half-second is neither fully above nor fully below: it's the instant the waterline crosses the lens, when the frame is split and the surface itself bows across the glass. Where water meets the housing it climbs into a curved lip — the meniscus — and that lip acts as a cylindrical lens, magnifying the strip of world just beneath the line and smearing a dark band along it. We need three things: to know where the line is, to bend the frame inside the band, and to darken the band's body with a brightened core where the film lenses the light through.

Finding the line reuses the exact projection Crossing the Lens derived — one point on the water surface, 1.5 m ahead of the camera, projected to screen space gives the waterline's height; a pair of sliding sines wobble it as waves slide across the glass. Inside the band, just below that line, we push the sample coordinates toward the line — compressing the frame upward, which is exactly what magnification looks like as a resample. Then we lay the dark meniscus body over it, exp(−g²) in the band coordinate g, with a tight bright core where the lip focuses the sky. Aside · Why one comparison decides everything

Every part of this effect — which grade to apply, where the band sits, when to seed droplets — keys off a single number: the camera's height minus waterHeightAt under it, the same query every floating body on the lake already makes. That's deliberate. If the housing used its own idea of where the surface was, the colour would flip a frame before or after the line you can see, and the eye catches that instantly. One source of truth means the moment the look changes is exactly the moment the rendered surface crosses the lens. No drift, no second opinion.

Step 4 · The crossingOne dive, every stage handing off

Now stack them in the only order that works. The wet lens is a resampler — it moves pixels — so it runs on the linear frame, before any colour work, alongside the speed pass's blur and DOF. That placement is the payoff of building the whole series as a chain: a droplet that bends the frame samples colour that hasn't been graded yet, so when the hour grade and the tone map run downstream, the refracted tap and its surroundings get coloured together. Per-pixel grading commutes with resampling; do the bending first and the seam never shows. Watch one dive run the full circuit — descend and the filter deepens, hold at depth for the full underwater world, breach and the band sweeps up the frame as the droplets seed and the colours drain back to air. OnwardWhat this unlocks

The chain now owns the camera through the one transition that used to force a cut: it grades by the hour, smears and focuses for speed, and refracts through water without a single quad glued to the lens. The hero up top is the whole post running live — plunge, hold, breach — through grade, bloom, and the wet lens at once. That closes the cinematic loop for playing the game. But Fish Hawk has a fifth verb we've barely touched: not soaring or spotting or diving, but witnessing — the slow pleasure of stopping the world and looking at it. The last post in this series hands the player the camera itself: freeze the clock, drift a free lens through the frozen moment, line it up against the rule of thirds, and fill a journal that earns its own entries.
