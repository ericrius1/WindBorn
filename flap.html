The Flap Cycle

A wingbeat is a loop, but it is not a sine. We warp the clock so the downstroke gets the time it pays for, fold the wrist on the way up, compress every flight style into one number, and let the feathers run a beat behind.

Scroll down to read ↓

In Fish Hawk, flapping is the expensive verb. Soaring is free, gliding is cheap, but every wingbeat will eventually burn stamina — which means the player will be watching wingbeats, judging them, rationing them. An animation the player studies that closely can't be a canned clip. It has to be built from the same parts the gameplay reads: a rate, an amplitude, a rhythm. Luckily we ended the last post holding exactly the right tool — a wing that flies to wherever its tip target sits. A wingbeat is just a target that moves in a loop. The whole question of this post is: what loop?

Step 1 · The clockNot a sine

Run the tip up and down on cos(2πt) and the result is instantly, bizarrely wrong — a bird treading water. The reason is that flapping is not symmetric work. The downstroke is the power stroke: wing fully spread, blade biting, the stroke that pays for everything. The upstroke is overhead — recovery between payments. A real bird spends more of each period on the half that pays, then hurries home.

So we warp time. Let phase p run 0→1 per beat and give the downstroke a share downFrac of it. Inside the downstroke, the stroke angle θ sweeps 0→π at one speed; the upstroke sweeps π→2π faster. Tip height is still cos θ — the shape is untouched, only the timing bends. At downFrac = 0.57 (our cruise) the descent lingers and the recovery snaps, and the same three numbers fall out of θ for free: vertical position, fore-aft sweep (sin θ — the tip draws a loop, not a line), and a bell curve over the upstroke that we'll use in a moment.

Step 2 · The strokeOffsets on the target

The clock's outputs become offsets on the wing target, layered onto whatever baseline the wing already wanted: vertical excursion amp·vert, fore-aft amp·sweep scaled by a stroke-plane tilt (a hovering bird rows air downward and backward, so its stroke plane tips back), and blade twist proportional to −sweep, pitching the leading edge down exactly during the power stroke the way the air would. The fourth offset is the most birdlike: during the upstroke, that bell curve multiplies the extension down, folding the wrist by up to a third. Aside · Real numbers

Cruising ospreys beat their wings at roughly 2.5–3.2 Hz — slow and rangy compared to a crow, exactly what 1.6 m of span predicts. And when one pauses mid-hunt to inspect a shadow, it "kites": body pitched steeply, stroke plane tilted far back, beating faster and deeper just to stand still on the wind. That posture is a point in our knob space, which is the entire idea of the next step.

Step 3 · GaitsHow she's flying, in one number

Collect those knobs — rate multiplier, amplitude, downstroke share, upstroke fold, twist, baseline extension and tip height, stroke-plane tilt, body pitch, tail spread — and any particular flight style is a row of values. We keep three rows. Hover: steep body, huge deep strokes, plane tilted way back, tail fanned. Cruise: the workhorse beat, moderate everything. Glide: amplitude collapsed to a residual breathing, wrists up, tips drooped below — the M — and the flap mix at zero. Then flight state compresses to a single scalar: gait01 runs hover→cruise→glide and every parameter lerps along it. Two details make the morph seamless. First, plain linear blending is actually safe here, because every parameter in the table is smooth and means the same thing in every row — there's no "blend a walk with a handstand" failure case hiding between hover and cruise. Second, and more important: the clock never resets. Phase keeps accumulating through any gait change, so moving the slider mid-beat just means the current stroke finishes under new management. Watch the wings when you slam from glide back to hover — no snap, because amplitude is the only thing that changed, and the stroke was already in progress at zero amplitude. The body joins in too: the gait carries its own pitch (a hovering bird stands nearly upright on the air) and the root bobs gently against the stroke, the reaction force made visible.

Step 4 · FeathersThe lag that sells it

Watch slow footage of any large bird and the wing never moves as one rigid sheet: the primaries arrive late. Each feather is a cantilevered beam loaded by air — it bends away from the motion and springs back. We fake the physics with the cheapest spring there is: each feather carries a damped spring that chases the stroke's vertical offset, and the feather deflects by how far behind it's running — deflection = (spring − driver) × gain, clamped to a sane angle and applied as one rotation at the quill. When the wing slams down, the spring hasn't caught up yet, so the difference is positive and the vane bends up — exactly what the air would do, derived from nothing but lag. Outer primaries get more gain and a lazier frequency (a longer beam is a softer beam), secondaries barely move, and an underdamped ζ around 0.5 leaves a flutter ringing at each stroke reversal. Forty-odd springs, one multiply-add each, and this is the single biggest jump in perceived quality of the whole series. The springs get their full derivation in the next post; here, just feel the difference — toggle them off and the bird turns to tin.

The osprey now flies in place: she hovers, cruises, glides, and her feathers ride the beat a breath behind. But everything so far is steady-state — a bird holding one behavior forever. The game is made of the other moments: the instant she folds into a dive, throws everything forward to brake, swings her feet to strike. Those are actions, and they deserve their own machinery — pose targets, blended by springs, layered right on top of this gait. That's next.
