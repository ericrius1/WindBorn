The Far Shore

An osprey sees gravel under its talons and the far rim of the valley in the same glance. This post makes one mesh do both: nested rings of detail that follow the camera, kilometers of terrain on a vertex budget that never grows.

Scroll down to read ↓

Carving the Cirque left us with a beautiful problem. The terrain is an analytic function — ask h(x, z) anywhere, get the mountain — but a mesh has to sample it, and the sampling rate is a brutal trade. Fish Hawk's bird crosses the whole valley in a minute of gliding and then dives to wingtip height over the beach. The same frame has to hold meter-scale gravel in the foreground and ridgelines four kilometers away — and it has to do it at 60 fps while the flight model, the water, and eventually a fluid simulation all bid for the same millisecond. Today's post is pure budget engineering, and it ends with the camera flying to the far shore without the mesh ever getting bigger.

Step 1 · The budgetWhy uniform grids lose

Put numbers on the pain first. Suppose the bird needs 1.5 m cells to make landing approaches read well. A uniform grid at that density covering the valley's ±3.2 km is (6400 / 1.5)² ≈ 18 million vertices — and every one of them runs our height function, a dozen-plus gradient-noise evaluations, in the vertex shader. Quadrupling the covered area quadruples the cost forever. The demo below holds the vertex count fixed at 37k and lets you stretch the area it covers: small extents look crisp and end at an absurd horizon; large extents reach the rim but turn the beach to mush. One knob, two failures. The readout keeps score of what a uniform 1.5 m grid would have cost.

The way out is in the camera's own geometry. Perspective projection scales everything by 1/distance — a 1.5 m cell 40 m away covers the same pixels as a 3 m cell 80 m away. So a mesh whose cell size doubles with distance from the viewer has roughly constant detail per pixel, everywhere, and its vertex count grows with the logarithm of the covered radius instead of its square. Doubling rings, not uniform grids.

Step 2 · Rings of detailThe clipmap

The classic shape of that idea is the geometry clipmap: a stack of square levels, all centered on the viewer. Level 0 is a full n×n-cell grid at the finest cell size. Every level above it has the same n×n footprint in a doubled cell size, with the middle n/2 × n/2 cells cut out — because the finer level below already covers them. Each level therefore covers twice the radius of the one before, and the whole stack covers 2^levels times the base extent on levels × roughly n² vertices. The hero above runs seven levels of 96 cells: ±4.6 km of valley on about 55 thousand vertices — three hundred times fewer than the uniform grid wanted.

Because the terrain is analytic, the rings carry no data. Vertices are dumb lattice points in cell units; the vertex shader multiplies by the level's cell size, adds the shared center, and evaluates terrainHeight(world) from last post's GPU twin. There is no heightmap to page in, no streaming, no seams between tiles that were baked at different times. A vertex knows its height wherever it lands. The demo colors each level so you can see the machinery: drag to orbit, and notice the rings chase the glowing marker, not the camera — the focus point is a parameter, which the game will set to the bird.

Aside · Why “clipmap”

The name comes from texturing: a mipmap stores a texture at every power of two resolution, and a clipmap clips each level to a fixed window around the viewer, because that's all you can ever see of it. Geometry clipmaps apply the same windowing to a terrain lattice. The insight that survives in our version is just the stack of nested, viewer-centered, power-of-two windows — ours never touches a texture, since the “data” is an equation.

Step 3 · SeamsWhere two resolutions meet

Every boundary between levels is a lie waiting to show. The fine ring's edge has vertices the coarse ring lacks — T-junctions. A fine edge vertex sitting between two coarse ones samples the true terrain there, but the coarse triangle spanning it draws the straight line between its neighbors. The fine vertex floats above or below that line, and the gap is a pixel-thin crack with daylight (or in the demo below, a hot red detector plane) shining through.

The fix is to make the fine level become the coarse one before they touch. Each vertex with an odd lattice index stores the axis toward its even neighbors; near the outer edge of its level it morphs its height toward their average: 

h = mix(h_fine, (h_even₁ + h_even₂) / 2, m)   // m: 0 inside → 1 at the boundary

At m = 1 the odd vertex sits exactly on the coarse triangle edge — the two levels agree vertex-for-vertex, by construction. The blend ramps over the outer 30% of each ring, so the resolution change is a gradient you can't point to, not a line you can. (One subtlety: diagonal vertices, odd in both axes, average their two diagonal neighbors, which only works because every quad is split along the same diagonal.) Rasterization can still leave sub-pixel pinholes where the two meshes overlap edge-to-edge, so each level also drops a skirt — a short curtain hanging from its outer edge. Toggle morph and skirts below under grazing light, with the crack detector behind everything: red pinholes are the bug, and you get to turn the fix on yourself.

Step 4 · SnappingStanding still while moving

One problem left, and it's the sneakiest. If the rings glide continuously with the camera, every vertex continuously resamples the terrain at new (x, z) — and the whole landscape shimmers as each vertex slides along the surface it samples. The mountains appear to crawl. The cure is to move the lattice in quantized hops: the clipmap's center snaps to a multiple of twice the finest cell, so wherever the window lands, its vertices fall on exactly the same world positions as last time. Same positions, same heights — the world stands perfectly still while the window slides over it, and geometry only changes at the rim of each ring, far from where you're looking.

The demo glides across the valley in wireframe so you can watch the lattice hop while the ridgelines hold absolutely still. The snap-level slider coarsens the hop: snapping at ring 4's pitch moves the grid rarely but by 48 m at once — a visible lurch at the periphery. Snapping at the finest pitch (our default) hops 3 m at a time, far below notice.

The valley now scales: the same 55k vertices serve a landing flare and a four-kilometer vista, and the budget line in the frame profiler doesn't move when the bird does. But everything we've drawn is bare rock wearing a debug tint. A mountain isn't gray — it's meadow up to where the soil thins, scree where rock sheds, snow where it can rest, and a treeline that draws the most legible contour in the landscape. Painting all of that, per-pixel, from nothing but altitude and slope, is The Treeline.
