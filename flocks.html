Other Wings

The evening airspace: swallows working the surface, a skein of geese in transit overhead, and an eagle climbing its invisible staircase of thermals. Three species, three draw calls.

Scroll down to read ↓

A lake with one bird on it is a tech demo. The osprey we lofted, flapped, and taught to fly needs an airspace that was busy before the player arrived and stays busy when they leave — that's what makes a world read as inhabited rather than instanced. But the other wings aren't scenery either. Each one earns a gameplay job: the swallows demonstrate surface-reading (their skim runs write the same bus-driven ripples the player learns to hunt by), the geese are the long-distance clock of the valley (a V crossing at altitude is dusk's punctuation mark), and the eagle is a promise of future trouble — in the finished game it will occasionally try to take your fish on the carry home. This post builds all three: meshes first, then a controller per species, because the interesting thing about these birds is that they need three different kinds of intelligence.

The meshes reuse the direct-loft kit from the osprey build — body as elliptical rings on a spine curve, wings as tapered two-layer vanes (dark above, pale below, because a bird overhead and a bird below you are different colors), everything faceted and merged into one geometry per species. No skeletons this time: the flap is a vertex-stage sine that lifts everything outboard of a body hinge, phase-hashed per instance, so an entire flock beats out of step in a single instanced draw call. A whole evening of birds costs three.

Step 1 · The swallowsSkim runs on the bus

Swallows are the lake's other surface-readers — they hawk insects in long flat passes centimeters off the water. Each bird runs a four-state machine: wander (banked meandering in the 3–10 m band, on a timer), dive (commit to a run: an entry point and a heading), skim (the heart of it), and climb back out. During the skim the bird holds a stiff spring on its altitude against the live surface — waterHeightAt(x, z, t) plus a 7 cm clearance — so it rides the ambient waves it's flying over rather than a flat plane. And every 1.7 m of travel it pays the toll that makes the whole system sing: 

disturb({ kind: "skim", x, z, energy: 0.006, radius: 0.05, vx, vz })

A wingtip kiss is a tiny event — about a hundredth of a fish rise — but the surface renders each one as a small ring, and a completed run reads as a dotted line of fading rings across the lake, precisely the signature real swallows leave on calm evening water. Note what we did not write: any code connecting swallows to ripples. The bird announces; the bus delivers; the water decides what a skim looks like. That decoupling is why one little event type keeps paying for itself series after series.

Step 2 · The geeseDeriving the V

Why do geese fly in a V? Not togetherness — economics. A wing makes lift by throwing air down, and that downwash has to curl back up somewhere: past each wingtip the air rolls into a trailing vortex, downwash between the tips, upwash outboard of them. For a wing of span b with a roughly elliptical lift distribution, the two trailing vortices behave as if separated by πb/4 — narrower than the wingspan itself — so the rising air starts at πb/8 from the leader's centerline, roughly 60 cm for a goose. Free lift, if you can park a wing in it.

So a following goose wants its inner wingtip riding just outboard of the bird ahead's vortex core. Convert that to a slot for the body: offset = πb/8 + b/2 plus a little slack — about 1.5 m to the side, a couple of meters behind, a hair high (the upwash is rising air, after all). Now the punchline: that's the entire formation logic. Every goose runs one rule — chase the slot behind your same-side predecessor with a critically damped spring — and the V assembles itself, leg by leg, each bird's choice of side propagating down the line. The V's half-angle isn't authored anywhere; it's atan(offset / gap), emergent from aerodynamics. Widen the wingspan slider below and the V obediently opens. Then hit the toggle that turns off vortex-seeking: same birds, same following instinct, zero physics — and you get a dumb queue. The V is the aerodynamics, visible from the ground.

Aside · Measured, not just modeled

This isn't only theory. Heart-rate loggers on pelicans flying in formation showed 11–14% lower heart rates than solo flight, and a study that GPS-tracked a trained flock of northern bald ibis found the birds really do hold station in the predicted upwash zone — and even phase their wingbeats to surf the rising part of the wake. Lead birds rotate because the point position gets no discount, which is why long skeins reshuffle in flight.

Step 3 · The eagleA patrol you'll learn to watch

The eagle needs the opposite of a state machine per second — it needs a routine. Real soaring eagles structure their day as climb-glide cycles: circle inside a thermal to bank altitude, then spend it on a straight glide to the next one. Our patrol is exactly that loop: in circle mode the bird carves a fixed-radius turn while climbing at 0.7 m/s, the whole gyre drifting downwind (thermals are columns in moving air — the same wind field the player rides pushes the eagle's elevator sideways); topping out near 110 m it picks a new point in its territory and glides, trading height for speed, until it arrives low and starts the next climb. One detail is worth stealing for any circling bird: the bank angle isn't animated, it's the coordinated-turn equation tan φ = v² / (g·r) — physics posing the mesh.

Why an eagle at all? Kleptoparasitism — and the readout below the demo is the game design hiding in plain sight. The patrol exposes its position every frame, and in the Fish Hawk season the carry-home leg after a catch will check it: an eagle high in a thermal while you're low with a fish is a problem you can see coming if you've learned to glance up. Same design law as the glints and the rings: no UI, just a world whose state is legible to a player paying attention.

Aside · The thief in the field guide

Bald eagles really do specialize in robbing ospreys — Benjamin Franklin famously grumbled that the eagle "does not get his living honestly" because it watches "the labour of the fishing hawk" and takes the fish. The maneuver is usually a high-speed stoop at the laden, slower osprey, which drops the catch rather than risk the talons. Ospreys respond exactly the way our future game loop will reward: climb immediately after a catch, and route home along the shore instead of across open water.

The airspace is now three deep: swallows on the film, geese in the transit layer, the eagle stacking thermals above everyone. Add the fish below from part one and the hatch between them and the vertical food chain is nearly complete — but the shore is still bare rock and beach gravel. A lake without reeds has no edge softness, no place for the wind to be visible on land, and frankly nowhere for all these insects to have come from. Next we plant the waterline: instanced grass and cattails by the tens of thousands, swaying to the one wind field, placed by the same altitude-and-slope rules that planted the forest. That's The Shore.
