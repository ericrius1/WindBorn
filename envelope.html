The Envelope

Every flight model has edges; the question is what they feel like. This post shapes the stall into a mush instead of a cliff, derives the dive that powers the game's signature move, and draws the whole flyable region as one chart.

Scroll down to read ↓

Give a player a stick and within thirty seconds they'll pull it all the way back, just to see. What happens in that moment defines the personality of the whole game. In most flight sims, the answer is a punishment: a wing drops, the world spins, you're learning about spin recovery when all you wanted was to look at a mountain. Fish Hawk's answer has to be different — there are no fail states on this lake, and the centerpiece of the game is a deliberate plunge straight down at the water. The edges of the four-force model aren't a place we fence off. They're where the dive lives.

Pilots call the set of speeds and angles where a wing flies the flight envelope. Today we map ours: the stall at the slow edge, the dive at the fast edge, and the flare — the maneuver that carries you from one to the other a wingspan above the lake.

Step 1 · The stallWhat the wing does when the air gives up

Back in Four Forces we drew C_L = 5.0·α and admitted it was only the beginning of the story. The linear slope describes attached flow: air bending smoothly around the wing's upper surface. Ask for too much angle — past about 16° for a wing like ours — and the flow can't make the turn anymore. It separates, the upper surface stops contributing, and the wing becomes something closer to a flat plate dragged at an angle, where lift follows ≈ 1.1·sin 2α: still some lift, just much less, with a lot more drag.

The crucial modeling decision is how to get from one regime to the other. Reality isn't a step function — separation creeps forward from the trailing edge over a few degrees — so we blend with a smoothstep across that range: 

s = smoothstep(16°, 23°, |α|)        // 0 attached → 1 separated
C_L = (1−s) · 5.0·α  +  s · 1.1·sin 2α

That blend factor s turns out to be the most reusable number in the post. It scales a drag penalty (separated flow is dirty), it fades the controls (separated wings answer slowly — authority drops by two-thirds at full stall), and later it will drive buffet shake and audio. One scalar, derived from geometry, propagating personality through the whole bird.

Step 2 · The mushFalling, politely

Now watch what the blended curve does to behavior. Pull past the stall and lift doesn't vanish — it sags. The bird starts sinking, which does something subtle and wonderful: sinking adds a vertical component to the oncoming air, which reduces the effective angle of attack, which moves the wing back toward attachment. The stall partially heals itself. The result is a mush: a nose-high, wallowing descent at 2–4 m/s with soft controls and a visible loss of crispness. Annoying, recoverable, instructive. Push the nose down for a second, the airspeed returns, the wing reattaches, and you're flying again.

We get all of that for free from the curve shape — no scripted "stall state", no animation takeover. The one thing we add is communication: a buffet shake that grows with s, because a real wing drums turbulence into the body before it lets go, and a player should never meet a stall they weren't warned about.

Aside · Why birds barely spin

Aircraft spin when one wing stalls before the other and the asymmetry winds the airframe up. Birds almost never do — they feel separation through their feathers and adjust each wing's angle dozens of times a second, and their mass is concentrated in the torso rather than spread along the wings. A point mass can't spin either, so our model is accidentally bird-accurate at its edge. The deep-stall wobble you see is the buffet layer talking, not the physics losing control.

Step 3 · The diveBuying speed with sky

The other edge of the envelope is where Fish Hawk's money shot lives. An osprey spots a fish from 20–40 m up, folds, and drops — hitting the water at 15–30 m/s. Where does that speed come from? Pure bookkeeping: trade potential energy for kinetic, v = √(2gh), and a 40 m drop is worth 28 m/s if nothing is subtracted. Drag is what subtracts, so diving well means shrinking it.

The tuck command does exactly that. Holding it folds the wings (the pose system already knows the shape), and in the model it cuts wing area by 70% and the drag coefficient roughly in half. Lift collapses too — which is the point: a tucked bird stops flying and starts falling with style. Run the numbers on terminal velocity with the tucked figures and you get well over 100 m/s — meaning our bird, like the real one, never gets anywhere near it. Every dive in this game is height-limited, not drag-limited: the lake's 40-meter sky simply doesn't hold more than ~28 m/s of speed. The physics quietly enforces the game's scale.

Getting out is the flare: release the tuck, wings snap to full area, and suddenly that ½ρV²S term is enormous — at 25 m/s the wing can pull 3–4 g without exotic angles of attack. Watch the n readout (load factor, lift over weight) spike in the pullout below. That number is free game-feel currency: later it will drive wind audio, camera shake, and the splash you make if you misjudge it.

Step 4 · The pictureOne chart to hold it all

Put the two edges on one set of axes — airspeed across, angle of attack up — and the envelope appears. The red line at 16° is the stall ceiling. The blue curve is the trim curve: for each speed, the α where lift exactly equals weight (C_L = 2W/ρV²S). Fly slower and the curve climbs toward the stall line; they meet at the minimum level-flight speed, about 7.6 m/s — below that, level flight is arithmetic­ally impossible. Fast and low-α, down in the right corner, is dive country.

The demo flies a scripted circuit — cruise, pull to the stall, mush, recover, tuck, dive, flare — while tracing its position in envelope space. Watching the dot orbit the chart is the best intuition pump I know for what a flight model actually is: not a set of animations, but a territory.

The envelope is mapped, but so far the air it's drawn in is dead calm.
