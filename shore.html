The Shore

The waterline gets soft: a meadow that leans with every gust and a reed bed standing in the shallows, two draw calls swaying to the same wind that steers the ripples behind them.

Scroll down to read ↓

Everything alive in this series so far swims or flies. But the thing that most makes a landscape feel inhabited is humbler: ground cover that moves. The osprey game needs the shore for three jobs. First, scale and parallax — when you sweep low over the bank on a hunting turn, grass rushing underneath is half the sensation of speed. Second, weather made visible on land: the wind field already steers the ripples, and when a gust crosses from water onto a meadow that bends in the same direction at the same moment, the world agrees with itself in a way you feel rather than notice. Third, ecology: the mayflies need a margin to swarm over, and reed beds are where a glacial lake's food chain actually lives. So this post plants the waterline — and like the forest before it, the budget rule is absolute: populations, not draw calls.

Step 1 · One bladeBend is a material, not an animation

A grass blade is eight triangles: a four-segment tapered strip, unit-height so instance scale is blade height, vertex-colored dark at the root and pale at the tip, with a little built-in droop so still air doesn't look like a comb. A cattail reed is about twenty: a three-sided stalk, the brown seed head, one arching leaf. That's the entire geometry budget, because — same trick as the conifers — the population differs only by instance transform, and all the life happens in the vertex stage.

The sway shader starts from real beam physics, simplified until it's three multiplies. A blade is a cantilever fixed at the root with the wind loading it along its length; its static deflection grows steeply toward the free end, so we bend each vertex by lean ∝ height² — the tip travels far, the root barely moves, and the blade appears to curve even though we never rotate anything. The lean direction and strength come from the spine wind, sampled once per frame on the CPU and handed to the GPU as two uniforms: one sample, ten thousand blades. Stiffness is a parameter: grass uses the full quadratic and a high compliance; reeds use a 1.6 exponent and a third the compliance, so they nod where grass bows. Then two details that sell it. A traveling gust wave — the bend amplitude is modulated by a sine moving through world space along the wind direction at the wind's own speed, wavelength matched to the wind field's 60 m gust scale — so gusts visibly sweep across the meadow instead of pumping every blade in unison. And per-blade flutter: a fast, small wobble phase-hashed from the instance index, free of any per-instance data. Push the wind slider and watch the whole population answer.

Step 2 · Where plants standAltitude, slope, and the waterline

Placement reuses the deterministic scatter recipe — a candidate lattice, integer-hash jitter, rejection filters, no Math.random(), so the shoreline you learn is the shoreline you keep. What's new is the filters, because grass and reeds read the terrain differently: 

grass   0.25 m < h < 26 − 18·hash      // the meadow band above the beach, ragged top edge
        normal.y ≥ 0.8                 // meadows are gentle ground
        fbm clumps                     // patches and partings, not fuzz
reeds   −0.7 m < h < +0.25 m           // rooted UNDERWATER, standing into the air
        hard clumping                  // rhizome colonies, not individuals

The reed filter is the one with teeth: it accepts candidates whose terrain height sits in a 95 cm band straddling the waterline, which is only ever true in a thin ribbon along the shore — the band where the bathymetry crosses zero. The mesh is planted on the bed and pokes through the water surface, which costs nothing and looks exactly right: stalks standing in the shallows, dry meadow behind, open water in front. Reeds also clump far harder than grass (a tighter threshold on the same noise field), because cattails spread by rhizome — a colony is one plant with ten thousand stems. Toggle the rules below and watch each one earn its keep: altitude off drowns the meadow, slope off carpets the bluffs, clumping off is static.

Aside · The reed bed is the nursery

Putting the reeds in the shallows isn't set dressing — it closes this season's loop. Emergent vegetation is where a real lake's littoral food web concentrates: nymphs graze its biofilm, fish fry shelter among the stems, and swallows nest within commuting range of exactly these margins. Our mayfly emergence sites were sampled from 0.5–6 m depths along this same shore, so the swarm, the rises, and the reed bed all crowd into the same hundred meters — which is why the dusk scene in the next post has a stage at all.

Step 3 · One weatherThe wind you can see twice

Here's the payoff shot for the spine architecture, and it costs zero new code: water and meadow in one frame, one wind. The lake's ripple amplitude scales with wind speed (the surface reads wind.speed every frame, as it has since the second post on this site), and the grass compliance multiplies the same number. Drag the slider from half a meter per second to ten: at the bottom the lake goes to glass and the meadow to a held breath; at the top, whitecap-grade chop and a meadow streaming flat, no coordination code anywhere — just two renderers quoting the same field. Swing the direction dial and both rotate together. In the finished game this is ambient truth the player absorbs without being told: look at any shoreline and you know, before you commit to a dive line, what the air over it is doing.

The hero scene up top is the assembled margin — tens of thousands of blades and a reed colony in two instanced draw calls, golden-hour light, ripples and rings on the bus-driven water behind. Every population in the season is now built: fish below, insects at the film, birds above, plants at the edge. One post remains, and it writes itself into the schedule the clock has been keeping all along — the moment all five systems are on screen at once, doing the same evening for their own reasons. That's The Dusk Event.
