Invisible Rivers

Altitude is the currency of Fish Hawk, and flapping is the expensive way to buy it. This post turns the world's one wind field into a landscape of free lift — gusts, ridge updrafts, thermal columns — and makes the invisible legible.

Scroll down to read ↓

Here's the movement economy of the finished game in one sentence: flapping costs stamina, altitude is stored stamina, and the landscape — read correctly — refills it for free. That loop only works if the air is a place, with geography you can learn. So this post is where the flight model meets the most important architectural decision on this whole site: there is exactly one wind field. The same vector function that steers the ripples on the lake is the one our bird's airspeed vector subtracts. When a gust darkens the water below you, your wings feel that same gust, because both systems asked the same question of the same function: 

wind.sample(x, y, z, t) → (wx, wy, wz)

Nothing in this post invents private weather. Ridge lift and thermals are influences — small functions registered onto the shared field that add their contribution wherever they apply. Flight reads the sum. So does the water. So will the grass, the clouds, and the whoosh in your headphones, in seasons to come. One field, one weather, no contradictions for the player to catch.

Step 1 · GustsThe base field breathes

The base field is a steady vector — direction and mean speed — modulated by smooth value noise in space and time. The speed multiplier wanders with a gustiness amplitude, and the direction swims a few degrees with a second, decorrelated noise channel. Sampled along a path, it produces the thing wind actually does: minute-scale lulls and surges that are spatially coherent, so two nearby points gust together but the far shore lags.

For the flight model, gusts arrive entirely through airspeed. The model never checks "is it gusty" — a headwind surge simply raises V, and lift rises with V², so the bird swells upward; a lull drops it. That's the honest mechanism behind the bumpiness of real flight, and it costs us zero extra code. In the demo, the streaks trace the live field over the lake. Drag the sliders and watch the water: ripple direction and amplitude follow, because every figure on this page shares the field — change the weather here and the bird in the hero above feels it.

Step 2 · Ridge liftAir can't enter a hill

Now the landscape starts generating lift, and the derivation is one boundary condition. Wind blowing at a slope can't flow through terrain, so near the surface its vertical velocity must match the ground rising beneath it. If the terrain height is h(x, z) and the horizontal wind is u, the air at the surface must climb at: 

w = u · ∇h        // wind speed × slope, along the wind

A 6 m/s breeze meeting a 30° slope is forced upward at about 3.5 m/s — five times our bird's minimum sink. The influence computes the terrain gradient by finite differences, dots it with the steady wind, and fades the result exponentially with height above the ground (we use a 25 m e-folding band). The same dot product goes negative on the lee side: descending air, the infamous sink that real pilots respect. We clamp lee sink gently — no fail states extends to the weather.

The result is the oldest trick in soaring: fly the windward face, parallel to the crest, and the hill flaps for you. The autopilot below is doing exactly that with its effort pinned to zero. Then rotate the wind direction slider and watch the river dry up — same hill, same bird, wrong wind. In the full game, reading which face is windward today is a skill the weather system will reward.

Aside · The kinematic boundary condition

w = u·∇h is the linearized form of a statement fluid dynamicists call the kinematic boundary condition — the full version says the velocity component normal to a surface vanishes at the surface. Real ridge flow adds compression speed-up above the crest and a turbulent rotor behind it; our exponential falloff is the honest minimum that keeps the part a soaring bird exploits. Swallows surfing the updraft along a lake bluff are riding the same equation at a meter's scale.

Step 3 · ThermalsColumns of paid-for sunshine

The second kind of free altitude comes from the sun. Ground that absorbs well — dry scree, dark rock — warms the air touching it until that air is buoyant enough to let go and rise as a column. We model a thermal as a Gaussian core: updraft strongest at the center, falling off with the square of radius, with a vertical profile that ramps up over the first 25 m (the air is still gathering itself) and caps out where the rising parcel cools to ambient temperature: 

w(x, y, z) = W₀ · e^(−r²/R²) · ramp(y) · cap(y)

A modest lake-country thermal — W₀ = 3 m/s, core radius 20 m, top at 120 m — out-lifts our bird's 0.7 m/s sink by a wide margin, but only if you stay in it. And staying in it is a geometry problem we already solved: the banked turn gives r = V²/(g·tan φ), so at 9 m/s you need about 30° of bank to hold a 15 m circle inside the core. Tighter bank costs more induced drag exactly as the lift gets stronger toward the center — thermalling is a live optimization problem, which is precisely why it's fun. Watch the trail below corkscrew upward; squeeze the radius slider and watch the bird work harder for less.

Step 4 · The varioMaking lift legible

Soaring has a perception problem: climbs of a meter per second are nearly invisible from inside the cockpit. Glider pilots solved it with the variometer — an instrument that reads vertical speed and reports it relentlessly. Fish Hawk has no instrument panel, but it still needs a vario; ours will eventually be feel and sound (rising audio pitch in lift is the plan for the audio season). Today we build the signal itself and learn its one design subtlety: smoothing.

Raw vertical speed is jittery — every gust and wingbeat spikes it. Smooth it with the same exponential damping the camera uses, and the time constant becomes a personality dial: too fast and the needle is a lie detector having a breakdown; too slow and you've drifted out of the thermal before the needle admits you found it. Around 3–4 s⁻¹ the instrument feels like a calm friend with good reflexes. Try both failure modes below — instrument tuning is game feel in miniature.

The air is alive now: it gusts, it climbs hills, it boils off warm stone, and a bird that reads it can stay up all day without a wingbeat. Mechanically, the flight model is complete. What remains is the difference between a model that works and a game that sings — response curves, a camera that breathes, the FOV kick, and the telemetry contract that lets every future system listen to the bird. That tuning pass is The Joy Pass, and it's where Lift sticks the landing.
