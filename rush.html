The Sound of Speed

There is no wind sound file in Fish Hawk. There is a flight model, and there is a synthesizer listening to it. Click the hero, fly a dive, and what you hear is your airspeed — nothing else.

Scroll down to read ↓

Three seasons in, this project can show you speed: the FOV kick, the streaking water, the trembling airframe at the edge of the envelope. But play any flying game with the sound off and you'll notice what's missing — speed you can't hear doesn't feel fast. The dive is Fish Hawk's centerpiece, and a dive is mostly an audio event: hiss building to a howl, a held breath, then water. So this season builds the game's ears, and it starts with the sound the bird can never escape: the wind of its own motion.

Here's the thing that makes this post fun: that sound doesn't exist in the world. When you stand in a gale, the roar you hear isn't sound that traveled to you from somewhere — it's turbulence boiling around your own head, pressure chaos created at the ear itself. Microphone people call it pseudo-sound. For us it's a gift, because it means wind audio isn't an ambience to play back. It's a signal to synthesize from state — and we already publish that state. The Joy Pass ended by exporting flightState precisely so a system like this one could read airspeed sixty times a second and turn it into pressure at your eardrums.

Step 1 · The atomOne noise source, one filter

Turbulent pressure fluctuation is the textbook example of a broadband random signal, so the raw material is white noise — equal energy at every frequency, the sound of pure chance. White noise alone sounds like a detuned radio. Every wind sound in this series is that same noise pushed through filters: a bandpass keeps energy near a center frequency and discards the rest, and its resonance Q sets how narrow the kept band is. Low center and low Q reads as distant rumble; a high center with high Q rings at its center like a whistle being approached.

That's the entire vocabulary. Before any mapping, get an ear for the atom itself — the demo below is one noise source and one bandpass, with the filter's ideal response drawn over the live spectrum. Find 300 Hz at low Q (a sheltering eave), then sweep up past 2 kHz and raise Q until it whistles.

Aside · Pseudo-sound

Real sound is a pressure wave that propagates; pseudo-sound is local pressure turbulence that never leaves the spot where it's made. It's why wind "noise" ruins outdoor recordings — the microphone isn't hearing the wind, it's being the wind — and why fur windscreens work: they move the turbulence away from the capsule. An osprey's head is a small, smooth capsule with feathers for a windscreen. What it hears in a dive is exactly what we're synthesizing: its own private storm.

Step 2 · The mappingAirspeed in, spectrum out

Now wire the atom to the flight model. The patch is three bands fed by one noise source, and every parameter is a function of airspeed V: 

body    lowpass    fc = 100 + 14·V        the whoosh itself
hiss    bandpass   fc = 280 + 36·V        air tearing past edges
scream  bandpass×2 fc ≈ 550 + 75·V, Q≈14  only opens above ~16 m/s

The cutoffs slide up with speed because faster flow shifts turbulent energy toward smaller, faster eddies — the spectrum genuinely brightens. Loudness rides dynamic pressure, the same q = ½ρV² that lift and drag are built on; in amplitude terms we use (V/32)1.6, normalized so a committed dive at 32 m/s hits full voice while a 10 m/s cruise stays conversational. The scream is the special case: two high-Q resonators a minor sixth apart whose gain is gated by a smoothstep that doesn't open until 16 m/s. Below that threshold a glide is all body and hiss; past it, the resonators rise together and the patch stops whispering and starts howling. One slider drives all of it below — or press the sweep button and listen to a glide become a stoop, hands-free.

Gusts ride on top for free. The synth samples the same wind field the bird flies through, compares the local wind magnitude to the mean, and lets that ratio breathe both the gain and the cutoffs a few percent. When a gust shoves the bird in the physics, the ear hears the shove too — one wind field, one weather, now audible.

Step 3 · The wingbeatWhump and buffet

Steady-state wind isn't the whole voice. Two event layers key off the flight model's telemetry. The first is the flap whump: each downstroke shoves a wing-sized parcel of air, and a shoved parcel of air is a low thud. We watch flapPhase — the wingbeat clock — and every time it passes mid-downstroke we fire a 150 ms burst of lowpassed noise plus a sine thump that falls from 95 to 45 Hz. The trigger is the physics' clock, not a timer, so when effort rises and the wingbeat quickens from 2.5 toward 4.5 Hz, the whumps quicken with it, exactly in step with the animation.

The second is stall buffet. The Envelope computes a stall blend — how separated the flow over the wing is — and separated flow is unsteady flow: it sheds vortices that beat against the airframe at around ten hertz. So the synth runs a ~10 Hz tremolo on the whole wind bed whose depth is the stall blend. At zero it's inaudible. As you haul the nose up and the wing begins to mush, the wind starts to flutter before anything visual happens — the same early warning the camera shake gives, now in your ears, and from the same number.

Aside · One context, one limiter

Every sound this season makes — wind, splashes, calls, music — flows through a single shared AudioContext and one master limiter: a compressor with a hard knee at −6 dBFS and a 20:1 ratio. Browsers also refuse to start audio without a click, and our demos build themselves while offscreen, so every demo starts silent behind a gate that a click opens and a watchdog closes when the demo scrolls away. Plumbing, not glamour — but it's why five noisy posts can coexist on one site without clipping or autoplay sins.

Listen to the hero again with all three layers in mind: body, hiss, and scream sliding with V; whumps locked to the wingbeat; buffet flickering when you pull too hard in the pullout. None of it is recorded, none of it is scripted — it's the flight model, rendered as pressure. That's the pattern for this whole season: find the state the world already publishes, derive a small patch from first principles, and let the simulation play it. Next we point the microphone down. The disturbance bus has been carrying splash events since the bird first learned to land — it's time the water answered out loud.
