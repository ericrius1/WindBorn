First Flight

Season one ends here: the lake from The Mirror, the bird from New Feathers, and the flight model from Lift, together in one scene for the first time. Click the water, take the stick, and fly. Land slow, drift a while, and run back into the air.

Scroll down to read ↓

Fifteen posts ago this site was an empty repository and a promise: a lake, a bird, and eventually a game about being an osprey. Since then, three series have each built their piece in deliberate isolation. The Mirror finished a glass-dawn lake — Fresnel skin, wind-steered ripples, a planar mirror world, rings that answer every touch. New Feathers built the bird and taught her to flap, pose, and land on water — on rails I scripted. Lift derived a flight model honest enough to stall and tuned until it was joyful — flown as a stand-in dart glyph. Today the rails come off and the costume goes on. The hero above is the milestone: the real bird, flown by the real flight model, over the real lake. Keyboard or gamepad, exactly as wired in The Stick. Fly low until your own ripples sharpen under you, bleed speed, settle onto the water, drift with the wind — then hold the flap and she runs herself back into the air. This post is the story of bolting three independently built systems together: what snapped cleanly, where the seams were, and why the whole thing needed less new code than any single essay before it.

Step 1 · The contractsIntegration was decided before any series began

The classic way this goes wrong is famous enough to have a smell. Three teams build three systems; each, needing weather or time or water, invents a private one; integration day becomes rewrite day, because the systems don't disagree about details — they disagree about reality. Stillwater's defense was set up before the first essay: four tiny shared modules called the spine, and a rule that nobody invents privately what the spine already provides. One wind field, wind.sample(x, y, z, t). One clock, clock.hour. One water query, waterHeightAt(x, z, t). One event channel, disturb(...).

Here is what that buys, concretely, in the scene above. The ripples you see are steered by the same wind vector the flight model subtracts from velocity to get airspeed — so when a gust crosses the lake you can watch it arrive on the surface and feel it drop a wing, and no code connects those two events except the shared field. The water the bird floats on is the same five-wave sum twice over: the GPU displaces the rendered surface with it, the CPU answers waterHeightAt with it, so the mesh you see and the function the physics queries can never drift apart — Glass Dawn built that parity on purpose. And the bus: the flight model has been emitting splash and skim events since Four Forces — there was just nobody listening. The lake's ring layer has been subscribed since Glass Dawn — there was just nobody flying. Put both on one page and the wiring already exists. Step 2 · One bodyDressing the point mass

The Lift series flies a dot. Its whole state is a position, a velocity, a bank angle, and an angle of attack — four forces on a point mass, by design, because game feel lives in how commands shape a few numbers. The New Feathers bird, meanwhile, solves her entire pose fresh every frame from a small pile of scalars: a gait setting, a wingbeat clock phase, a handful of pose weights chased by springs. Nothing plays a clip. That choice was made for animation reasons long before flight existed — and it turns out to be exactly the shape integration wants, because the flight model's diagnostics are scalars too. The bridge between a physics dot and a feathered body is about a dozen assignments per frame: 

orientation ← airflow direction, lift direction, α   (the glyph's trick)
gait        ← flap effort, airspeed                   (glide ↔ cruise ↔ hover)
tuck pose   ← the model's tuck command
brake pose  ← height above water × sink rate          (the flare, as physics)
bank        ← body.bank                               (lift rolls between wings)

Only one seam fought back: there were two wingbeat clocks. The flight model has owned a flapPhase since The Joy Pass — the camera's flap-bob keys off it, and the audio series will key its wing whumps off it too. The animator had its own clock from The Flap Cycle. Run both and they drift apart within seconds: wings beating visibly out of time with the camera bob they're supposed to cause. The fix is demotion. The animator's phase is now assigned from the model's clock every frame, never advanced on its own — one clock, three subscribers. Aside · The bird is still a dot

Nothing about the physics changed in this post. Lift, drag, weight, and thrust still act on a single point; the mesh is a costume the point wears. This is the oldest trick in game animation, and it is honest where it counts — the forces really do come from α and airspeed — and cheap where it doesn't: no per-feather aerodynamics, no articulated inertia. If the dive someday needs the wings to carry real force (a mid-tuck gust catching one wing), the contract point is that there is still exactly one body to apply it to.

Step 3 · The handoffWho owns the bird

The flight model treats water as a floor, not a fail state: clamp the altitude, scrub speed with belly drag, announce a splash. That is perfect for a botched low pass and useless for floating — a floor holds the keel dry, and there is no buoyancy anywhere in the aerodynamics, nor should there be. Touch and Go already solved floating with Archimedes linearized into a damped spring toward waterHeightAt − immersion — but that bird flew a script. Now a player can arrive at the surface in any state, at any speed, from any angle. The real integration question of this milestone turned out to be: who integrates the body this frame?

The answer is a three-state ownership machine, and it is small enough to recite. Air: the flight model owns the bird. Float: the buoyancy spring owns it, plus wind drift and water drag. Run: a takeoff script owns it — near-vertical hover strokes, speed building stride by stride, lift following speed. The transitions are the design. Skimming below a ground-speed threshold hands the body to the water. Holding flap hands it to the run. Climbing clear, fast enough, hands it back to the aerodynamics mid-wingbeat. Through every change of government the same FlightBody stays the storage for position and velocity, so the chase camera and the published FlightState telemetry never notice a seam. And every owner speaks the same language on the way through: a splash at touchdown, slow wake pulses while drifting, a skim per stride on the run — all of it rings today, all of it fluid momentum and headphone audio in seasons to come. Aside · The shake

Real ospreys add a fourth state we don't have yet. A few wingbeats after hauling out of the water, the bird rouses mid-air — a full-body shake, exactly like a wet dog, that flings a cloud of spray and costs a visible moment of ragged flight. Plumage sheds most of the water, but a soaked osprey can be carrying a meaningful fraction of her own body weight in it, and the shake is worth the wobble. It's high on the list for the splash season: the cloud of droplets is a particle problem, and the wobble is one line in the flight model.

That's the milestone: fly, land, float, take off — the verb soar, playable end to end, built from three libraries that had never met before this page and one page of glue that mostly signs contracts already written. It's also, honestly, a stage set. The mountains are silhouette props; the lake has no bed under it; the splash is a polite analytic ring. Season 2 starts digging. The Basin series carves a real glacial valley — terrain whose windward slopes will feed the ridge-lift influences the wind field already accepts. And The Splash replaces the rings with a real fluid simulation living in a window around the bird. Here's the part I'm most confident about, and it's a direct payoff of Step 1: because every floating thing asks waterHeightAt instead of a particular implementation, the float you just played survives that swap without changing a line. The provider changes behind the function; the bird won't know the difference. She'll just finally make a real hole in the water — which is good, because next season she learns the verb the whole game hangs on. Season 1 was fly. Season 2 is dive.
