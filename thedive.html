The Dive

The plunge already looks right — the tuck, the crown of spray, the submarine instant, the breach. This post answers the question the spectacle dodges: did it work? The dive is the whole reason Fish Hawk exists. Everything else — the water, the bird, the flight model, the fluid sim, the breach — was built so that one moment could feel like dropping out of the sky into a real lake. By the first catch it already looked complete. But looking right and being a game are different things. A flight sim where every plunge succeeds is a screensaver; a game needs a dive that can be done well or badly, and a way to tell the difference that isn't a number floating over the fish. So this post adds the scoring: the small piece of math that turns a beautiful animation into a skill.

The design rule from the loop still holds — no fail states. A missed dive is not a punishment; it is a half-minute of daylight spent and a fish that swims off. The scoring exists to make a good dive feel earned, not to make a bad one hurt. That points the whole design at one number: a catch probability, computed at the instant the talons hit the water, from things the player actually controls.

Step 1 · Four factors, one productWhat makes a strike land

Watch a real osprey and the same four things decide every dive. The angle: it commits on a steep line, around sixty to seventy degrees below horizontal — shallow and it skips off the surface, dead vertical and it can't adjust for the fish's motion. The speed: ospreys hit the water at fifteen to thirty meters per second; too slow and there isn't enough punch to reach a fish a meter down, too fast and the strike blows past and stuns itself. The aim: the horizontal miss between the talons and the fish, measured against the talon reach — and because the fall takes real time, you have to aim where the fish will be, not where it is. And the depth: a fish at the surface is a gift, a fish three meters down needs a fast, true line.

The crucial design choice is how to combine them. Not a sum — a product. p = aim1.3 · speed0.9 · angle0.7 · depth0.6. A sum would let a perfect line at walking speed average out to a decent score; a product says a dive fails if any factor is bad, which is exactly how diving feels. Perfect aim at no speed still misses. The exponents just say which factor matters most: aim first, then speed, then angle, then depth. Aside · Leading a moving fish

The lead is the part that feels like real hunting. A fish cruising at a meter a second is half a meter away by the time a dive from eight meters up arrives — so the dive has to be aimed ahead of it. We compute the time-to-impact as a simple ballistic fall (height equals initial down-speed times time plus half g times time squared, solved for time), then project the fish forward at its current velocity. Aim there. It is the same arithmetic a quarterback uses, and the same a fish hawk's brain does without arithmetic at all.

Step 2 · The catch has weightCarrying it home

A clean dive earns a fish, and the fish is not just a point scored — it is a load. An osprey can lift maybe nine hundred grams, and you feel every gram of it on the way up. So a successful catch rolls a fish with a mass (cleaner, steeper dives tend to land bigger ones), and that mass feeds a small carryEffect: a heavier fish costs the bird up to a fifth of its usable flap power and adds a steady downward tug, half a meter per second squared to nearly one. The bird turns the catch head-first to cut drag, the way a real one does, but the climb home still sags. This is what makes the dive a decision and not a reflex: a fat trout is a real meal that knocks down your hunger, and it is also a real anchor between you and the nest.

Nothing here is a special case in the flight model: the carry is just extra sink and scaled effort fed into the same point-mass integrator the forces post built. The bird doesn't know it's carrying dinner; it only knows it's heavier.

Put the two together in the hero and the dive becomes the loop's tense center. You read the water for a fish worth taking, line up a steep fast pass, lead the cruise, and hit the real sim window talons-first — and a beat later you know, from the splash and a single word, whether you're climbing out empty or laboring home with a fish. The probability is invisible; the spray and the weight tell you everything. Next, the place all that laboring is for: the nest, and the home that grows from a handful of catches carried home across a handful of days.
