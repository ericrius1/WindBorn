Seeing the Bottom

The lake has been opaque since part one — a mirror with nothing inside. Today we open it: light that bends at the surface, dies channel by channel on the way down, and comes back up carrying the lakebed with it.

Scroll down to read ↓

Everything so far has happened at the surface: the Fresnel skin, the wind ripples, the mirror world. But Fresnel has been writing a check we haven't cashed. If only 2% of the light reflects when you look straight down, then 98% goes in — hits something, comes back out — and our shader has been waving that away with a flat dark constant. For most of a deep lake, fine. But Fish Hawk's entire hunt happens in the shallows: the osprey reads cruising shadows against sunlit sand, players judge the dive by how the bottom falls away, and the shoreline only looks like a shoreline if you can see it continue underwater. So today the water becomes a participating medium: we follow the transmitted ray down, charge it the toll, and bring back what it saw.

Step 1 · The tollBeer–Lambert absorption

Start with what water does to light that travels through it. Consider a thin slab of thickness Δd: it absorbs a fixed fraction of whatever enters it — twice the photons in, twice absorbed, same percentage. Stack slabs and the survival fractions multiply, and "multiply a fixed factor per unit distance" has exactly one continuous solution, the exponential: 

T(d) = e^(−a·d)

— transmittance T after path length d, with a the absorption coefficient in units of 1/m. The part that makes water water: a is wildly different per color channel. We use (0.42, 0.09, 0.035) for red, green, blue — red dies roughly ten times faster than blue. Aside · Where the numbers come from

Pure water genuinely absorbs ~650 nm red at about 0.35/m while barely touching blue — that asymmetry is real physics, not art direction. A real lake adds dissolved organics and suspended particles, which absorb and scatter more (and push the hue toward green), so our coefficients run a little hotter than pure water and the leftover glow is folded into a single "scatter color" term rather than simulated. One honest simplification on top: we charge the toll for the eye's path up but not the sun's path down — a constant factor the clarity slider absorbs anyway.

Step 2 · The bendRefraction finds the bed

Absorption needs a path length, which means we need to know where the transmitted ray actually goes. Light crossing into a slower medium bends toward the normal — Snell's law, sin θ₂ = sin θ₁ / 1.333 — and the shader gets this in one intrinsic: refract(view, normal, 1/n). Crucially, the normal in that expression is the rippled normal from part two, so every passing wave re-aims the underwater ray. That's the entire mechanism behind the most water-like thing you'll see today: stones that swim. The bed isn't moving; your line of sight to it is.

Then we intersect the bent ray with the lakebed. The bed is analytic — a height function bed(x, z), sand dunes built from smooth noise — so we solve for the crossing iteratively: guess the path using the depth directly below the entry point, land, re-measure the height error where we landed, correct once. For gentle beds two steps sit within a pixel of the truth, and "gentle" is a contract our lakes keep. The crossing point gives us the albedo to fetch and the path length to charge. Aside · The osprey already knows

Refraction makes everything underwater look shallower than it is — apparent depth is roughly true depth divided by 1.33 — and it displaces the image of a fish away from the fish itself. Ospreys compensate: they steepen their final approach (a vertical strike has no refraction error to correct) and hit feet-first along the line their dive established. When we build dive scoring later in the project, this offset is a gameplay variable, not a rendering artifact — striking where the fish appears will be the rookie mistake.

Step 3 · The water columnAssembling the shallows

Now the body term — the flat constant we've dragged along since part one — gets its real definition. What comes up from below is the bed's color filtered by the round trip, plus the water's own scattered glow filling in everything the filter removed: 

body = bedColor · T(path)  +  scatter · (1 − T(path))

The two terms trade off automatically. At your feet, T ≈ 1: all bed, no glow — sand and stones in honest color. Out past a few meters of path, T → 0: the bed contribution starves and body converges to exactly the deep-water constant we'd been hardcoding. The old fake was the limit of the new truth all along, which is why nothing else in the material changes: the same mix(body, reflection, F) + glint skin now just has a body worth looking at. And the seams are clean by construction — the bed is an interface (a height function and a color function), so when the terrain series ships real bathymetry, the water reads it through the same two calls. OnwardWhat this unlocks

The lake now has an inside. Looking down: sand, stones, depth you can judge with your eyes — the osprey's hunting ground. Looking out: the same mirror as before, because Fresnel decides who wins at every angle and it always favored reflection toward the horizon. Every optical system this series promised is built. What's left is the payoff: putting them all in one scene, at the one hour Fish Hawk opens on — first light, dead calm, mountains doubled in black glass — and wiring the surface to the disturbance bus so the world can finally touch it.
