The Adaptive Score

Five layers, three world inputs, no playlist. Slide the day, climb the air, bring the weather in — the music follows the world because the world is the conductor.

Scroll down to read ↓

Everything The Ear has built so far is diegetic — sound with a cause you could point at: airspeed, a bus event, a wall. Music is the one layer with no source inside the world, which makes it the easiest thing to get wrong. Fish Hawk is a quiet game; a loop that restarts every two minutes would wear through it in an evening. What the game wants instead is music that behaves like weather: always there, never the same, changing because the world changed. So the score is not a track — it's an instrument the world plays, with three hands: altitude, the world clock, and the weather.

Step 1 · The contractFive notes that can't collide

Adaptive layering has a hidden requirement that shapes everything: if layers fade in and out independently, then every possible combination of them will eventually sound together, so every pair must be consonant by construction. The cheapest airtight guarantee is the major pentatonic scale — D E F# A B for us. Five notes, and the smallest interval between any two of them is a whole tone: no minor seconds, no tritones, none of the intervals that grind. It's why wind chimes are tuned pentatonically — any gust plays a valid chord — and our score is, frankly, a very organized wind chime.

Try to break it below. The labeled keys are the scale; play any two and watch the summed waveform — scale pairs beat slowly and settle, while off-scale neighbors (try F# against G) buzz with the roughness the scale exists to exclude.

Step 2 · The mathCrossfades that don't dip

Layers come and go by crossfade, and crossfades have a classic trap. Loudness lives in power — amplitude squared — but a fader moves amplitude. Crossfade two equal layers linearly and at the midpoint each contributes 0.5 amplitude: the amplitudes still sum to 1, but the power is 0.5² + 0.5² = 0.5. Half the power, −3 dB: an audible hole in the middle of every transition. The fix is to fade along a curve that conserves power instead: gA = cos(uπ/2), gB = sin(uπ/2), so that gA² + gB² = 1 for every blend u — the Pythagorean identity, moonlighting as a mixing law. The demo runs both laws with a live RMS meter underneath; flip to linear and the dip draws itself.

Our engine has five layers, not two, so we generalize the same idea: each layer computes a raw weight wᵢ from the world, and the gains are normalized in power across the set — 

gᵢ = √( wᵢ / max(1, Σw) )

Whenever the weights sum to at least 1, total power Σgᵢ² is exactly constant no matter how the mix is distributed; when everything ducks at once (a stormy midnight at zero altitude), the max(1, ·) lets the music genuinely get quieter instead of boosting whatever is left. The cos/sin pair falls out as the two-layer special case.

Step 3 · The handsKeying layers to the world

Now the musical decisions, one layer per world condition. A drone — root and fifth, low and slow — holds weight 1 always; it's the lake's pedal point and the floor the normalization stands on. Meadow, a warm mid-register pad gliding between four chords, owns daytime at low altitude — hunting height. Aloft takes over as altitude climbs through the 18–65 m band: sparse high bells on the pentatonic set, scheduled a beat ahead on a loose grid, thinner and brighter exactly when the ground falls away. Night crossfades in through the hour window the daylight smoothstep leaves behind, darker B-minor colors moving at half speed. And storm rides the weather input — a pulsing low fifth that pushes everything else down through its weight alone (each layer's window multiplies in 1−k·storm), no special ducking logic anywhere.

Every window is a smoothstep, never a threshold, for the same reason the flight model smooths its commands: the inputs are continuous, so the music must be too. Altitude in particular wobbles constantly — a bird working a thermal crosses 30 m a dozen times a minute, and a hard-edged switch there would turn the score into a metronome of its own indecision. The 18–65 m band is wide on purpose: it takes a deliberate climb to bring the bells in fully, which quietly teaches the player that altitude is a place, not a number.

The bells deserve one implementation note, because they're the only scheduled notes in the engine. Web audio runs on two clocks: the render loop's, which stutters whenever the tab breathes, and the audio context's, which is sample-accurate. Notes must be placed on the second using the first — the standard lookahead pattern. Each frame, while the aloft layer is audible, the engine schedules any bell whose start time falls within the next 400 ms, on a loose grid of 0.7 to 2 seconds with a coin flip per slot. The jitter is deliberate: a strict grid reads as a sequencer, and this layer is supposed to read as wind chimes — caused, not composed.

Aside · Chords that glide

The pads never retrigger. Each pad voice is a pair of detuned triangle oscillators that simply glide to the next chord's frequency along an exponential approach — portamento, the way a choir slides between chords without consonants. It costs nothing (the oscillators run anyway), it can never click, and it makes the harmony feel continuous rather than sequenced. The chord clock ticks every nine seconds; nobody is meant to notice.

Listen to the hero for a full lap of its 96-second day with the storm slider at zero, then again at one. Same five layers, same five notes — but dawn at altitude sounds like glass and bells, and a storm at dusk sounds like held breath. That's the adaptive bargain: we don't write music for moments, we write a space of music and let the world walk through it. Every system this season has now been built — wind, water, voice and echo, score — each mountable with one call and each driven by spine state. One post remains, and it's an exam: turn the screen off and fly the lake on sound alone.
