The Night Shift

The clock keeps running after sunset. A moon with a real path takes the sun's old job, stars wheel around a fixed pole, and the lake — black now — carries a single road of light. The finale of Air & Light is the quietest scene in the game.

Scroll down to read ↓

Fish Hawk has no fail state, but it does have an honest clock — and a day you spend fishing badly ends in the dark. We always wanted that to be a reward rather than a punishment: the moonlit lake is the photo-mode hour, the screen you leave running. Real ospreys are diurnal — by rights the bird should be on the snag, head under wing — so night exists for the player who stayed to watch. It is also the last hole in this series' contract. The atmosphere already dims honestly as the sun drops; what is missing is everything that shows up after: a second light source with its own path, a few thousand points of light with a shared axis, and water optics at one four-hundred-thousandth of the energy budget. Tonight we ship all three.

Step 1 · The moonA path, and phase as a dot product

The moon costs us almost nothing, because part one already built the machinery: a body's direction from latitude, declination, and hour angle. The moon is just a second caller with a lag — Stillwater fixes it 12 hours behind the sun with its own slight declination, which makes every night a full-moon night that rises near sunset and sets near sunrise. (A real moon slips ~50 minutes later each night and waxes as it goes; one season, one moon — declared, like the April sun.)

Here is the part that still delights me: we never draw a phase. The moon in these scenes is an actual sphere, hung along its direction and lit by the actual sun direction — the same vector that set below the horizon hours ago. The sun is below our horizon; the moon, high in the sky, still sees it. Lambert shading does the rest, and the illuminated fraction follows the textbook formula k = (1 − cos ψ)/2 with ψ the sun–moon angle. Step 2 · The starsA lattice in the sky

Stars want to be cheap. Instead of point sprites or a texture, they live inside the sky dome's fragment shader: take the view direction, scale it onto a 3D lattice, and let each lattice cell roll deterministic dice — one candidate star per cell, with a jittered position, a power-law brightness (most stars faint, a few brilliant, like the real magnitude distribution), a temperature tint from blue-white to orange, and a twinkle phase. The same cell always rolls the same dice, so the sky is the same sky every night of the game.

Motion comes free and correct. The dome turns once a day around the celestial pole — the axis from part one, standing exactly 46.6° above the northern horizon. So we rotate the sampling direction around that axis by the hour angle before hashing: the entire field wheels rigidly, circumpolar stars circling the pole, southern stars rising and setting, with zero geometry moved. Twinkle strengthens toward the horizon, where starlight crosses the most air — the same air-mass argument as everything else in this series. Aside · The exposure confession

Moonlight is about 400,000× dimmer than sunlight. Render that honestly through a fixed tone map and the screen is black. Real eyes solve it with dark adaptation, so we do the cinematic equivalent: night scenes raise the tone-mapping exposure (×5 here, stated in the readout), and the moon's light is boosted to a playable ~1/250 of the sun with a blue-shifted tint standing in for scotopic vision — the eye's rod cells, which dominate at night, cannot see red. Two declared lies in service of one truth: nights you cannot see are nights nobody witnesses.

Step 3 · The glitter pathMoonlight on black water

Now the payoff scene. At night the Fresnel arithmetic from the very first post goes stark: the water body term is essentially black (nothing below to scatter back), the sky reflection is faint star-field and moon-glow, and the only loud term left is the specular glint — pointed at the moon now instead of the sun. That long road of light running from the far shore to your feet is thousands of wave facets, each briefly tilted just right to mirror the moon at you. Its geometry is pure statistics: the path's angular length is set by the spread of surface slopes, roughly ±2σ around the mirror direction, and σ scales with the wind — because every wave amplitude in the table does. Glass-calm water gives a single sharp moon-image; a 4 m/s breeze smears it into a kilometer of shimmer. Aside · Why the path always points at you

Everyone on a shoreline sees their own glitter path, aimed directly at them — it is the locus of facets whose mirror bounce happens to connect your eye to the moon, and that set is different for every observer. It is the same reason the sun glint chased the camera in The Skin of the Lake: a mirror path is a relationship, not a place.

Step 4 · The whole nightAssembly

The hero at the top is the series finale running live: the atmosphere carrying moon-scattered blue and an airglow floor, the star lattice wheeling overhead, the full moon dragging its glitter road across water ripples that still answer the one wind field, with the Mirror's lake swapped onto the moon through the same environment seam it has always used — sun direction, sun color, sky function. The lake never learned that night exists. It just reflects what it is given, which was the design bet of this whole season.

OnwardWhat this unlocks

Air & Light is complete: a clock-keyed sun and palette, fog and mist with closed-form math, raymarched cumulus on the spine wind, a weather machine that rains on the bus, and now the night. Every later series reads this contract — the grading post keys its color by the hour, the adaptive score will know dusk from dawn, and the game's photo mode inherits a sky worth pausing for. Next up the valley comes alive: fish that school and rise, mayflies at golden hour, swallows working the surface — an ecosystem that reads the same clock this series just taught to glow.
