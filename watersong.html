What Water Says

Every ring on this lake is a disturbance-bus event, and every sound is the same event, heard. Click the water anywhere — closer is louder, left is left — and turn the rain up until it sings.

Scroll down to read ↓

Fish Hawk's whole hunt is acoustic at the end: spot, dive, splash. Last post gave the dive its rising scream; this one gives it the payoff — and not just for the player's own strikes. The lake is supposed to feel inhabited, and a lake you can hear inhabits itself: a fish rises behind you and you turn before you think. The infrastructure for all of this has existed since Touch and Go, when the bird first started publishing disturb() events on contact. The disturbance bus already carries every splash, rise, raindrop, and wingtip kiss as a tiny record: {kind, x, z, energy, radius}. The fluid sim will listen to that bus someday. The foam layer will listen to it. Today, a synthesizer listens to it.

That's the design worth pausing on: the audio doesn't get special events. The same fields that will size a wave tell us everything a sound needs — where gives distance and stereo side, energy gives loudness and, as we're about to derive, pitch.

Step 1 · AnatomySlap, bloop, and the bubble formula

Listen to anything hitting water and you'll hear three sounds in strict order. First the slap — the surface tearing open, a broadband noise burst a few tens of milliseconds long. Then the body — the famous bloop. When an object punches through, it drags a cavity of air underwater, and that cavity rings like a bell. Its pitch comes from a hundred-year-old result, the Minnaert resonance: an air bubble of radius R in water oscillates at 

f ≈ 3.26 / R    (R in meters, f in hertz)

— derived by balancing the springiness of the compressed air against the inertia of the water around it. The constant is √(3γP₀/ρ)/2π with γ = 1.4 for air and lake-surface pressure and density plugged in. It says everything we need: a talons-first strike entrains a ~5 cm cavity and rings near 65 Hz, deep and round; a raindrop entrains a 3 mm bubble and plinks above a kilohertz. And because the cavity shrinks as it collapses, the pitch glides up — that rising character is what makes a bloop a bloop. Third comes the tail: the crown's droplets fall back, each making its own millimeter bubble — a sparse shower of high plinks trailing off.

One number drives all three. The bus's energy scalar sets a length scale size = ∛E (energy to length, the cube root), and from it the cavity radius, the slap brightness, the loudness, and the droplet count. Take the splash apart below — each button plays one component — then slide the energy from raindrop to strike and listen to the Minnaert pitch fall as the cavity grows.

Aside · Minnaert's bell

Marcel Minnaert published the bubble-resonance formula in 1933 in a paper titled On musical air-bubbles and the sounds of running water — he wanted to know why brooks babble. The answer is that a brook is a bubble factory, and every bubble chimes once at the frequency its radius dictates. All water sound is, more or less, bubbles being born: rain, surf, wakes, your faucet. Synthesists love the formula because it's invertible — choose a pitch, and you know how big the splash that makes it must be.

Step 2 · The busEvents become voices

Wiring the synth to the world is now almost embarrassingly short. We subscribe once to the bus. Each event computes its distance and bearing from the listener — the bird's ears, read from the same flightState the wind synth tracks — and gets a tiny spatial chain: a gain for distance (1/(1+(d/18)²), a soft inverse-square), a lowpass for air absorption (distant splashes lose their highs first), and a stereo pan from the left-right component of the bearing. Then the event's kind picks a recipe: a rise is nearly all bloop — fish barely break the surface; a skim is all slap and droplets — a wingtip never entrains a cavity; a full splash gets the complete anatomy. The hero above is exactly this code: the rings you see and the sounds you hear are the same bus messages, consumed by two different subscribers.

There's one defensive layer worth confessing: a voice budget. A storm can put hundreds of events per second on the bus, and each full splash is a dozen scheduled nodes. The listener spends from a budget of about six splashes a second, refilled continuously — beyond that, events are dropped, starting with the ones too far away to matter. You cannot hear the difference; you can absolutely hear the audio thread surviving.

Step 3 · LappingThe shore, derived, not looped

Between events, a lake is never silent — it laps. The lazy move is a looped recording, but we have something better: the actual surface. The spine's waterHeightAt(x, z, t) is the same wave sum that the Mirror series renders and the same one the bird floats on, so we ask it, every frame, how fast the surface is moving at the listener: |∂h/∂t|. That rate drives an envelope follower — fast attack, slow release — which gates a band of pink noise. Each passing crest swells the wash; each trough lets it sigh back. Raise the wind below and the lapping grows and brightens because the waves do: wind speed scales the spine's wave amplitudes, which steepens ∂h/∂t, which opens the gate wider. Nothing in the audio knows about wind. It only knows the water, which knows the wind.

Step 4 · RainA texture made of events

Rain is the bus's stress test and the prettiest demo of the event-driven idea. One raindrop is just a very small splash — energy around a millijoule, a millimeter bubble, a single bright plink (press the button below). A storm is thousands of them, and at that density we switch to granular thinking: each rain event becomes a single cheap grain — fifteen milliseconds of highpassed noise, one in six carrying a plink — and texture emerges purely from statistics. Slide the rate up and listen to the transition: at five drops a second you hear individual plinks; at fifty you hear rhythm dissolving; at two hundred it fuses into the broadband hush every roof has taught you. The spectrum below shows the fusion happening — discrete spikes blurring into a smooth band.

Aside · Why grains, not voices

The full splash recipe at 200 events/s would schedule ~2,400 audio nodes per second and bring the graph to its knees. The grain costs two nodes and is gone in 30 ms. This is the standard granular-synthesis trade: below ~20 events/s the ear tracks individuals, so spend on fidelity; above it the ear hears population statistics, so spend on density. The crossover is audible in the demo if you listen for the moment "drops" become "rain".

The lake now answers: strikes boom, rises bloop behind you, the shore breathes with the wind, and weather will arrive on the bus already audible. Two senses down. But both so far are reactive — the world making sound at the bird.
