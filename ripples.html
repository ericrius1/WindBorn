Wind over Water

A lake is a wind gauge you can read from a mile up. Today the still skin from part one starts moving: five traveling waves, one shared wind field, and a surface the rest of the game can trust.

In part one we built the optics of water — the Fresnel ramp, the glitter path — and it already read as a lake. But it read as a lake in a photograph. Fish Hawk is a game about wind: the osprey's whole movement economy will run on ridge lift and thermals, and the same air that holds the bird up drags across the water below it. If the lake doesn't answer the wind, the world is lying. So this post builds the answer: ripples as a sum of traveling waves whose headings and heights come from one shared wind field — the exact object the flight model will read later. One weather system, every subscriber agreeing.

Step 1 · One traveling waveThe unit of water motion

Start with the smallest thing that can move like water: a single sinusoid gliding across the plane. Pick a unit direction d, a wavelength λ, an amplitude A, and write the height at position (x, z) and time t as: 

h = A · sin( k·(d·x) − k·c·t ),    k = 2π / λ

k is the spatial frequency; c is the phase speed — how fast crests travel. And here physics hands us a gift: c is not a free parameter. For deep-water surface waves, gravity and surface tension together set the speed of every wavelength: 

c(λ) = √( gλ/2π  +  2πσ/(ρλ) )

The first term is gravity (long waves run faster); the second is surface tension pulling tiny ripples flat (σ ≈ 0.072 N/m for water — very short waves also run fast). Between them sits a slowest wave, around λ ≈ 1.7 cm at 23 cm/s. This is dispersion — different wavelengths travel at different speeds — and it's the reason real water never looks like a marching texture: the pattern itself won't hold still. The demo drives its crest speed straight from this formula; change λ and watch the speed follow. One more detail worth noting: the surface normal is computed from the exact derivative of h — we differentiate the sine by hand instead of finite-differencing it, so lighting stays clean at any zoom.

Aside · Why a sine is honest here

The full equations of water motion are unfriendly, but linearizing them for small amplitudes — wave height much smaller than wavelength — yields exactly these traveling sinusoids, and crucially, makes them superpose: the surface from several waves is just their sum. A mountain lake is the best-case scenario for that assumption: centimeters of chop on hundreds of meters of fetch. Ocean storm swell breaks the small-amplitude deal and needs sharper crest shapes; our lake never will.

Step 2 · The chorusA spectrum of five

One sine is hypnotic and fake. Real wind doesn't excite one wavelength; it pours energy into a whole spectrum, long swell to fine capillary dressing. We approximate that spectrum with just five waves, chosen by hand: wavelengths stepping down 11 m → 5.3 m → 2.1 m → 0.9 m → 0.45 m, each shorter wave weaker and angled differently. Because the linearized system superposes, the surface is literally h = Σ hᵢ — five sines added. Scroll through what addition does to the profile:

Five is a tuned budget, not a law: each wave costs a sine in the vertex shader and a cosine in the normal, and five is where I stopped being able to point at the repetition. The table also gets one knob the physics doesn't strictly own — choppiness, a tilt that scales wave i of N by chop^(i/(N−1)): above 1 it pushes energy into the short end (gusty, textured), below 1 it calms the dressing and leaves the swell (oily, heavy). Aside · A declared cheat about speed

The dispersion formula says an 11 m swell should travel about 4.1 m/s. The table runs it at 1 m/s, on purpose: at our camera distances the true speeds read as frantic, like the lake is fleeing the scene. The relative ordering is preserved — long waves still outrun short ones, so nothing looks reversed — but absolute speeds are tuned for calm. It's the one place this surface knowingly leaves the physics, and it's written down in the wave table's source as a comment so future-me doesn't "fix" it.

Step 3 · The steering wheelOne wind field

Now wire the table to the weather. Stillwater has a single wind object — a vector field wind.sample(x, y, z, t) with a mean direction, a mean speed, and smooth gusts layered on top. The waves take two things from it. Heading: each table wave's direction is a fixed offset from the wind direction, fanned across about ±70°, because wind-driven waves travel broadly downwind but never in lockstep. Height: every amplitude scales linearly with wind speed — 3.5 cm of base amplitude per m/s — so a dying breeze takes the texture with it, and dead calm is glass. That last property is load-bearing for the finale of this series, and for the game: dawn stillness isn't a separate art asset, it's just wind.speed → 0.

Step 4 · The float testTwo evaluations, one truth

Here's the architectural decision that pays for this entire approach: the wave sum is analytic. It's a closed-form function of (x, z, t), so we can evaluate it anywhere, on any processor. The GPU evaluates it per vertex and per pixel to draw the surface you see. The CPU evaluates the same five-wave table inside the spine's waterHeightAt(x, z, t) — the site-wide water query that later series will use to land a bird, bob a fish, float feathers. No readback, no sync, no approximation: two transcriptions of one formula, agreeing to the floating-point digit.

The demo below is the proof. Each buoy just sets y = waterHeightAt(x, z, t) and tilts to the analytic normal, while the water under it is pure GPU. If the two ever drifted apart you'd see the buoys sink or hover; they sit glued at any wind speed. (One honest detail: the big lake meshes only displace vertices with the three longest waves — the centimeter-scale dressing lives in the normals, below vertex resolution. This demo's patch is small and dense enough to displace all five, which is what makes exact parity visible.)
