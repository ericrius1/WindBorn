A Day at the Lake

One whole day, conducted by a single clock. Dawn mist burns off the glass, a weather front crosses at noon, the dusk hatch turns the surface to rings, and the moon comes up over black water — flown free the whole way, with the first sounds the lake has ever made. The last two milestones were honest graybox. First Flight gave the bird a body and a sky to fly in; The First Catch gave it a hunt in a real valley. Both were also, by their own admission, empty: a placeholder dawn that never moved, no weather, no other life, and not a single sound. Season 3 was the season of filling the world in, and this milestone is the proof that it filled — not a slideshow of features, but one continuous day you can fly from first light to moonrise. The thesis is severe and it is the whole game: the world runs on a clock, and everything subscribes to it. Nothing here is scripted to a timeline. There is one number — the hour — and the sun, the fog, the fish, the insects, the music, and the color of the water are all just functions of it.

Step 1 · One hour, everywhereThe clock as the only knob

Start with the sky, because the sky keeps the time. The Color of Air derives the sun's direction and the day's palette straight from the clock hour; Morning Mist adds the dawn fog that thickens before sunrise and burns off as the hour climbs; The Cloud Layer drifts cumulus on the same wind the bird rides; The Night Shift brings up a moon on a real path and a star field once the sun is gone. None of those modules talk to each other. They each read clock.hour and answer independently, and the result agrees because they are all describing the same sun.

The same is true below the waterline. The schooling fish read the hour for their appetite — listless at midday, ravenous at the dusk hatch — and a single function, hatchIntensity(hour), decides how hard the whole food chain is feeding. So building "the day" is not orchestration; it is subscription. One module gathers every subscriber, reads the hour once per frame, and pushes it into all of them. Below, that whole machine runs with a scrub bar where the player would be: grab the hour and park the day at any minute — watch the sun swing, the fog thin, the fish wake for the hatch, the moon rise — or let go and let it sweep.

Aside · Why a day is ten minutes

The clock has a dayLength in real seconds — the hero runs a ten-minute day by default, and the slider lets you stretch it to twenty or crush it to two. Ten minutes is a deliberate compromise: long enough that dawn and dusk feel like transitions rather than a strobe, short enough that a reader who came to fly sees the whole arc without committing an afternoon. The real game will run days far longer and let you sleep through the dark hours at the nest, but the milestone's job is to show the cycle, so it spins the cycle fast.

Step 2 · The front crossesWeather that arrives over minutes

A day is not just a smooth ramp of light; weather walks across it. Weather Fronts is a small state machine with three moods — calm, breezy, storm — but the trick is that it never snaps. Force a state and every quantity it owns eases toward its new target on its own time constant: the wind shifts over a few seconds, the sky greys over nine, the rain arrives fast because a squall line is a wall. The machine writes the one shared wind field, so when the front's wind builds you can watch it: whitecaps break on the lake, the cloud layer thickens overhead, and the rain falls as thousands of honest rain events on the disturbance bus — each one a ring the surface answers, each one a patter the audio will answer too.

In the hero, this is scheduled rather than random: the day's front is level design. A breeze builds after one o'clock, the squall line crosses mid-afternoon, and the sky is rinsed clean in time for the hatch — so the day has a shape, a middle act with some drama in it, instead of dice. Below, the machine is yours to drive directly. Punch calm, breezy, storm in any order and watch every value chase its target at its own pace — the wind quick, the overcast slow, the rain a wall that arrives and leaves.

Step 3 · The dusk chainThe hatch, the rises, the swallows

Dusk is the event the whole ecosystem is built to deliver, and it is the clearest proof that nothing here is faked. As the hour crosses into golden light, hatchIntensity climbs and the chain turns on in order: mayflies rise off the warm surface, the fish rise to them — each rise stamping a ring on the bus, exactly the rings the hunt reads — and the swallows wake to skim the same hatch, their wingtips writing their own faint lines on the water. The Dusk Event assembled this chain; here it simply runs as one strand of a day that happens to be passing through evening. Below, scrub the dusk window and watch the food chain switch on link by link, or let it play and loop.

Aside · The first sounds

This is also the milestone where the lake first makes noise, and it is all synthesized live — no recorded clips anywhere. The Sound of Speed filters noise banks whose cutoff and gain track the bird's live airspeed, so a dive screams and a glide hisses; What Water Says turns every splash and rise on the bus into a burst of filtered noise scaled by the event's energy; The Adaptive Score crossfades music layers keyed to altitude, hour, and weather. One call wires all of it to the spine. Because browsers refuse audio without a gesture, the hero stays silent until you click "listen" — and because everything ties to the disposable scene, all of it goes quiet the instant you scroll the demo offscreen. No sound survives teardown.

That's the milestone: a whole day, dawn to night, weather and hatch and moonlight and sound, flown free — the verb witness, playable end to end. And for the first time a milestone is not apologizing for what's missing. The valley lives. What it lacks now is not systems but polish: the day is rendered honestly but flatly, with no color grade tying the image to the hour, no bloom on the low sun, no depth of field smearing the world past the wingtip in a dive, no lens droplets after a breach. And it is still a sandbox, not a game — there is no hunger, no nest, no reason to fly beyond the flying. Season 4 closes both gaps. First a cinematic pass grades every hour and bends the light; then every system on this page and the two before it converges into one lake with a loop, a hunt, and a home — the game the whole project has been building toward. Season 3 was witness. Season 4 is the game.
