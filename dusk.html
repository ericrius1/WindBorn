The Dusk Event

One evening, on a loop: the light goes gold, the mayflies lift off, the fish come up to meet them, the swallows come down, and the eagle keeps climbing. Nobody is in charge. Everybody is on time.

Scroll down to read ↓

Every series on this site ends by assembling its parts, but this one assembles into something the game has been quietly promising since the first post: the dusk event, the scheduled hour when the whole valley becomes the HUD. In Fish Hawk this is prime hunting time — the rings are everywhere, the light is forgiving, and a day spent learning the lake pays out in twenty minutes of easy reads. Mechanically, it's the four previous posts running simultaneously: the school, the hatch, the other wings, and the swaying margin, under a golden-hour sky. The work of this post is the assembly itself: one conductor, real coupling between the layers, and a frame budget that survives all of it at sixty.

Step 1 · The conductorOne number runs the evening

There is no "dusk event manager" in the codebase, and that's the point. The only conductor is clock.hour — the same world clock the spine has carried since the scaffold — and a handful of pure functions of it. The hatch spawner reads hatchIntensity(hour). The fish's hunger is hatchIntensity(hour) — appetite tracks opportunity. Swallow activity is a rescale of the same curve, because the swallows are eating the same hatch. The light rig keys its whole palette — sun elevation and color, horizon glow, hemisphere fill, fog — off the hour, peaking gold in the last hour before sunset. Four systems, zero messages between them; they synchronize the way an orchestra without a conductor does, by all reading the same clock. The figure below plots the four response curves with one cursor through them: the entire evening's choreography, as arithmetic.

This design has a consequence worth naming: dusk is not an event. Nothing fires at 19:00; there's no trigger to miss, no state to desync, no save-file flag. Scrub time backward and the evening politely runs in reverse. When the weather season later wants a storm to suppress the hatch, it will multiply one function by another — clouds darken the sky, intensity reads lower, fish stay down — and every dependent behavior will follow without a single new connection.

Step 2 · The chainCoupling, not coincidence

Running four systems on one clock makes them simultaneous. Making them matter to each other is the difference between an ecosystem and a screensaver, and the currency is the mayfly. Duns and spent spinners float on the film; the hatch hands that list to the school every frame, and each fish rise removes a real particle — eaten means gone. Swallows hawk the airborne stage: any bird passing within a wing's reach of a flier takes it. So the swarm you see is a standing crop — spawn rate in, predation out — and it responds the way populations respond. Toggle the fish off below and the surface stages pile up; toggle the swallows off and the dance column thickens within seconds. Turn both back on and watch the equilibrium find itself again. Nothing tells the swarm to shrink: it's just losing members to two predators that are each following their own rules.

The player's information channel falls straight out of this. Rises cluster where floating duns cluster; duns drift from where nymphs emerged; nymph habitat is the 0.5–6 m silt belt along the bathymetry. So when you read a patch of rings at dusk, you are reading the lakebed through four layers of biology — which means learning the lake's geography is learning where dinner is. That's the hunt working as designed, before the hunt mechanics even exist.

Aside · Why everything eats mayflies

A real hatch is one of the great energy transfers in freshwater: biomass that spent a year accumulating on the lake bottom converts itself into surface protein in an hour, with no defenses — adult mayflies can't even eat, let alone flee. Trout abandon caution for it, swallows time their evening foraging around it, and on the big mayfly lakes the emergence is dense enough to show up on weather radar. An efficiency our budget appreciates: one prey species feeds the entire cast.

Step 3 · Holding sixtyThe budget of a living lake

The dusk scene is the most populated frame the site has rendered: two hundred fish, a couple thousand mayflies, two dozen birds, tens of thousands of plants, the lake surface, and the terrain. The reason it holds 60 fps is that population size and draw-call count were decoupled in every post: one geometry per species, one instanced draw per population, behavior on the CPU writing nothing but instance matrices, deformation — tail wiggle, wingbeat, grass bend — in the vertex stage where it costs per-vertex, not per-creature. The CPU side stays honest too: the boids pair test is the only super-linear cost in the scene, and at two hundred fish it's under half a millisecond; everything else — hatch stages, skim runs, slot-chasing geese — is a few arithmetic lines per agent per frame.

The demo below is the assembled scene with a kill switch per system. Watch the fps readout as you toggle: plants are tens of thousands of instances but nearly free (static matrices, vertex-stage sway); the hatch is thousands of moving instances and costs its matrix uploads; the fish cost their pair test. That's the profile the final game inherits, and the knobs — fish count, hatch capacity, glint pool — are exactly where the future performance post will reach for them.

Step back and look at what the season built. A lake that keeps an appointment: every evening, on a clock the whole world shares, the light turns gold and the food chain surfaces — insects rising, rings spreading, swallows stitching dotted lines between them, the eagle circling its tower of warm air above it all. No scripts, no triggers; five populations reading three spine contracts (clock, wind, disturb) and one terrain function. For the game, this is the table set: the spot verb now has real signal to read, the hunt has prey with honest positions, and the thief has a perch. What the valley needs next is a voice — wind that roars past your ears in a dive, water that answers every ring on the bus with a sound. That's the next series, The Ear.
