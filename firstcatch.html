The First Catch

Season two ends in the water. A real basin holds the lake, a real fluid simulation lives in a window around the bird, and the dive is the whole point: read the rings, tuck, punch through the surface, grab in the dark, and burst back into the light with a fish.

Scroll down to read ↓

First Flight ended on an apology: the mountains were silhouette cardboard, the lake had no floor, and the splash was a polite analytic ring drawn on a flat sheet. It was a stage set with a real actor on it. The verb was soar, and it worked — but the game is named for a hunt, and you can't hunt over a stage set. Season 2 was three series of digging a real hole for the bird to dive into. The Basin carved a glacial valley out of warped ridged noise and ran the bathymetry on under the water, so the lake finally has a bottom that the water knows about. The Splash built a real fluid simulation that lives only in a small window around the action and blends into the ambient lake at its rim. Under took the camera below the surface and assembled the dive cycle. The hero above is all of it at once, flyable: the same player-driven bird from First Flight, now diving into water that pushes back. Click in, read the surface for cruising fish, line up, and commit. There is no fail state. A missed dive costs you a wet climb-out and another lap — never a retry screen.

Step 1 · The surface is the HUDReading the water for fish

The whole design of the hunt is a refusal: no markers, no minimap, no glowing fish. The fish telegraph themselves, and the surface of the lake is the only HUD there is. That sounds like a stylistic choice, but it's mostly a physics one. A fish cruising the shallows catches the low sun on its flank and throws a glint; a fish rising to feed breaks the surface and stamps a ring that spreads outward on the disturbance bus. Both of those are real signals a real osprey reads, and both of them already exist in the engine for other reasons — the glints are the schooling fish catching the sun, the rings are the same bus packets the lake has answered since the very first water demo.

So the spotting mechanic costs almost no new code: point the school at a shallow bay, let it cruise and rise, and let the surface do the talking. The catch is that the lake's own ripple rings are a close-range effect — the ring pool is small, the sim window is only a few meters wide. A rise has to be legible from eighty meters up, at cruise altitude, where the local surface shader is a smear. So the bus feeds a second, coarser telegraph too: flat expanding geometry rings, one per event, readable from the air. Below, a school feeds under a flat-bottomed tank — everything beneath the surface stripped away so you can practice the one skill the hunt asks for. Turn the feeding activity up and watch the rings multiply; turn the wind up and watch the chop start hiding the glints, exactly the way it hides them for a real bird.

Aside · Why ospreys, specifically

The osprey is the only hawk that hunts this way — a near-vertical plunge, feet first, fully submerging from heights of ten to thirty meters. Its outer toe is reversible, so it can clamp a slippery fish two-and-two instead of three-and-one, and its talons are unusually long and round in cross-section. Dense, oily plumage keeps it from waterlogging, mostly; even so a soaked osprey hauls a real fraction of its own weight in water out of the lake on every dive. That weight is a gameplay number, not just a fact — it's why carrying a catch in this build costs you power and trim in the air.

Step 2 · The window follows the diveA simulation that travels

A glacial lake is a couple of kilometers across. Simulating fluid across all of it at a resolution fine enough to see a splash crown would melt the laptop, and it would be wasted: ninety-nine percent of that water is calm and far away, perfectly described by the cheap analytic wave sum the Mirror lake already renders. The trick from The Sim Bubble is to simulate only where it matters. A fixed-size grid — a few hundred cells on a side, covering a window twenty-some meters wide — follows the bird around the lake. Inside the window you get the real thing: two-way coupling, a crown of spray, spreading wakes. At the window's rim the simulated height cross-fades into the ambient analytic surface, so the seam between "real sim" and "fake lake" is invisible.

The piece that makes this honest is the waterHeightAt(x, z, t) contract. The whole engine asks that one function for the surface height — the floating bird, the plunge floor, the camera's surface-crossing test, the splash audio. Installing the sim swaps the provider behind that function: now it answers with the simulated height wherever the window covers, and the ambient sum everywhere else, and nothing that calls it has to know. The float you flew in First Flight survives the swap without a line changed; it just finally makes a real hole. Below, a scripted diver loops the dive forever so you can stare at the one seam — the window sliding to keep up, the depth term read straight from the bathymetry so waves slow and steepen over the shallows. Drag the blend rim in, or toggle the hard edge, and watch the seam appear.

Step 3 · The grab, in the darkOne short window under the surface

Here is where the dive stops being a flight problem and becomes a hunt. The plunge itself is honest physics: entry pays a splash proportional to kinetic energy, then the roughly eight-hundred-fold jump in density scrubs the bird's speed to nothing in about a quarter second, and buoyancy — a feathered body is mostly trapped air — turns it around and rows it back toward the light. Inside that brief submarine moment is the grab. For a window of barely a second, talons-first and early in the plunge, the bird reaches: any fish within a fixed talon radius is caught and rides home; reach short and the fish bolts for the deep hole.

That single radius check is the entire fishing minigame, and keeping it that simple is deliberate. There is no quick-time event, no button mash, no aim cursor underwater. You aimed the whole dive; the grab is the dive's verdict, read once. The submarine moment is also where Under earns its keep: the camera crosses the surface lens with you — a meniscus band on the way down, droplets streaking the glass on the way up — the key light loses its red as you go deeper, and a depth dome stands in for water in every direction. Below, the grab runs in slow motion so you can watch the talons close. Slow time further, widen or pinch the talon reach, and replay until the geometry of the catch is obvious.

Aside · The frame budget

The hero scene runs three expensive systems at once — a planar-free but still costly sim step, a spray particle system, and an underwater render path — so the budget is spent deliberately. The fluid grid is held to a hundred-sixty cells square inside a twenty-eight-meter window; spray is capped at a couple thousand sprites; the terrain is one graded mesh, fine where you fly and coarsening geometrically out to the rim, so the kilometers of far shore cost almost nothing. The sim mirrors its texture for one frame's latency to avoid a read-back stall. That all leaves headroom to hold sixty on an M-series laptop, which is the only frame rate this project considers shipped.

That's the milestone: spot, dive, splash, grab, breach — the verb dive, playable end to end, built almost entirely from three series that each shipped their piece in isolation and met for the first time on this page. The glue is small because the contracts were set years of essays ago: one water query the sim swaps behind, one bus every splash and rise rides, one bird that doesn't care which law of nature owns it this frame. And like First Flight, it is still honestly a graybox. The valley is real terrain but the light is a placeholder dawn; there is no weather, no time of day, no sound. The fish school and rise, but the lake is otherwise empty — no swallows skimming, no mayflies on the water, no eagle to steal your catch. Season 3 fills the world in. First Flight gave the bird a body and a sky to fly in; this milestone gave it a hunt. The next one gives it a day — dawn mist burning off glass water, a weather front crossing at noon, the dusk hatch that turns the whole surface to rings, a moonlit night, and the first sounds the lake has ever made. Season 2 was dive. Season 3 is witness.
