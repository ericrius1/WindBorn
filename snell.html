Snell's Window

Look up from underwater and the whole sky — horizon to horizon — fits inside one bright circle. Outside it, the surface mirrors the lake back at itself. One angle separates the two, and we can derive it in a line.

Scroll down to read ↓

In the last post the camera learned to cross the surface; this post decides what it sees once it's down there. Fish Hawk's underwater second plays out staring at the ceiling of the lake — the osprey grabs, turns, and rows for the light — so that ceiling has to be worth staring at. And the physics hands us something better than any matte painting: from below, the air–water interface splits into two sharply different regimes. Straight overhead, a circle of compressed sky. Everywhere else, a mirror — not a tinted, partial, Fresnel-ish mirror, but a perfect one. The whole thing falls out of the refraction law we've been using since we first looked into the shallows, just run in the opposite direction.

Step 1 · The derivationRunning Snell backwards

Snell's law relates the angles (measured from the surface normal) on the two sides of an interface: n₁·sin θ₁ = n₂·sin θ₂. Going air→water, as in Seeing the Bottom, light bends toward the normal: divide by n = 1.333 and the angle shrinks. Going water→air the same equation bends rays away from the normal: 

sin θ_air = n · sin θ_water

Now push the underwater angle steeper and watch the right-hand side. At θ_water = 30°, sin θ_air = 0.667 — fine, the ray exits at 41.8°. At 45°, sin θ_air = 0.943 — the exit ray is skimming at 70.5°. And the moment n·sin θ_water reaches 1, the exit ray lies flat in the surface itself. Beyond that there is no solution: no real angle has a sine greater than one. The crossover is the critical angle: 

sin θc = 1/n  →  θc = asin(1/1.333) ≈ 48.6°

Past θc the interface stops transmitting entirely — total internal reflection. Not 98% like a good household mirror; all of it, by conservation, because there is nowhere else for the energy to go. And the handoff isn't a hard switch: the Fresnel equations (the same ones from the skin of the lake, by reciprocity) send the reflected share climbing smoothly to 1 as θ approaches θc, so the window dims into the mirror at its rim rather than cutting. Step 2 · The geometryThe circle overhead

Flip the picture around — light runs the same paths in both directions, so an underwater eye receives sky along exactly the rays that could have exited. Every direction within 48.6° of vertical sees through to the air; every direction outside doesn't. That's a cone, apex at your eye, opening 97.2° wide, and the entire 180° dome of the sky maps into it: the zenith at its center, the horizons compressed into its last sliver of rim. The squeeze is wildly nonuniform — the middle of the window is a nearly honest sky, while the outermost degree of cone holds tens of degrees of low sky folded together, which is why the rim of a real Snell's window burns with whatever color the horizon has.

The cone's angle never changes, but the circle it cuts in the surface grows with depth: r = d·tan θc ≈ 1.13·d. Sink to two meters and your window is a 4.5 m circle; the rest of the surface, out to every horizon, is mirror. Aside · The fish already knew

Everything that hunts through this interface lives by its geometry. A trout holding station sees the entire above-water world — herons, anglers, ospreys — through its private 97° porthole, which is why experienced fishermen crouch: dropping your silhouette toward the horizon pushes you into the compressed rim where you're a smear of color, or out of the window entirely. The osprey plays the other side of the same projection — its steep final approach in the dive isn't just about refraction error at the fish, it also keeps the bird inside the blown-out brightness around the window's center, where wings are hardest to pick out against glare.

Step 3 · The mirrorOutside the glass

Now render it. The ceiling is the same displaced plane as the topside surface — same wave table, same uniforms, glued vertex-for-vertex so a crossing camera never sees a seam — drawn back-face so it only exists from below. Per fragment we run the machinery straight out of the derivation: refract(view, −normal, n) hands back the exit direction, or the zero vector beyond the critical angle. Inside the window we look that exit direction up in the sky (sun disc included, so the sun hangs inside the circle at its refracted position) and pay the Fresnel transmittance. Outside, reflect() sends the view ray back down into the lake, and the mirror shows what the lake has: the medium's glow, and anything bright enough to matter. Aside · Why the rim shimmers

On real lakes the window's edge is never a clean circle — it frays and breathes. The reason is in the normals: every passing wave tilts the local surface, and a tilted facet carries its own critical cone with it. A 5° wave slope swings the local window edge by 5°, so the rim becomes a band where window and mirror interleave at wave frequency. We get this for free: the refract/reflect split runs on the rippled normal from the wave table, not the flat plane, so wind speed directly sets how nervous the boundary is. Glass calm, razor rim; breeze, boiling lace.

OnwardWhat this unlocks

The underwater world now has its ceiling: a sky compressed into a circle, a mirror beyond it, a rim that responds to the wind, and a sun that sits where refraction says it should. The grab moment finally has something overhead worth composing against. But the same bent sunlight that builds the window doesn't stop at the surface — it keeps going down, and the waves focus it into writhing bright filaments on the lakebed. Those caustics, the fog the light dies into, the shafts it draws through the water, and the bubbles that rise through all of it are the next post — the last one before we assemble the dive.
