Waves as Arithmetic

Every ripple in this pond is computed, not scripted: two numbers per cell, one rule, sixty times a second. This is the start of the machine the dive will land in.

Fish Hawk's whole pitch lives in about two seconds of gameplay: the osprey folds, falls, and hits the lake talons-first. If that splash is a canned animation, the game has no centerpiece. So this series builds a real fluid simulation — water that rings, reflects, piles up, and pushes back. Our lake already looks right: wind-driven waves, real reflections, depth-tinted shallows. And when something touches it, the finale scene spawns an expanding analytic ring from the disturbance bus. That ring is a good fake — but it's deaf. It doesn't bounce off a shoreline, two rings pass through each other without a thought, and nothing can surf it. Today we replace scripted motion with computed motion: the wave equation, on a GPU grid, derived from almost nothing.

Step 1 · The stateTwo numbers per cell

Chop a square of water into a grid, 256 cells on a side. Each cell stores the surface height h (meters above calm) and the vertical velocity w = ∂h/∂t (how fast that height is changing). That pair is the entire state — about half a megabyte for sixty-five thousand cells. The physics is one question asked everywhere at once: what accelerates the surface?

Picture one cell sitting higher than its four neighbors. Water under pressure flows away from the pile, so the pile falls; a cell in a dip gets filled. The restoring acceleration is proportional to how far the cell sits from the average of its neighbors — not from zero. A perfectly flat-but-tilted region feels no force at all, which is exactly right: a slope by itself isn't a wave. Written out for cell (i, j) with spacing dx: 

lap = (h[i−1,j] + h[i+1,j] + h[i,j−1] + h[i,j+1] − 4·h[i,j]) / dx²
∂w/∂t = c² · lap

That neighbor sum is the five-point stencil, the discrete Laplacian ∇²h — the curvature of the surface. The continuous statement, ∂²h/∂t² = c²∇²h, is the classical wave equation, and c is the speed its disturbances travel at. For today c is a dial we turn by hand; part two derives what actually sets it (the answer is the depth of the lakebed, which is a much better story). Aside · Why height fields at all

Real splash hydrodynamics is 3-D: pressure fields, vortices, entrained air. A height field collapses all of that to "the surface is a function h(x, z)" — it cannot overturn, so a true plunging breaker is out of reach. For a mountain lake that trade is nearly free: everything an osprey makes — rings, wakes, crowns — reads as surface motion, and the budget is two floats per cell instead of a volumetric grid. The one thing we'll genuinely miss (the underwater cavity a dive punches) gets faked honestly in part five with particles.

Step 2 · The marchPing-pong, or how a GPU takes a step

Integrating is two lines per cell. Kick the velocity with the acceleration, then drift the height with the new velocity — the "kick-then-drift" order (symplectic Euler) conserves the wave's energy far better than updating both from old values: 

w ← (w + c²·lap·dt) · damp
h ←  h + w·dt

On a CPU you'd loop over cells. On the GPU all sixty-five thousand cells update simultaneously — and that concurrency springs a trap. My update reads my four neighbors' heights; my neighbors are updating at the same instant. Do I read their old height or their new one? Whichever thread happens to run first wins, and the answer changes every frame: a race condition baked into the physics. The fix is never to write where you read. Keep two copies of the state: every substep reads buffer A in its entirety and writes buffer B in its entirety, then the buffers swap roles. A is always "the past," B is always "the future," and no thread can see a half-written present. That swap dance is ping-pong, and it's the structural heart of every grid sim in this series. Step 3 · The speed limitThe CFL condition

There's a number hiding in that update that decides whether the simulation is physics or an explosion. In one substep dt, a real wave moves c·dt meters. But information on the grid moves at most one cell — dx — per substep, because the stencil only reaches one neighbor out. If c·dt > dx, the true wave outruns the grid's ability to pass the message along; the discrete system can't represent what should have happened, the error compounds every step, and the surface tears itself apart within a second. The dimensionless ratio 

CFL = c · dt · √2 / dx     (the √2 is the diagonal of a 2-D grid)

must stay below 1 — the Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy condition, and it is not advisory. Our stepper measures the frame time, computes the largest stable substep, and runs as many substeps as needed (a fast pond at 60 fps usually needs one or two). Aside · Damping is honesty, not defeat

The damp factor multiplies the velocity by e^(−k·dt) each substep. Real ponds lose wave energy to viscosity and to air; an undamped grid rings forever, and worse, the shortest representable waves (two cells per crest) accumulate numerical junk. A small k — we use 0.1 to 0.2 per second — fades a splash over tens of seconds, which is what a real lake does. The hero's damping slider makes the difference obvious: zero feels like a gong, too much feels like syrup.

Step 4 · The wallsReflect or absorb

One decision is still implicit: what happens at the edge of the grid, where a cell is missing a neighbor? Our stencil clamps — an edge cell reads itself in place of the missing neighbor. That makes the height gradient zero across the wall, which physically is a perfect mirror: waves bounce straight back with no loss, like a bathtub. Sometimes that's exactly right (it will be the shoreline's job in part two). But our final simulation is a small window onto a huge lake — a wave that leaves the window should be gone, not reflected back as a ghost. For that we add a sponge: in a rim a few cells wide, both h and w get an extra damping that ramps up quadratically toward the edge. OnwardWhat this unlocks

We now own a pond that computes itself: state, stencil, ping-pong, a speed limit we respect, and walls we choose. But it has one big lie in it — that constant c. Real water's wave speed isn't a dial; it's set by the depth underneath, which is why swell slows down, bunches up, and steepens as it feels a beach. We already built that beach: The Lakebed gave the whole valley a depth function.
