Shallow Water

Swell rolling onto a real shoreline of our valley — slowing, bunching, steepening — because the simulation now reads the lakebed under every cell. The wave speed dial from part one becomes physics.

Scroll down to read ↓

Part one ended with a confession: our pond's wave speed c was a slider. Real water doesn't have a speed dial — it has a bottom. The wave speed at every point is set by how deep the water is right there, which is why ocean swell, invisible in deep water, rears up and breaks on a beach. Fish Hawk needs this for real reasons: the osprey hunts the shallows where fish flash near the surface, the game's shorelines are where splash meets land, and The Basin series already built the bathymetry — a depth function terrainHeightAt(x, z) the whole site shares. Today we derive the shallow-water equations from scratch and wire that function straight into the integrator. The payoff: drop a stone anywhere in the valley's lake and the rings respond to a lakebed we sculpted three posts ago.

Step 1 · The bookkeepingColumns, flux, and two laws

Forget the membrane picture from part one; this time we do honest fluid accounting. Stand a column of water on each grid cell: still depth d(x, z) from the lakebed up to the calm line, plus the live surface offset η on top (our old h, renamed because it now rides on a variable bottom). Give each column a horizontal velocity v = (vx, vz) — in shallow water the whole column moves together, which is the approximation the name announces: it holds when wavelengths are long compared to the depth.

Two conservation laws close the system. Mass: the water sliding between columns is the flux q = d·v (a deep column carries more than a shallow one at the same speed). Wherever more arrives than leaves, the surface must rise: ∂η/∂t = −∇·(d·v). Momentum: a tilted surface means more water — more pressure — under the high side, and the imbalance accelerates the column downhill: ∂v/∂t = −g·∇η. Scroll through the whole argument:

Substitute one law into the other (take ∂/∂t of the mass equation, insert the momentum equation) and for constant depth you get ∂²η/∂t² = g·d·∇²η — exactly part one's wave equation, with the mystery dial exposed: 

c = √(g·d)

Gravity times depth, square root. Two meters of water carries waves at 4.4 m/s; twenty centimeters, at 1.4 m/s. The discrete update is the same ping-pong machinery as before — read buffer A, write buffer B — just with three state numbers per cell instead of two, central differences for the divergence and the gradient, and the depth buffer consulted at every neighbor. Aside · One declared cheat

Shallow-water theory treats every wave as a long wave. Over the deep basin (20 m and more) that gives centimeter splash ripples a speed of 14 m/s — they'd flee the window before you saw them. Real physics fixes this with dispersion (short waves travel at √(gλ/2π) instead, the formula from Wind over Water), but dispersion doesn't fit a local stencil. So the sim caps the effective depth at about 1.5 m: wherever the lake is deeper, waves run as if it were 1.5 m deep. Near shore — everywhere shallower than the cap — the true depth takes over and shoaling is honest. It's the same kind of declared look-cheat as the wave table's slowed swell, written down in the source where future-me will find it.

Step 2 · ShoalingWhy waves stand up on a beach

Now make the depth a ramp and the equations start telling stories. A swell train marches toward a linear beach: as d shrinks, c = √(g·d) shrinks with it, so the front of each crest is always slower than the back. Three things follow, all visible below. The crests bunch up — wavelength shortens in proportion to speed because the period can't change. The wave steepens — the energy flux E·c must stay roughly constant, so as c drops the amplitude grows (Green's law, amplitude ∝ d^(−1/4)). And the crest line bends parallel to the shore — the end of a crest in shallower water lags the end in deeper water, refracting the whole front. None of that is coded anywhere. Aside · Where this model stops

Follow a crest far enough up the ramp and real water breaks: the crest outruns its own base and overturns. A height field cannot overturn — η is one value per location — so our waves steepen, then the damping and the wet/dry boundary quietly absorb what a real beach would spend on whitewater. The foam channel in part six paints the visual consequence without simulating the violence. Honest budget: breaking is the one shoreline phenomenon we fake.

Step 3 · The real lakebedReading the Basin

The ramp was a rehearsal; the lake has the real thing. The Lakebed established the contract: terrainHeightAt(x, z) is negative underwater, positive on land, zero exactly at the shoreline — and it ships with a GPU twin, terrainHeightNode, that evaluates the same composition in a shader. So filling the sim's depth buffer is one compute pass: d = max(0, −terrainHeight) at each cell's world position. Cells with d = 0 are land. They carry zero flux — and a zero-flux wall is precisely the reflecting boundary from part one, so waves bounce off the shoreline without a single line of shoreline code. The sponge rim from part one still guards the window's outer edge, where the lake continues but our grid doesn't.

OnwardWhat this unlocks

The simulation is now made of the same world as everything else: one depth function shared with the terrain renderer, the fish (soon), and the wind's ridge lift. But it still lives on a parked grid a few dozen meters wide, and Fish Hawk's bird goes wherever it pleases over a square kilometer of lake. We can't afford that lake at splash resolution — and we don't have to. Next post builds the series' namesake trick: a fixed-budget window that follows the action, hands off seamlessly to the ambient analytic water at its rim, and swaps itself in as the site-wide water provider. The lake will be simulated only where it matters, and nobody watching will be able to tell where the seam is.
