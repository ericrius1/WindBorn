The Loop

Everything the lake does is already built. What it lacks is a reason to fly: hunger that creeps, daylight that runs out, days that add up, and a save file to carry them between. Here is that skeleton — and the rule that a missed dive costs an hour of light, never an hour of progress.

After three seasons, Stillwater is a world that moves on its own. A whole day runs from dawn mist to moonrise; an osprey hunts in a real valley; the food chain turns on at golden hour. You can fly all of it, and for a while that is enough. But fly it long enough and a question surfaces that no shader answers: why? Why dive, when a missed strike costs nothing and a clean one earns nothing? Season 4's last series, the one the whole project has been climbing toward, is the answer — and it is the smallest amount of game that makes the world feel like one. This first post builds the skeleton: the few numbers that turn flying into being an osprey for a few days.

The design constraint is severe and it shapes everything: no fail states. Fish Hawk is A Short Hike, not a survival game. You cannot lose. A missed dive is not a penalty; it is a spent half-hour of daylight, and there are plenty of half-hours in a day. So the state has to nag without punishing — it has to give you a reason to fly toward a fish without ever making you dread the water. That balance lives in exactly four numbers.

Step 1 · Four numbers and a saveThe state machine

Here is the entire game model, and you can play it with no graphics at all. Hunger runs from zero (full) to one (starving); it creeps up while you fly — roughly four minutes of flying from full to the edge — and a caught fish knocks it back down. Daylight is not stored anywhere: it is the world clock. A function reads clock.hour and reports how much usable light is left, from first glow around five to last light at nine in the evening; spending time is spending light. Day count increments when you roost for the night at the nest. And the nest stage grows as you feed at home — the subject of a later post. All four serialize to localStorage under "fishhawk:save", so a day can end and the next one begin.

Below is that model, live and in your hands — no scene, just the state. Fly an hour, take a dive, eat, sleep, and reload the page: the save survives. Note that the dive button spends daylight whether or not you imagine it landed. That asymmetry — time always spent, fish sometimes caught — is the loop's whole tension, and it never crosses into punishment.

Aside · Why hunger, and not health

Hunger is a gentler master than a health bar. A health bar implies damage, and damage implies an enemy and a death. Hunger just rises, and the worst it does is make the next meal more welcome. Even fully starved, the osprey flies exactly as well — the only thing that changes is the word in the corner of the screen and a faint shift in the music's mood. The state is a reason to fish, never a threat. That is the difference between a game you can lose and a game you can only spend an evening inside.

Step 2 · Daylight as the budgetThe clock you spend

If a missed dive can't cost progress, what does it cost? Time. The day is the resource, and the world clock that the sky, the fog, and the fish already read is also the budget you draw down. The figure below scrubs the day over the bay where the school lives: watch the daylight readout fall as the hour climbs, and — just as importantly — watch the fish. They are listless at midday and ravenous at the dusk hatch, because their appetite is the same hatchIntensity(hour) the ecosystem runs on. That coupling is the strategy of the whole game: daylight is finite, and the hours when a dive is most likely to pay are the hours the light is going. You learn, without being told, to spend your best light on the rises.

That is the loop: soar (spend stamina and light), spot (read the water), dive (spend a chance at a fish), feed (answer the hunger), and across a handful of days, build something at the nest. The hero up top flies that cycle on its own so you can see the shape of it — a day rolling over, a catch going down the hatch, the day count ticking up. Everything from here is making each verb real. Next, the one that has no UI at all: spotting a fish the way an osprey does, by reading the surface, with not a single marker on the screen.
