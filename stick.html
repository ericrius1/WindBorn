The Stick

The physics can fly; now you get to. This post wires keyboard and gamepad into bank, pitch, and effort commands, derives why banking turns the bird at all, and builds the chase camera that turns numbers into sensation.

Scroll down to read ↓

Four Forces gave us a bird that obeys the air. But a game is a loop between two nervous systems — the simulated one and yours — and right now the wire between them is missing. Today we install it. In Fish Hawk you will spend ninety minutes holding this exact stick: every dive starts with a pitch command, every thermal turn is a held bank, every panicked flare off the water is the same input pipeline. If this layer is mushy, the whole game is mushy. If it's right, you stop thinking about your hands.

The design rule that everything below follows: inputs are commands, not state. Your keyboard never sets the bank angle — it requests one, and the bird leans toward the request at the rate a 1.5 kg body with 1.6 m of wing plausibly could. That single layer of indirection is the difference between piloting an animal and dragging an icon.

Step 1 · CommandsFaking an analog soul for digital keys

The model wants three channels: bank in −1…1, pitch in −1…1, and effort in 0…1. A gamepad stick supplies the first two natively — analog, already smooth. A keyboard supplies square waves: A is either down or up. Feed that raw ±1 into the model and every turn entry is a slap.

So keys get ramped: the command slews toward the key's target at a finite rate — about 0.2 s from center to full deflection, slightly faster on release. Tap A and you get a nudge; hold it and the command eases to full lock. Two rates, rise and fall, and suddenly WASD has half the expressiveness of a stick. Gamepads skip the ramp (their hardware already moved through the middle) and instead get a deadzone, re-scaled so there's no jump at its edge — drift protection without a numb center.

Aside · Which way is up?

Aviation convention says stick back pitches the nose up. Fish Hawk maps W / stick-forward to climb, because that's what every non-pilot's thumb expects, and our players are people who like birds, not people with type ratings. The mapping is one sign flip in one file. Feel is full of decisions like this: physically arbitrary, experientially decisive.

Step 2 · The turnBanking is steering, for free

Here's the part that still delights me: we don't write any turning code. The bank command rolls the lift vector — the model takes lift perpendicular to the airflow and rotates it around the flight path by the bank angle φ. Tilted lift has a horizontal component, and an unbalanced horizontal force is, by definition, a centripetal acceleration. Setting L·sin φ = mV²/r and keeping the vertical balanced (L·cos φ = mg) gives the turn radius: 

r = V² / (g · tan φ)

At cruise (10 m/s) a gentle 30° bank turns in about 18 m; a steep 45° tightens that to 10 m — numbers that will matter enormously when we're trying to stay inside a 20 m thermal core two posts from now. Note what the formula doesn't contain: any tunable. The demo below measures the actual flown radius and compares it against the prediction, live. The two readouts disagreeing by more than rounding would mean the physics is lying.

There's a bill, of course. Banked level flight needs L = W/cos φ — more lift than weight, which means more angle of attack, which means more induced drag. Steep turns are expensive; the bird sinks or slows unless it works. That cost isn't a penalty we scripted, it's the physics doing game design for us.

Step 3 · The cameraA cameraman with a heartbeat

The camera is the player's inner ear: it's how acceleration becomes sensation. Ours is a chase rig with three smoothed quantities — a follow position behind the bird's horizontal heading, an aim point ahead of the beak, and the field of view. Each one chases its target with frame-rate-independent damping: 

pos ← lerp(pos, target, 1 − e^(−λ·dt))

The exponential form matters. A naive lerp(pos, target, 0.1) smooths twice as hard at 30 fps as at 60 — your camera's personality would depend on the player's GPU. With 1 − e^(−λdt) the lag λ is a real physical rate, identical on every machine. The aim point leads the bird by a few meters, so when you bank, the camera looks into the turn before the body swings — the camera anticipating you is most of what people mean by "responsive".

Drag the stiffness slider down and the rig turns seasick; crank it and the camera becomes a rigid pole and the world stops feeling fast. There's a band in the middle — around 3–4 s⁻¹ — where the camera feels like a competent cameraman on a following boat. Then hit the rigid toggle to feel exactly what the smoothing was buying you.

Step 4 · Speed in the lensFOV is the speedometer

Fish Hawk has no HUD, so speed has to live in the picture. The strongest single cue is field of view: widening the lens exaggerates optic flow at the frame's edges, the same peripheral streaming your visual system uses to judge speed on a highway. The rig maps airspeed above cruise to extra FOV — about a degree per m/s, smoothed through the same exponential damping so it swells rather than pops: 

fov = 55° + 1.1 · max(0, V − 9)

Nine m/s is cruise, where the lens sits at its neutral 55°. A 25 m/s dive opens it past 70°, and because the change rides the airspeed, a dive into a headwind reads faster than one with the wind behind you — which is true, aerodynamically and emotionally. There's a second trick (kicking the FOV on acceleration rather than speed) that we'll save for the joy pass, where it belongs.

We can now point the bird anywhere and feel it go. Which means players will immediately do what players do: pull up until something gives, and dive until the water gets loud. Right now the model's edges are merely there — next post we make them good. The Envelope is about stalls that mush instead of punish, dives that earn their speed, and the flare that saves you a meter off the lake.
