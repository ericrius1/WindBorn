The Grade

Every system so far renders honest light. This series renders honest film: a short chain of per-pixel functions between the scene and the screen, keyed to the hour, that makes the lake feel filmed instead of computed. Drag the hour — the look follows the sun.

Season four opens with a confession: Fish Hawk has been rendered, not photographed. The lake reflects what the sky hands it, the sun dims honestly as it sets, and yet a screenshot at golden hour looks like a simulation of golden hour rather than a photograph of one. The difference is not in the scene. It is in the four centimeters between a film camera's sensor and the cinema screen — exposure, response curve, color timing — and games earn it the same place film does: after the frame is rendered, one function per pixel. This series builds that pass, and this post builds its spine. By the end we will know exactly what a "color grade" is, mathematically, and ours will read the same world clock as everything else on the lake.

Step 1 · The tone mapFrom unbounded light to a screen

Start with the only non-negotiable stage. Our renderer works in linear HDR radiance: a pixel's value is proportional to light energy, and light has no ceiling. The sun glint on a wave facet is easily twenty times brighter than a white feather. The display, meanwhile, can show 0 to 1. Something must map one onto the other, and that something is a tone curve. Scrub through the choices:

The naive map is min(c, 1) — clamp. It is also the worst: every value past 1 lands on the same white, so the glitter path, the sun's halo, and the bright cloud rim merge into one dead plateau. Reinhard's curve, c / (1 + c), fixes the clipping by compressing all of [0, ∞) into [0, 1) — but it compresses everywhere, so the image goes flat and never actually reaches white. The curve that behaves like film is an S: a toe that eases shadows in, a near-linear middle, and a shoulder that rolls highlights off gradually. We use the standard rational fit (c·(2.51c + 0.03)) / (c·(2.43c + 0.59) + 0.14) — the same family as the renderer's built-in filmic curve. Aside · Why film has a shoulder

Photographic film responds to light logarithmically near its limits: each doubling of exposure darkens the negative by a roughly constant increment until the silver halide crystals saturate — gradually, not all at once. The shoulder of the S-curve is that chemistry. Digital sensors clip hard, which is why video that "looks digital" usually means highlights that hit a wall. We are simulating the chemistry on purpose.

Step 2 · The knobsLight on one side, paint on the other

The tone map splits the chain into two worlds, and every grading knob belongs to exactly one of them. Before the curve, values are still light, so gains behave physically. Exposure is a single multiply, counted in stops: c · 2^stops, one stop = twice the light, exactly like opening a camera's aperture. White balance is a per-channel gain — warm tilts red up and blue down — and applied here it behaves like the sun actually changing color rather than like a tinted sticker on the screen.

After the curve, values are paint: a bounded 0..1 signal that we may bend for taste. The classic three-way color corrector is lift / gamma / gain, and it is one line: out = (gain · (c + lift · (1 − c)))^(1/gamma). Look at the boundary behavior and the mystery dissolves — at c = 0 the formula returns gain · lift, so lift raises the floor (shadows); at c = 1 it returns gain, so gain scales the ceiling (highlights); gamma is a power curve that pivots on both endpoints and bends only the middle. Per-channel, that is six dozen Hollywood looks in three vectors. Contrast rotates values about a grey pivot, (c − p)·k + p with p ≈ 0.42 (18% scene grey after the sRGB transfer), and saturation is just mix(luma, c, s) — sliding each pixel along the axis between itself and its own grey. Aside · Order is not negotiable

Run saturation before the tone map and bright saturated colors blow out unevenly per channel, shifting hue as they clip — sunset orange turns yellow. Run exposure after it and you brighten paint, not light: the S-curve's shoulder has already eaten the highlight detail you were trying to reveal. Linear work on the linear side, display work on the display side. Most "my grade looks cheap" bugs are stages in the wrong order.

Step 3 · BloomWhat bright does to a lens

One physical effect refuses to stay inside its pixel. Point a real camera at the sun's glitter path and light scatters in the lens body, between the elements, even inside the eye — a halo spills out of every bright source. Painters have faked it for centuries; renderers call it bloom, and it is the single strongest "this was photographed" cue we can add. The recipe: extract everything above a threshold (in HDR, before tone mapping — brightness means something there), blur it, add it back. The blur is a Gaussian, and Gaussians have the property that makes this affordable: they are separable. A 2-D Gaussian is the product of two 1-D ones, so an N×N-tap blur becomes N taps horizontal plus N taps vertical — and running the passes on progressively downsampled copies turns a hundred-pixel halo into a handful of small blurs. Step 4 · The hour keyA grade that tells time

A film gets one grade per scene, hand-timed by a colorist. Our scene is a whole day. The honest move is the one the sky palette already made: key everything to sun elevation, not the wall-clock hour, so the look tracks the light geometry that causes it. We define a handful of stops — deep night, late twilight, blue hour, sunrise, golden hour, morning, midday — each one a full settings object (exposure, temperature, contrast, saturation, lift/gamma/gain triples, vignette, bloom drive), and smoothstep-ease between neighbors so no boundary ever shows. Night lifts the floor and drains color toward scotopic grey, the same declared lie as the night exposure boost; sunrise pushes warm gain against shadows that keep their blue; midday gets out of the frame's way almost entirely, because the strongest grade is the one you stop noticing. Aside · A grade you can name is overtuned

The temptation with a working grade is to crank it until every hour looks like a movie poster. The discipline that survives playtesting is the opposite: if a player can say "the game goes orange at sunset", the gain triple is too hot. The stops above move exposure by halves of a stop and saturation by fifteen percent — enough that dawn feels different from noon, not enough that anyone can point at why.

OnwardWhat this unlocks

We now own the frame after it is rendered: a chain that resamples nothing and recolors everything, keyed to the same clock the sun, the fish, and the score already obey. The hero up top is the whole post running live — grade and bloom following the hour, with the amount slider as the before/after switch. But a chain that only recolors is leaving the camera's best trick unused: a film camera smears what moves, defocuses what it isn't looking at, and widens when the world rushes in. Next post the chain starts moving pixels — motion blur, radial streaks, and dive-depth focus, all driven by the flight telemetry the joy pass promised the camera would someday read.
