The Breach

Every system this season was built for the next ninety seconds of scroll: the osprey circles, tucks, punches through the surface, takes a fish under Snell's ceiling, and climbs out shedding water. The dive, assembled.

This is the post the Under series exists for — honestly, it's the moment the whole project exists for. Fish Hawk's centerpiece verb is the dive, and every ingredient is finally on the shelf: a real osprey with action poses wired to springs, a flight feel that knows what a committed dive is, a surface the camera can cross, a ceiling overhead and bent light below. What's left is choreography and physics: a state machine that strings nine phases into one motion, and the honest numbers for what water does to a 1.5 kg bird arriving at fifteen meters per second. The hero above runs the full loop; the steps below take it apart.

Step 1 · The spine of itThe dive cycle

The cycle is a state machine — circle → hover → dive → strike → plunge → rise → climb → recover — where each phase sets two kinds of outputs: forces on a point body (the position the bird actually flies) and requests to the pose system (what the body does about it). Nothing here plays an animation clip; the tuck is the same spring target it's always been, triggered by a phase instead of a button.

The physics worth deriving lives in three phases. The dive is gravity plus commitment: tucked, the bird stops resisting and falls, gaining roughly v = √(2gh) — call it 11 m/s from six meters, nudged higher by a final wing push; real ospreys arrive at 15–30 m/s. The strike swaps the silhouette: feet snap forward past the head in the last meter and a half, because talons, not the beak, are the landing gear. The plunge is the violent one. Water is ~830× denser than air, so the drag that was negligible a wingbeat ago is suddenly everything: speed decays like e^(−kt) with a constant measured in fractions of a second, the bird sheds better than 3 g in the first quarter second, and a 15 m/s entry is spent by a meter and a half down. That's the physical reason an osprey can't hunt deep water — the dive buys about one body length of reach below the surface, ever. Aside · Feet-first is aim correction

The strike pose isn't theater. Refraction displaces a fish's image from the fish — apparent depth is true depth over 1.33 — and the osprey's answer is geometry: steepen the final approach until the error mostly cancels, then commit along that line with eyes closed behind the nictitating membrane. Hitting feet-first puts the grabbing surface at the front of the splash, a reversible outer toe makes the grip two-and-two around the fish, and barbed footpads (spicules) hold what the talons caught. Dive scoring in the game will charge for entry angle for exactly these reasons.

Step 2 · The payoff frameThe grab

Underwater, the cycle has a quarter of a second to close the deal — so the demo below gives you a slow-motion slider, because at full speed the grab is nearly subliminal (it is in nature, too). The camera holds in the water and watches the whole event arrive: the strike silhouette punching through the ceiling in a sleeve of bubbles, the talons closing on the fish, the turn toward the light. The fish is a deliberately minimal stand-in — a lofted body, a tail-beat in the vertex shader, real countershading — enough to be worth diving at until the ecosystem series ships real schooling fish and we swap it out behind the same two lines of interface.

One detail to watch for: once caught, the fish rides head-first. Real ospreys shuffle their grip mid-climb to point the catch along the flight direction like a torpedo, cutting its drag roughly in half — a 1.5 kg bird hauling a 300 g fish does not get to ignore aerodynamics. Aside · The membrane, again

From the strike pose to the grab, the osprey is flying blind — the nictitating membrane closed the moment before entry, and the aim was frozen at the top of the strike. Biologists clocked the whole underwater event at well under a second for most successful dives. That number is quietly load-bearing for the game: the grab can be generous about targeting because the player, like the bird, committed before the splash. Skill lives in the approach, not in underwater steering.

Step 3 · The exitWater off the wings

The breach sells the weight or nothing does. A soaked osprey is carrying a film of water that can run to a noticeable fraction of body mass on top of the fish, so the climb-out is deliberately laborious in the cycle: hover-gait flapping at full effort for modest climb, none of the easy glide of the approach. The water itself leaves in two channels we already own. The lens overlay streaks the camera if the camera came through too. And the body sheds spray: an instanced-droplet emitter samples points around the bird, launches them with the body's velocity plus a kick, flies them ballistic, and lands them on the lake — where a few of them ping the disturbance bus as micro-rain, so the rings under a climbing osprey are made by the same machinery as everything else that touches this lake. Aside · What we're still faking

Two honest placeholders remain in this scene. The splash itself is rings plus bubbles plus spray — energetic bookkeeping, not fluid — and the crown and cavity of a true talon strike are the flagship fluid series' job; the bus events these demos emit are exactly the coupling surface it will listen on. And the fish swap, as above. Both replacements land without touching the dive cycle, which is the point of building against interfaces.

OnwardWhat this unlocks

The dive cycle is the last system of the Under series, and with it Season 2's promise is one integration away: spot a real fish from altitude over the real bathymetry, dive through a real splash, and come out heavier — playable, in one valley. The underwater second is no longer a cut or a fade; it's a place with a ceiling, light, residents, and consequences, and the camera goes there and comes back every single dive. That's the loop the whole game hangs from. Next stop: wiring it into the playable milestone, where you get the stick.
