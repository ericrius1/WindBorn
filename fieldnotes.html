Field Notes

Four of Fish Hawk's five verbs are about doing. The fifth is about looking. This post builds photo mode — freeze the world, drift a free lens through the held moment, frame it against the rule of thirds, and fill a journal that earns its own entries.

Scroll down to read ↓

Fish Hawk is a small game about a big feeling: being an osprey on a lake ringed by mountains, at first light, with the whole day ahead. Soaring, spotting, diving, and building the nest are the verbs you do. But the lake we have spent this whole project building — the glass dawn, the sky that keeps time, the moonlight on black water — exists to be seen, and a world worth living in deserves a way to stop and witness it. So the last system before the game itself is the one that turns the player from a pilot into a photographer. Photo mode is three familiar tools fused into a fourth thing: a pause that's deeper than a pause, a camera that floats free of its rig, composition lines borrowed from a century of cinematography, and a journal that quietly notices what you point the lens at. None of it is hard. The trick is where the pause lives.

Step 1 · Freezing timeOne substitution, the whole world stops

Every demo on this site that animates does it the obvious way: it keeps a running total of seconds and feeds that to the waves, the wingbeats, the drifting clouds. t += dt, every frame, forever. That single line is also the reason most games can't freeze cleanly — pausing means hunting down every system that holds its own clock and stopping each one, and you always miss one. The fix is to invert the ownership. Instead of each scene accumulating its own seconds, the photo controller owns one number and the scene asks for it: t = photo.worldTime(dt). While photo mode is off, that call returns an ever-growing total, exactly as before. While it's on, it returns the same number it returned last frame. Every wave, every wingbeat, every cloud reads t and therefore stops together, in the same instant, with no per-system plumbing at all. Freezing the world becomes a one-flag operation. Aside · The clock is paused, not the camera

There's a deliberate asymmetry here. The world clock stops — the sun hangs, the waves hold, the simulated time the wet lens and the grade read goes still. But the camera's own time keeps running, because you still want to fly the lens around the frozen moment. Photo mode pauses the spine clock and the scene's worldTime; it leaves the input loop, the render loop, and the shutter flash on their own real-time tick. Freeze the subject, free the observer — that split is the entire feel of a good photo mode.

Step 2 · FramingLines a century of photographers agree on

A frozen moment still needs to be composed, and composition has a small kit of guides that show up in every viewfinder ever made. The rule of thirds drops two lines each way at 1/3 and 2/3, splitting the frame into nine cells; placing your subject on a line, or a point of interest where two cross, reads as more deliberate than dead-centre. The golden-ratio variant cuts a little tighter, at 0.382 and 0.618 — that's 1 − 1/φ and 1/φ, the two divisions of the golden section — and a centre cross helps when you do want symmetry, like a mountain mirrored in still water. We draw all of them as a screen-space overlay node, the last stage in the chain after the grade and the tone map, because guides are interface, not light: one pixel wide, a soft anti-aliased line, riding on top of the finished frame. Step 3 · The free cameraOff the rig, onto a leash

In play, the camera is a chase rig — it follows the bird, smooths its own motion, breathes its FOV with airspeed the way the joy pass tuned it. Photo mode unbolts it. Drag to look (yaw and pitch, clamped just shy of straight up and down so you never tumble), WASD to fly relative to where you're pointing, Q and E for down and up, shift to sprint. The pose seeds itself from wherever the chase camera was looking the instant you entered, so the transition is seamless — you take the wheel from exactly the framing you had.

The one unobvious piece is the leash. A free camera with no limit invites the player to fly off across the valley and discover the edges of the world — the seam where the terrain LOD gives out, the back of a billboard. Photo mode is for exploring a moment, not for leaving the scene, so the lens is tethered: it may roam a fixed radius from the point where you entered, and beyond that the position is gently clamped back onto the circle. Step 4 · The journalA notebook that fills itself

Here's where photo mode stops being a screenshot tool and becomes a game verb. Fish Hawk has no quest log and no checklist — the whole design is to read the world, not a HUD. But the world is full of moments worth catching, and we'd like the player to be quietly rewarded for catching them. So we define a set of moments, each one a tiny predicate over the state the spine and the flight model already publish: glass dawn is "before half-past five, wind under three"; the tuck is "wings folded and sinking fast"; under the mirror is "the camera is below the surface". Fire the shutter and the journal tests every unwitnessed moment against the current context, records the ones that match, and hands you the line of prose you just earned. The witnessed set persists to local storage, so the journal remembers across sessions — your field notebook fills across the in-game days, one caught moment at a time. Aside · Moments are data, not code

Each journal entry is a plain object — an id, a title, the prose you earn, and a when(ctx) function — sitting in a list. Adding a new one to chase (a double rise, a heron at the shallows, the first snow on the high ridge) is appending to an array, not threading a new flag through the game loop. That's the same discipline the rest of the project runs on: the moment knows the condition that defines it, the shutter just asks each moment whether it's true right now. The game's sense of "things worth seeing" becomes a file you can grow.

OnwardWhat this unlocks — and what comes next

That closes Glass & Grain. The chain that started as a single tone curve now grades by the hour, blooms the sun glitter, smears and focuses for speed, refracts the crossing through water, and finally hands the whole apparatus to the player as a camera they can stop the world with. Every stage reads the same spine the rest of the lake obeys, and the whole pass mounts wholesale — one object the game can switch on. Which is exactly what happens next. We have spent this entire project building systems in isolation: a surface, a bird, a flight model, a basin, a fluid sim, an underwater world, a sky, an ecosystem, a soundscape, and now this cinematic pass. The final season stops adding and starts assembling. The hunger and the daylight, the hunt without a HUD, the scored dive, the nest that grows across days — and then all fifty-some systems lit at once, holding sixty frames a second, on one lake. The game.
