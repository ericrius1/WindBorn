The Joy Pass

Same physics, same numbers, completely different bird. The final post of Lift is about the last ten percent — curves, breath, and lens — that separates a model that moves from a creature that flies.

Scroll down to read ↓

Four posts in, the flight model is mechanically done. Forces keep it honest, the stick makes it yours, the envelope gives it edges, and the wind gives it a landscape. And yet — flown back to back against the bird I'm imagining for Fish Hawk, it's not there. It moves correctly. It doesn't yet fly. The gap is what this post is for, and the hero above is its thesis: a single button that switches every tuning decision on and off at once. Fly a lap, hit the feel button, fly the same lap. The physics never changes. Everything changes.

Game feel has a reputation for being mystical. It isn't — it's a small set of signal-processing decisions applied to inputs and outputs, each one boring alone, transformative together. We'll take them in order: shaping what your hands send in, shaping what your eyes get back, and finally publishing the bird's state so every future system can listen to it.

Step 1 · InputResponse curves: precision in the middle

A linear stick spends its precision uniformly, but flying doesn't want it uniformly. Ninety percent of flight is small corrections — a few degrees of bank to recenter a thermal — and the remaining ten percent is full-lock commitment. The classic fix is an expo curve, which trades center sensitivity for edge authority while keeping the endpoints fixed: 

out = (1 − e) · x  +  e · x³

At e = 0.45, half-stick gives about 35% command instead of 50% — the middle of the stick becomes a fine instrument, and the corners still deliver everything. Pair it with the deadzone from The Stick (re-scaled so there's no jump at its edge), and a worn gamepad stops whispering phantom commands without going numb. The curve below is live — hold a key or a stick and watch your input ride it.

Step 2 · The camera livesBreathing and the wingbeat

A perfectly still camera reads as a tripod, and tripods don't have heartbeats. The joy pass adds two tiny periodic signals to the chase rig's position. The first is breathing: a few centimeters of slow vertical drift, two incommensurate sine waves so it never quite repeats. It's imperceptible — and immediately perceptible when you remove it. The second is keyed to the bird: a bob synchronized to the wingbeat clock, scaled by flap effort, so working hard feels like working hard through the camera, and settling into a glide visibly settles the whole frame.

Both signals are additive offsets on top of the smoothed rig — they never steer, so they can't fight the player. The sliders below isolate them. Magnitudes are deliberately tiny: feel lives below the threshold of conscious attention, and the moment a player can see the camera acting, it's overtuned.

Aside · The threshold rule

Almost every number in this post was tuned by the same loop: raise it until you notice it, then back off a third. Camera breathing, FOV kick, buffet shake — each is strongest just under the level where the player would name it. The craft isn't in inventing the effects (they're three lines each); it's in the restraint. Joy is mostly subtraction with good taste.

Step 3 · The lensKick on acceleration, not speed

The Stick mapped airspeed to field of view — the steady-state speedometer. The joy pass adds the transient: a kick term driven by acceleration along the flight path, smoothed just enough to not jitter. The distinction matters because feel is about derivatives. Cruising fast is a state; getting fast is an event. When you tuck into a dive the kick widens the lens during the surge — the moment of commitment gets an exclamation mark — then fades as acceleration tops out, handing off to the speed term that holds the wide look while you're fast. 

fov = 55° + 1.1·max(0, V − 9) + k·max(0, dV/dt)

Watch the demo's dive cycle with the kick at zero, then at half a degree per m/s². Same dive, same peak speed — but with the kick, the dive has an entrance. Stall buffet rides the same channel in reverse: the shake that warned you in The Envelope is scaled by the same stall blend the physics computes, so the camera trembles exactly when the wing does.

Step 4 · The contractPublish the bird

The last piece of the joy pass isn't for players — it's for the rest of this project. Everything we've built knows things other systems will desperately want: the audio season will synthesize wind noise straight from airspeed and time flap whumps off the wingbeat phase; the post-processing season wants speed for motion blur; the game season wants altitude and position for zones and journal moments. None of them should reach into the flight model's internals. So the model publishes — once per frame, into one flat, documented object: 

flightState = {
  airspeed, groundSpeed, verticalSpeed, altitude,
  bank, alpha, heading,
  flapping, flapPhase, tuck, stall,
  onWater, x, y, z,
}

Every field is a plain number (one boolean), so readers can't mutate the bird by accident, and the contract is one import with no plumbing: import { flightState } from "lib/flight" and read it whenever you like. The producer side is a single publishFlightState(body) call from whatever scene currently owns the bird. The telemetry panel below is rendered entirely from that object — it's a working preview of what a wind synthesizer will hear: airspeed for loudness and filter cutoff, flapPhase wrapping for each wingbeat, stall for buffet roar, verticalSpeed for the vario's voice.

That closes Lift. Season one set out to build a lake that looks like water, a bird that moves like a bird, and flight that feels like flight — and all three now exist as working systems with shared contracts between them: the surface reads the wind, the bird reads the water, and everything that comes next reads the bird. What this unlocks is the first playable milestone — open dawn water, a living sky, and you in it — and beyond that, the season where we finally give this lake something worth diving for: a valley, a real splash, and the world under the surface.
