The Fish

Two hundred and sixty trout, three steering rules, and one lakebed. Nothing in this school is scripted — the schooling, the cruising contour, and the rises all fall out of arithmetic any single fish could do.

Scroll down to read ↓

Fish Hawk has no minimap, no quest markers, and no glowing fish icons. That's not minimalism for its own sake — it's the design: the ecosystem is the HUD. When you're circling over the lake deciding where to hunt, the information you act on is the information a real osprey acts on: a glint where a flank catches the sun, a shadow sliding over pale sand, a ring spreading where something rose. Which means the fish can't be spawn points with a timer. They have to be a population that lives somewhere, moves somehow, and — crucially — tells on itself in ways the player can learn to read. This post builds that population: a school of underwater boids pinned to the bathymetry we derived under the lake, with the first telegraph (the glint) wired up at the end.

The plan is three steps, each one a force added to a fish's tiny brain: the three classic flocking rules, then the terrain terms that turn an abstract flock into lake fish, then the payoff on the surface.

Step 1 · Three rulesA school is not a thing

A school of fish has no leader and no plan, and that's the whole trick: schooling is an emergent behavior, which for us means we never have to animate it. Each fish runs the same three steering rules every frame, each one computed only from neighbors within a small radius: 

separation  steer away from neighbors closer than 0.7 m   // don't collide
alignment   match the average heading within 2.4 m        // swim the same way
cohesion    steer toward the local center within 3.6 m    // don't get left out

Each rule produces a desired velocity; the steering force is desired − current, clamped to a maximum turn authority (about 2.2 m/s² for our trout — fish corner well, but not instantly). Separation deserves one refinement: the push from each too-close neighbor is weighted by 1/d², so the fish about to hit you dominates the fish merely nearby. The three forces are weighted, summed, and integrated, and the speed relaxes back toward a per-fish cruise speed so the school doesn't either stall or run away with itself. Why these radii? Order matters more than the values: separation must be the shortest range and cohesion the longest, so the rules can't deadlock — push apart up close, pull together at a distance, and agree on a heading in between. Drag the weights below and find the failure modes yourself: cohesion alone collapses the school into a knot, separation alone is gas, and alignment is the magic that turns a crowd into a current.

Aside · The cost of honesty

Our school does the naive thing: every fish checks every other fish, about 20,000 pair tests for 140 fish. At a few hundred fish that's well under half a millisecond, so honesty is free. The classic upgrade — bin fish into a coarse grid, check only the 27 neighboring cells — drops the cost to nearly linear, and that's the path the full game takes if the lake ever needs thousands. The rules themselves never change; only the bookkeeping does.

Step 2 · The lakebed votesDepth preference from bathymetry

The tank demo above could be anywhere. Real lake fish are somewhere: a trout holds over structure, follows the shore contour, and avoids both the beach (too shallow, too bright, too dangerous) and the open middle (nothing to eat). We get all of that from two more forces, both powered by the canonical terrain function — the same arithmetic that built the mountains keeps the fish honest.

First, the comfort band: each fish samples the water column under itself, column = −terrainHeightAt(x, z). If the column is shallower than ~2 m or deeper than ~10 m, the fish steers along the depth gradient back toward the band — and here the cached bathymetry lattice from the Basin pays off again, because its gradientAt tells us which way "deeper" is for ~50 nanoseconds a query. The school ends up draped along the 2–10 m contour like a curtain, which is exactly where a real littoral school sits. Second, the vertical hold: a gentle spring pulls each fish toward its preferred swim depth (about 1.6 m down), clamped to stay a margin above the bed and below the surface. Together the two forces mean the shape of the lakebed is visible in the shape of the school — squeeze the comfort band with the sliders and watch a hundred and eighty fish redraw the contour for you.

Aside · Why fish hold a depth at all

Real drivers are temperature and food: lakes stratify in summer into a warm upper layer and a cold deep one, and trout ride the boundary — the thermocline — where oxygen and temperature are both tolerable, making feeding runs upward. Our single "preferred depth" number is a stand-in for all of that, and it's deliberately a parameter: at dusk the hatch post will shove it toward the surface, which is the entire drama of the next essay.

Step 3 · The telegraphGlints: fish you can hunt

Now flip to the osprey's side of the surface. From above, the water is a mirror — mostly reflection at grazing angles — and the school is invisible. What gives a shallow fish away in real life is specular: a trout's flank is a mirror too, and as the fish rolls and yaws while it cruises, that mirror sweeps past the sun-to-your-eye angle and fires a brief flash through the surface. Glints are events, not states: a fish that never turns never flashes.

We model exactly that and nothing more. Every fish shallower than about a meter gets a candidate glint: a small additive streak lying on the surface at the fish's position, stretched along its heading. Its brightness is shallowness × flash — shallowness fades the glint out by depth (more water, more absorption, less mirror), and flash is a slow per-fish cycle standing in for the body roll, raised to a power so it spikes briefly instead of glowing steadily. The result reads precisely like the real thing: scattered winks that say fish, here, shallow, without one pixel of UI. In the demo below the school has been pushed up to cruise depth; drag the hour toward sunset and the low sun makes the show better — which will matter, because dusk is when everything in this season converges.

Take stock of what the game just gained. A FishSchool you can query — every position, depth, and velocity, sixty times a second — pinned to real bathymetry, plus a surface layer that telegraphs the shallow ones to a circling player. The school also carries a rise behavior (a fish driving to the surface after floating food), but we've kept it dormant in this post, because rises deserve their own story: they're triggered by the biggest scheduled event in the valley's day. Next post, the world clock hits seven in the evening, a hundred thousand mayflies make a terrible decision, and the fish finally break the surface — each one announcing itself on the disturbance bus as a ring you'll hunt by. That's The Hatch.
