Morning Mist

Distance has a color, and dawn has a breath. This post gives the air thickness: ridges that fade by Beer–Lambert, fog that pools where the math says it must, and the lake's morning exhalation — condensing in the cold hours, burning off as the sun climbs. Press play and let it.

Fish Hawk opens on mist. That has been the plan since the first sketch: the player arrives at dawn, the lake is glass under a low white blanket, and as they learn to fly the blanket quietly leaves. It is the world clock made visible — the same clock.hour that placed last post's sun now decides how much water hangs in the air. But mist is just the loudest case of something every outdoor frame needs: air you can see across. A mountain three kilometers away does not look like a mountain thirty meters away painted small; it looks washed, lifted toward the sky color, its contrast eaten. Painters call it aerial perspective and have faked it for six centuries. We get to compute it, because we already own every ingredient.

Step 1 · Aerial perspectiveDistance, by Beer–Lambert

Recall the one-sentence sky from The Color of Air: light crossing air is attenuated by T = e^(−β·d), and what replaces it is in-scattered sky. Both halves apply to a ridge just as well as to the sky behind it. Light leaving the rock loses 1 − T of itself before reaching you; the very same air scatters skylight into the path, adding fog color in exactly the proportion it stole. One mix covers both: 

seen = mix(fogColor, surfaceColor, e^(−β·d))

That exponential is why depth reads the way it does: each kilometer multiplies the surviving contrast by the same factor. At β = 2.2×10⁻⁴/m, one kilometer keeps ~80%, three keep ~50%, and the rim of the cirque goes properly atmospheric. The fog color is not a constant grey, either — it is the skyColors(hour) palette's fog entry, so distance is warm at golden hour and slate-blue before dawn, for free. The terrain below is the real basin; drag to pan along the rim and pull the air-density slider through its range.

Aside · Why painters got it right first

Leonardo's notebooks describe painting distant things "bluer and less distinct, in proportion to their distance" — an empirical Beer–Lambert, four hundred years before Beer or Lambert. The proportionality he eyeballed is our exponent; the "bluer" is the in-scattered term being sky-colored. A good landscape painter is an analog renderer with very good priors.

Step 2 · Height fogThe exponential layer, in closed form

Uniform fog is a lie with a short shelf life: real air thins with altitude. Density follows ρ(y) = ρ₀·e^(−y/H), where the scale height H says how fast — and the lovely thing is that the integral along a straight ray has a closed form, so we never march a single step. For a ray from camera height y₀ to a point at y₁, distance d, the optical depth works out to 

τ = ρ₀ · d · e^(−y₀/H) · (1 − e^(−Δ))/Δ,   Δ = (y₁ − y₀)/H

— the plain ρ₀·d you would have guessed, corrected by where the ray sits in the column. As Δ → 0 the correction goes to 1 and we recover constant-density fog; that limit needs explicit handling in the shader or a level ray divides by zero. Two sliders below: with H large the fog is an even haze; squash it to a few meters and it pools into a fog sea in the basin with the ridges riding above — the inversion-layer shot every mountain photographer camps for. Half the column always sits below 0.69·H (that is ln 2 doing its usual work).

Step 3 · The exhaleMist on the world clock

Now make it dawn. Lake mist is height fog with a biography: overnight the land radiates its heat away, the air an arm's length above the water cools below its dew point, and the lake — still holding yesterday's warmth — feeds vapor into it. It condenses: a layer a few meters thick, dense, sitting exactly on the water. After sunrise the new sun heats the air back through the dew point and the mist evaporates from the top down, gone by mid-morning. We encode that life as a schedule, mistAmountForHour(hour): rising through the small hours, holding through first light, burning off between seven and half past nine, with a smaller return after dusk.

Structurally the mist is a second exponential layer — H of five meters against the air's nine hundred — and the two optical depths simply add before the final exponent, because that is how transparent media stack. One more honesty knob: a uniform blanket looks like dry ice, so the mist column is modulated by drifting fractal noise — wisps — advected by the one wind field, sampled at nose height. The same wind that will carry the bird carries the morning.

Aside · The declared approximation

The closed form assumed the layer is horizontally uniform; wisps break that assumption, so where exactly along the ray should the noise be sampled? We sample once, at the far end, and accept the error — a real answer means marching the ray, which is a budget we are saving for the clouds. Watch the wisps closely and you can catch the cheat; watch them casually and you never will. Most realtime fog is built from exactly this kind of negotiated surrender.

Step 4 · AssemblyOne dawn, three layers

The hero scene at the top of this page is the three steps composed: aerial perspective on the rim, a whisper of valley haze, and the scheduled mist riding the clock — over the same LakeSurface-recipe water from Glass Dawn, rebuilt here with a tint hook so the fog gets the final word on every fragment. Nothing in the scene knows about anything else; they all just read the spine. The clock turns, the sun climbs, the palette warms, the mist thins. Witness, the verb.
