Light, Bent

The dancing light on a lakebed isn't painted anywhere — it's a census of where the waves steered the sun. We derive it from the wave table we already have, then march it through the water as god rays. Click the water to strike it.

The underwater second of Fish Hawk's dive has a ceiling now — Snell's window and its mirror — but the space under it is still a void with a floor. Real lake water at ten in the morning is busy: ropes of light writhing across the sand, shafts hanging in the murk, the distance dissolving into teal, bubbles ticking upward after every disturbance. None of that is decoration. The caustics tell you the sun is out and the surface is moving; the fog tells you how far you can hunt; the bubbles tell you something just hit the water and where. This post builds all four, and the satisfying part is that the headline act — the caustics — needs no new simulation at all. It's a second derivative of the wave table we've been carrying since part one of the Mirror series.

Step 1 · The derivationCaustics are a map's Jacobian

Start with parallel sunlight hitting a rippled surface. Each point of the surface refracts its ray by Snell's law — toward the local normal — so a wave trough, curved like a magnifying glass, converges its rays, and a crest spreads them. For the gentle slopes of our lake we can linearize the bend: a facet tilted by slope ∇h deflects the refracted ray from vertical by about (1 − 1/n)·∇h. Let that ray fall d meters to the bed and it lands displaced by 

P(x) = x + β·∇h(x),    β = d·(1 − 1/n) ≈ 0.25·d

— a map from entry points to landing points. Light entered the surface uniformly, so the brightness at a landing point is just how much the map compressed area around it — one over the determinant of its Jacobian: 

I ∝ 1 / |det(I + β·H)|,    H = the Hessian of h

Where neighboring rays converge, det → 0 and the floor flares: those are the filaments. Here is the gift: our surface is a sum of sines, so its second derivatives are exact, in closed form, three multiplies per wave. No caustic texture, no photon scatter, no render target — the bed shader evaluates the same five-wave table the surface displaces with, twice differentiated, and divides. Aside · Why pool caustics look like cells

The filament network you see on a swimming-pool floor is the fold set of this exact map — mathematicians call the bright lines caustics because they're where the determinant vanishes and the intensity formally blows up (in practice, diffraction and the sun's half-degree width cap the spike; our shader caps |det| at a small epsilon for the same effect). The cell-like look comes from the map folding along closed curves: each trough-lens draws a bright loop around its own patch of bed.

Step 2 · The mediumFog, and light with a direction

Caustics put light on surfaces; the water between surfaces needs its own treatment. Distance fog underwater is Beer–Lambert wearing a different hat: along a view ray of length s, the object's light survives as e^(−σ·s) and the gap fills with the medium's glow — the teal that scattering hands back. Every underwater material here ends with that one-line mix, with σ on a slider (turbidity: gin-clear to glacial flour).

God rays are the same caustic number, marched. A shaft of underwater light is bright where the sun ray passing through it entered the surface at a focusing point — so the brightness of any point in the volume is the caustic intensity at its sun-entry point, dimmed by the sun's path down and the view path back. A full-screen pass marches each view ray in sixteen jittered steps, evaluates the (three-wave, cheaper) caustic at each sample's entry point, applies the two Beer–Lambert tolls, and adds. Aside · One sun, three readings

Hour, sun direction, and sun color all come from the same dawn-sky rig the finale scene keyed to the world clock. Drag the hour slider and three things move in lockstep: the shafts lean (entry points slide with the sun's azimuth), the caustic lanes migrate across the bed, and the whole palette warms — because all three read the one sun. That agreement is the spine doing its job; no demo here owns a private sun.

Step 3 · The exhaleBubble columns

When something punches through the surface it drags air down with it, and the lake hands the air back as a column of bubbles over the next couple of seconds. We don't simulate the entrainment — that's the fluid series' territory — but the bus tells us everything we need: the disturbance events that already make rings carry a position and an energy, and the plume subscribes like any other listener. A strike spawns a source that trickles out bubbles over ~two seconds (that's what makes a column instead of a puff), each one an instanced sphere with honest small-bubble physics: terminal rise speed in the tens of centimeters per second (drag balances buoyancy in milliseconds at this size, so we never integrate the transient), a wobble from the vortices it sheds, slight growth on the way up as the water pressure thins (Boyle's law — about 10% from two meters), and a pop at the surface.
