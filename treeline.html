The Treeline

The mountain stops being gray. Every color in this hero — meadow, beach gravel, scree, bare rock, snow — is a rule written on just two numbers: how high the ground is, and how steep. No textures, no painting, no exceptions.

Scroll down to read ↓

A real mountainside is a map of its own physics. Snow rests where it's flat enough to stay; rock shows where it's too steep for soil; trees stop at the altitude where summers are too cold to grow wood; meadow fills in what's left. Which means we don't need an artist to color the basin — we need the rules, and the terrain will paint itself. That's not just thrift. In Fish Hawk those bands are gameplay legibility: sun-warmed scree is where thermals will live, the meadow ring is where the shore life happens, and the treeline is the most readable altitude line in the valley — a natural altimeter for a game with no HUD. And because the rules are functions, the forest we plant in the next post can ask the same rules where trees belong and land exactly on the painted band.

Everything below runs per pixel in the fragment stage, reading two values the displaced mesh already carries: world height y and the surface normal's vertical component normal.y. That second number is worth a sentence: for ground with gradient ∇h, the unit normal's y-component is 

up = 1 / √(1 + |∇h|²) = cos(slope angle)   // 1 = flat ground, 0 = vertical wall

so it's a pure steepness meter, independent of which way the slope faces. up = 0.72 is a 44° pitch — remember that number, it comes back when we plant trees.

Step 1 · AltitudeThe layer cake (a useful lie)

Start with altitude alone, deliberately crude: quantize y into hard 40 m bands on a cold-to-warm ramp — lake-blue lowlands, green mid-slopes, gray heights, white crowns. The result reads instantly as a contour map, which is exactly the point: you can see that height alone already organizes the valley into believable zones. You can also see everything wrong with it. The bands are perfect horizontal stripes — cliffs get painted “meadow green” if they happen to sit at meadow height, and every band edge is a contour line a surveyor could sue over. Real mountainsides don't do stripes.

Step 2 · SlopeThe second axis

Now the other number, alone. The demo below ignores altitude entirely and maps steepness to a heat ramp: green where 1 − up is small (flat), amber through the hillsides, red where the ground approaches a wall. Look at how much structure one number recovers — the headwall cliffs, the gentle outlet valley, the talus aprons under the crests, the flat bench the lake sits on. Altitude says where on the mountain; slope says what the ground is doing there. Neither is sufficient; together they're nearly the whole story.

Step 3 · The rulesPainting with both hands

The real material is a stack of weighted layers, each weight a smoothstep on altitude, slope, or both, composited bottom-up like geology. In the order they're applied: 

meadow   base coat, mottled by a broad “moisture” noise field
forest   3 m → treeline, gentle ground only (up > 0.7)     // darker floor under the canopy
beach    a thin gravel strip hugging y = 0
rock     takes over as up drops below ~0.6, faint strata bands
scree    mid-steep band (walls shed, aprons catch), above half-treeline
snow     above the snowline, where up > ~0.6                // snow can't rest on walls
silt     below y = 0, darkening with depth

Two site-wide constants anchor the stack: the treeline at 240 m above the lake and the snowline at 330 m. Both are sliders below, and both are exported constants the rest of the game reads — move the treeline here and you've moved it for the forest, the thermals, everything.

The remaining trick is the one that makes it look real: dithering. Every threshold gets a little fine-grained noise added to its input — the snow test becomes smoothstep(snowline − 28, snowline + 28, y + 34·noise) — so band edges wander tens of meters instead of tracing contours. The same wobble field perturbs the forest edge, the beach width, the rock threshold. Hit “dither on/off” in the demo and watch the layer cake from Step 1 reappear inside the finished material; that toggle is the whole argument for the technique. The “show tree band” button highlights the forest-floor weight in blue — hold that thought, because that exact function is the planting mask next post.

Aside · What a treeline actually is

Alpine treelines track summer warmth, not winter cold: across the world's mountains, trees give out roughly where the growing-season air averages 6–7 °C, because below that, wood simply can't be built fast enough to matter. That's why treeline elevation falls as you move poleward, and why it's such a crisp line — a few degrees of lapse rate is a few hundred meters of altitude. The straggling, wind-dwarfed trees right at the edge are called krummholz, German for “crooked wood.” Our scatter rules will shrink trees toward the line for exactly that effect.

Worth pausing on what we did not do: no texture maps, no UV unwrapping, no splat maps to author or stream. The entire surface of a kilometers-wide valley is a few dozen lines of per-pixel arithmetic on two varyings the mesh was already computing — which means it works unchanged on every ring of the clipmap, at every distance, on a mesh whose vertices never stop moving. The depth-tinted shallows even get their lakebed for free: the silt layer continues under the water because the rules don't know the water is there.

The valley has its colors now, but the forest is still paint — a green weight function, not trees. An osprey needs the real thing: a canopy with gaps to read fish through, snags to perch on, groves that cast shade and break the wind. Next post we turn the painted band into a hundred thousand instanced conifers — each one standing exactly on the surface, each one placed by rules you've already met — and publish the perch points the whole rest of the game will land on. That's The Forest.
