The Crown

A weight drops, the lake throws a ring of droplets, and every one of them lands somewhere and rings the water again. Drag the time slider down and watch a splash the way a high-speed camera would.

Put a real splash next to our coupled sim and one absence screams: nothing leaves the water. The height field is a function — one height per location — so the moment water needs to exist above other water, the model is structurally out of moves. And the crown is not a garnish. Freeze any photo of a kingfisher or an osprey hitting a lake: the white ring of flung droplets IS the splash's silhouette; the rings spreading underneath are almost invisible by comparison. Fish Hawk's centerpiece dive cannot ship without it. So today we leave the grid: a pool of a few thousand particles on the GPU, born where the impact happens, paid for by its energy, and — this is the part that makes them look attached rather than decorative — launched by the simulation's own velocity field. Step 1 · The budgetEnergy buys droplets

Every disturbance on the bus carries an energy number — the same scalar the momentum impulse and (eventually) the audio synthesizer read. Spray spends it too, with square-root scaling on both axes: droplet count ≈ 90·√E and launch speed ≈ √E·(speed constants), because kinetic energy goes as v², so the velocity a budget can afford goes as its square root. A fish rise (E ≈ 0.05) buys a couple dozen lazy droplets; a full talon strike (E ≈ 1–4) buys hundreds, moving fast. Nothing else changes — same pool, same shader — so the whole continuum from plop to eruption is one slider:

Aside · A pool, not a list

The droplets live in a fixed GPU pool (4,096 slots) with a wrapping write cursor: each burst claims the next N slots, alive or not. No allocation, no compaction, no CPU round trip — a dead droplet is just a slot whose scale is zero. The cost of the whole system is therefore constant whether the air is empty or full, which is exactly the property the sim bubble taught us to demand: budgets that do not depend on how exciting the moment is.

Step 2 · The flightBallistics with drag

Airborne water is refreshingly cheap physics: gravity pulls, drag resists, nothing else. Each droplet integrates v ← (v − g·dt·ŷ) / (1 + k·dt), p ← p + v·dt — the same symplectic kick-and-drift as the wave grid, one particle per GPU thread, no neighbors to read so no ping-pong needed. The drag constant k matters more than it looks: with k = 0 droplets fly textbook parabolas and read as gravel; real droplets are light enough that air noticeably brakes them, rounding the top of every arc. Note what we deliberately leave out: droplets never see each other. Real spray is dilute almost immediately after launch — collisions between airborne droplets are rare enough that ignoring them costs nothing visible — and independence is what makes the GPU mapping perfect: one thread per droplet, no neighbor reads, no ping-pong, no ordering. Rendering rides the same buffer: each droplet is a camera-facing sprite whose position the vertex stage reads straight from the storage buffer the compute pass just wrote. The droplets are born, fly, and die without a single byte crossing back to the CPU.

Step 3 · The launchInheriting the surface

Where a droplet starts matters less than what it starts with. The crown's signature flare — droplets leaning outward as they rise — isn't a spawn-cone parameter; it's inheritance. At the moment of impact the momentum injection has the surface rushing radially outward, and real droplets are pinched off from that moving water. So the spawn kernel samples the sim's velocity field at each droplet's birth position — a storage-buffer read inside a compute shader, the same fieldNode the renderer uses — and adds it to the launch velocity. Physics decides the crown's shape: hit harder, the field rushes faster, the crown flares wider. Turn inheritance off and the same droplets become a polite, wrong, decorative fountain:

Step 4 · The rejoinRain on the bus

Every arc ends at the water. A droplet crossing y = 0 on the way down dies in the pool — but it shouldn't just vanish: each landing is a tiny impact of its own. The honest implementation would read droplet landings back from the GPU; instead we use the trick this site keeps reaching for — evaluate the same math twice. At emit time the CPU runs the identical ballistic formulas to estimate when and where a handful of representative droplets will land, and schedules small "rain" disturbances at those times — straight onto the bus, where the sim (and later the audio) hears them like any other event. Aside · Real crowns, briefly

The crown's rim breaking into regularly-spaced points (the bit that looks like an actual coronet) is a Rayleigh–Plateau instability — the rising sheet's edge is a liquid cylinder, and liquid cylinders bead up at a preferred wavelength. We don't simulate the sheet, so our crown is made of droplets from birth; at game framing the difference is invisible. The Worthington jet — the column that fires straight up when the cavity collapses — we get a free echo of, because the momentum impulse's rebound converges at the center. High-speed photography of splashes is a genuinely beautiful rabbit hole; this page is the minimum that looks true at ten meters.

OnwardWhat this unlocks

The splash now has its full vertical anatomy: rings below, crown above, rain between. What it lacks is memory. Watch any real strike and the lake stays marked for half a minute — a patch of foam drifting on the currents, stretched by wakes. And moving bodies should write longer signatures: the V-shaped wake fan that trails anything crossing water. Both are the same machinery, advected by the sim's velocity field.
