Wakes and Foam

The series finale: the osprey dives on a lake that ripples, sprays, foams, and remembers. Drag the time slider down to one-tenth and watch every system this series built fire in order.

Two systems remain between us and the strike Fish Hawk's trailer will lead with. The first is memory: our lake forgets a splash in seconds, but a real strike leaves a pale stain of foam that drifts and stretches for half a minute — it's how you can glance at a lake and read what happened ten seconds ago. The second is the signature of motion itself: the V-shaped wake fan behind anything crossing water, which our wake events hint at but we've never examined honestly. Both turn out to ride the same machinery — the velocity field we've been integrating since part two — and once they're in, there is nothing left to build before the bird goes in the water.

Step 1 · MemoryFoam rides the flow

Foam is entrained air — bubbles whipped into the water by violence — and it has a property height can't fake: it's a material, not a wave. Waves move energy through water that mostly stays put; foam IS stuff, and it goes where the water goes. So we give every cell a fourth number (the state vector is now η, vx, vz, foam — one vec4, no new buffers) and move it with the flow each substep. The mover is a semi-Lagrangian step, and it's worth knowing because it's the workhorse of all visual fluids: instead of pushing my foam downstream (a scatter — racy on a GPU, as part one taught us), each cell walks backward along its own velocity, source = position − v·dt, and gathers the foam from where its water just was. Four bilinear taps, no races, ping-pong friendly. Add exponential decay — bubbles pop — and sources: impacts and wake events deposit foam through the same Gaussian impulses as everything else. Aside · Shading the stain

Foam changes the optics, not just the color. Bubbles scatter light diffusely, so foam kills the two things that make water read as water: the Fresnel mirror and the sun glint. The surface material fades both out where foam is dense and blends toward a flat sky-lit off-white — which is why the stain reads as something on the water rather than a white decal under it.

Step 2 · The wedgeWhat a wake actually is

Now the fan. A moving body rings the water continuously — think of it as dropping pebbles as fast as it travels. Each ring expands at the local wave speed c; the body, meanwhile, is somewhere else already. If it moves slower than c, every ring outruns it and the pattern just bunches ahead. But the moment v > c, the body escapes its own rings, and the only place wavefronts can reinforce is along their common tangent — a wedge trailing the body with half-angle sin θ = c/v. One honest distinction before the demo. On deep water, dispersion (every wavelength its own speed — the formula from season one) changes the answer entirely: the envelope over all those speeds locks the wedge at 19.47° no matter how fast the boat goes. That universal constant is Kelvin's celebrated result, and it's why every duck and every ferry on a deep lake wears the same collar. Our sim is shallow-water — non-dispersive, one wave speed — so it obeys the simpler Mach-style law above, wedge narrowing as speed grows. For a splash sim this is the right trade (and near shore, where d is genuinely small, it's not even a compromise — shallow-water wakes really do behave this way). Step 3 · The ensembleLayers of a strike

The splash you see in the hero is not one effect. It's four systems firing in order on one event: the momentum impulse (part four) digs the cavity and rings the rings; the spray pool (part five) spends the energy budget on a crown; the foam channel catches the violence and remembers it; and the rejoin rain stitches the spray back into the surface. Because they all subscribe to the same bus event, they layer without knowing about each other — and you can peel them apart. Step 4 · The strikeEverything, slowly

And so: the hero. The bird from season one circles on its gait blends, tucks, falls, flares its strike pose, and hits a lake where the window has been following it the whole time. The impact is one disturb() call. The sim digs the cavity and rings outward; the crown flares with the surface rush; foam blooms and drifts; the rain patters back; and as the bird flaps off the water, its wake events stripe the surface behind it. At one-tenth speed you can watch the causality — impulse, rebound, crown, rejoin — like frames from a high-speed camera, because slow motion here isn't an effect: it's the same integrator taking smaller steps.

And the frame budget never notices any of it. That was the series' quiet thesis, so tally it at the finale: the window is 192² cells whether the lake is glass or chaos; the spray pool is 4,096 slots whether zero or four hundred are alive; foam is a fourth channel in a buffer we already owned; the mirror readback is one fixed-size copy per frame. Every system bought with a constant budget, every budget chosen on its own page. When the game later runs this strike over the full valley — terrain, forest, bird, sky — the splash will cost on the worst frame exactly what it costs on the calmest one.

OnwardWhat this unlocks

The Splash set out to build the game's centerpiece, and the pieces are now all in the library: a shallow-water window that follows the action (FluidSim), the seamless surface and provider swap, two-way coupling for anything that floats, spray, foam, wakes.
