Action Poses

The dive tuck, the air brake, the feet-first strike, the float. Each is a dozen numbers, not an animation clip — springs do all the easing, so any action flows into any other from wherever the bird happens to be.

Scroll down to read ↓

Fish Hawk's verbs each have a shape. Dive: wings swept to slivers. Land: everything flared into a parachute. Strike: talons where the eyes were looking. Rest: a folded bird riding the swell. The gait system handles how the osprey travels; these are the moments she acts — and in a game they arrive on a button press, at any phase of any wingbeat, from any previous state. Hand-authored animation clips fight that brief: a clip has a first frame, and the bird is never conveniently on it. So Fish Hawk's actions aren't clips. They're targets.

Step 1 · The tableA pose is a dozen numbers

We already speak a compact pose language without noticing. The wing listens to extension, tip lift, tip sweep, twist, and an elbow pole; the tail has spread and pitch; the body pitches and settles; the neck cranes; the legs are a few joint angles and a toe-spread scalar. A complete action is one value for each — about a dozen numbers. Our repertoire is four rows. Tuck: extension 0.30, tips swept 30 cm back, tail closed, head stretched toward the water — span costs drag and drag is the enemy. Brake: everything becomes an air brake at once — wings thrown forward and high with the leading edge pitched way up, tail fanned flat against the airflow, legs dropping. Strike: wings flung high out of the way, feet swung forward to where the eyes point, outer toes already reversing. Float: wings folded to nothing, tail held up out of the wet, body settled low. Notice what authoring looked like for each of these: not a timeline, just nudging numbers until a still photograph appeared. That's the quiet advantage of the target representation — a pose is debuggable the way a material is. If the brake's tail reads weak, there is exactly one number to raise, and it gets stronger in every approach the bird will ever fly, at any speed, from any direction. A library of clips would need re-exporting; a library of rows needs a slider.

Step 2 · The springThe only easing in the game

Snapping is obviously wrong; the question is what "smooth" should mean. Easing curves have the same flaw as clips — they restart from zero and forget your momentum. The physical answer is a damped spring: 

x″ = ω²·(target − x) − 2ζω·x′        ω = 2π·freq

Acceleration toward the target, proportional to how far away you are; friction against your velocity, proportional to how fast you're going. Integrate it semi-implicitly (update velocity first, then position — stable at game stiffnesses) and you get an easing that starts from the current value and the current velocity. Nothing ever pops, because nothing ever teleports. The damping ratio ζ is the personality knob: below 1 it overshoots and rings like flesh; at exactly 1 — critical damping — it arrives as fast as possible without overshooting; above 1 it crawls. Step 3 · The blendSprings over the gait

Now wire springs to the table. Each pose gets a weight; pressing a button moves that weight's target to one and the others to zero; springs chase the targets; and the blended pose is the weight-mixed table row applied on top of whatever the gait wanted this frame. The layering rule that makes it musical is a single field in each row: flapGain, how much of the wingbeat survives the pose. The brake keeps 20% of its flap (a flutter as she settles), the tuck and strike keep none, the neutral row keeps it all. Because the weights are spring values rather than booleans, a half-arrived brake interrupted by a strike just redirects mid-flight — there is no blend tree, no transition matrix, only physics refusing to teleport.

The update order is worth writing on the wall, because every animation bug for the rest of the project will be a question about it: gait first (what flying wants), poses mixed over it (what the action wants), the stroke's offsets added last and scaled by the blended flapGain, then a single IK solve per wing turns the final target into joints. Springs live only on the weights, one per pose — four scalars of state for the entire action system. Aside · The real strike

The hunting dive this system exists for is wonderfully specific. An osprey spots a fish from 10–40 m up, kites for a moment to line up the refraction error, then folds and drops — steeper and slower than a falcon's stoop, with mid-course corrections all the way down. In the last instant she throws her wings up and back and swings both feet forward past her head, so what enters the water first, at up to 15–30 m/s of plunge, is eight opened talons. Photographers call the posture the "osprey star". Our strike row is that star, fifteen numbers pretending to be a photograph.

Step 4 · The sequenceTriggers, not timelines

If poses are targets and springs are the easing, then a whole hunt sequence is just a schedule of trigger conditions: glide until the commitment point, tuck through the drop, brake when the water is two body-lengths away, strike at half a meter. No keyframes anywhere — change the dive's entry height and every transition re-times itself, because each phase begins wherever the previous one physically left the bird.
