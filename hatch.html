The Hatch

The world clock crosses seven in the evening and the lake changes state: mayflies up, fish up, rings everywhere. Watch one full evening play out — it loops every couple of minutes.

Scroll down to read ↓

Last post ended with a loaded gun: a school of fish carrying a dormant rise behavior, waiting for a reason to use it. This post is the reason. In the finished game, the dusk hatch is the daily event that reorganizes everything — fish that spent the afternoon holding the 2–10 m contour come up to feed, and every gulp at the surface emits a rise on the disturbance bus, which the water renders as an expanding ring. For the player circling overhead, rings are the third and best telegraph (after glints and shadows): a ring is a fish, right there, committed to the surface. For the designer, the hatch is the daily rhythm the whole game schedules itself around: hunt the evening rise, or go to bed hungry.

Mechanically the hatch is three small systems: a timing function on the world clock, a particle population with a real life cycle, and the coupling that lets fish eat particles. None of them is individually clever; the drama is entirely in the wiring.

Step 1 · The clock decidesIntensity as a function of hour

Everything keys off one pure function, hatchIntensity(hour) → 0..1: a Gaussian bump centered at 19:24 with a standard deviation of about an hour, plus a faint late-afternoon shoulder. Why dusk? Real mayflies time emergence for the failing light, the best compromise between two predators: too early and the duns drift in full view of fish below and birds above; too late and the cold kills the wing-drying. The curve below is the season's metronome — the wind field says what the weather is doing, the clock says what time it is, and this function turns time into appetite.

The important design decision is that intensity is a function, not a state machine. Nothing stores "the hatch is on": the spawner asks the function, the fish's hunger asks the function, and (next post) the swallows' activity asks the function. They can never disagree, the way ripples and grass can never disagree about the wind, because they're all reading the same arithmetic.

Step 2 · One insect, five livesThe life cycle as a state machine

A mayfly isn't one particle, it's five in a trench coat — and the stages matter to gameplay, because only some of them are food at the surface. Each particle carries a stage and a timer, and moves through the chain: 

NYMPH    swims up from the bed at ~0.25 m/s          // invisible from above, en route
DUN      rides the surface film while wings dry      // 4–12 s — THE vulnerable window
FLYING   climbs to the swarm band, dances            // bobbing column, holds station upwind
SPINNER  dips to the water laying eggs, 2–4 touches  // each dip is a tiny bus event
SPENT    flat on the film, drifting                  // easy calories until it expires

The motion per stage is a few lines each. Nymphs rise with a wriggle. Duns sit on the live surface — their height is waterHeightAt(x, z, t), so they bob with the ambient waves — and drift at a small fraction of the wind. Fliers run a damped spring toward a personal altitude in the swarm band with a sinusoidal dance on top, thrusting gently upwind to hold station (which is why a real swarm streams like a flag: every insect faces the wind). Spinners descend, kiss the water, and bounce — and each kiss may emit a rain-kind disturbance with raindrop energy, because to the water an egg-laying touch and a drizzle drop are the same event. Emergence position isn't random either: nymphs of the big lake species live in silt at 0.5–6 m, so spawn sites are rejection-sampled against the bathymetry once at startup. Below, the whole conveyor runs up close with each stage tinted — drag the hour and watch the population pipeline fill and drain.

Aside · The oldest wings in the air

Mayflies (order Ephemeroptera, "short-lived wings") have been doing this for over 300 million years — before birds, before pterosaurs, almost before anything else flew. They're also the only insects that molt after getting wings: the dun that escapes the water molts once more into the glassy-winged spinner that mates, lays, and dies, often inside a day. Adults can't eat; they don't even have working mouths. The entire airborne phase is reproduction on a deadline, which is why the hatch is so punctual — and so feastable.

Step 3 · Rings on the busFish meet flies

Now connect the layers. Every frame, the hatch hands the school a list of surface food — duns and spent spinners, the floating stages. The school update takes a hunger argument (the demo gives you a slider; the game passes hatchIntensity(clock.hour)), and each cruising fish shallower than ~3.5 m rolls a small chance per second, scaled by hunger, to commit to a rise: pick the nearest floating fly within reach, drive at the surface, and on arrival — 

disturb({ kind: "rise", x, z, energy: 0.04 + 0.04·size, radius: 0.12 + 0.3·size })

That call is the entire interface between the fish and the rest of the game. The water surface subscribes and draws an expanding capillary ring, exactly as it did for the osprey's landing; the audio season will subscribe and synthesize the plip; and the hunt will read the school's recent-rise list to score your approach. The fish never knows who's listening. After the gulp it eats the fly (the hatch removes the particle — this is a real transaction, not an animation), then dives on a refractory cooldown so no fish machine-guns the surface. Rises cluster where duns cluster, duns cluster where nymph habitat is, and nymph habitat is bathymetry — so the rings you'll hunt by are, four links down a chain, the shape of the lakebed showing through.

Aside · Reading rises like a fly angler

Anglers classify rise forms the way we're teaching the player to: a slow head-and-tail roll means fish sipping spent spinners; a splashy rise means chasing emergers before they escape; a subtle bulge with no break means feeding just under the film. The information density of a ring is real — size, sharpness, and rhythm all say what the fish is doing. Our single ring-per-rise is the first rung of that ladder, with energy already scaled by fish size so bigger fish make bigger promises.

The lake now has a daily appointment. Fish hold their contour all afternoon, the clock leans into evening, the swarm lifts off, and the surface starts printing targets — no UI, just consequences. But fish aren't the only ones who keep this appointment. Swallows hawk the same swarm from the air, writing their own dotted lines on the water; geese commute over it at altitude in a V we'll derive from wingtip physics; and something much bigger is circling with opinions about your future catches. The airspace gets crowded next, in Other Wings.
