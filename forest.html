The Forest

A hundred thousand trees, five draw calls. One tiny conifer is stamped across the valley by deterministic rules — altitude, slope, clumping — and the dead ones matter most: snags publish the perch points an osprey game runs on.

Scroll down to read ↓

Last post painted a forest-floor band onto the mountainside and promised it was a planting mask. Time to collect. Fish Hawk needs a real canopy, and not for scenery: trees are how the valley gets scale when you're flying (a bare mountainside reads as miniature; a forest with 10-meter trees tells your eye the truth), groves and clearings are the texture you navigate by, and a handful of standing dead trees — snags — are where the whole game loop lands. The osprey eats on a perch, builds its nest on the biggest snag, and launches every hunt from one. So this post has two products: a forest you can see, and a list of perch coordinates the rest of the series will treat as level data.

Two constraints shape everything. The frame budget says the forest must cost almost nothing — we'll spend tens of thousands of trees, not tens of thousands of draw calls. And determinism says the forest must be the same forest forever, on every machine, with no stored level file: like the terrain itself, every tree position must be a pure function of coordinates and a seed. Learn where the gap in the canopy is, and it's there tomorrow.

Step 1 · One treeFifty-two triangles of conifer

When something will be drawn a hundred thousand times, its triangle count is the only beauty metric that matters. Our conifer is the archetype with nothing left to remove: a five-sided tapered cylinder for the trunk and three stacked open cones for the canopy, darker at the bottom tier where a real spruce holds its oldest needles — about 52 triangles, colored per vertex so there's no texture to fetch. The geometry is built unit-height (base at y = 0, tip at 1), which buys a clean trick: the instance's scale factor is the tree's height in meters. And because a conifer is rotationally symmetric, instances don't even need a rotation — position and scale tell the whole story. Spin it up close below; it is not a hero asset, and at thirty meters among ten thousand siblings, it absolutely is.

Step 2 · A hundred thousandInstancing

The reason forests are cheap in 2026: the expensive unit of GPU work is the draw call — validating state, binding buffers, kicking off a pass — not the triangle. Instancing submits one geometry once, plus a buffer of per-instance transform matrices, and the GPU stamps the whole population in a single call. A hundred and fifty thousand of our conifers is under eight million triangles, well inside budget for one draw — drag the slider below from a thousand trees to all of them and watch the frame time readout shrug.

The trees aren't statues, either. The material leans each canopy along the wind, with deflection growing as height-squared so tips sway more than trunks (in unit space, which makes tall instances sway proportionally further in meters — exactly right for free). The sway direction and amplitude track the one shared wind field every frame, so when a gust darkens the lake, the shore pines lean with it. Per-tree variation costs nothing: a hash of the built-in instance index gives each tree its own sway phase, tint, and brightness — no extra attributes, no uploads.

Step 3 · The rulesWhere trees actually grow

Step 2's forest is a jittered grid on a flat plane — a tree farm. Real placement is ecology, and ecology here is three rejection filters run over a candidate lattice. Every 3 m cell across the valley proposes one candidate, jittered inside its cell by the same integer hash that builds the terrain — a million candidates, each accepted or rejected by pure arithmetic: 

altitude  3 m < h < treeline − 30·hash      // above the wet beach, below a ragged top edge
slope     normal.y ≥ 0.72                    // nothing roots past ~44°
clumping  fbm(x/95, z/95) < threshold        // groves and clearings, not uniform fuzz

The altitude ceiling subtracts up to 30 m of per-tree hash so the treeline frays naturally instead of ruling a line across the mountain, and trees within the last 70 m below the line shrink to barely half height — krummholz, as promised last post. The clump filter is a broad noise field with a keep-threshold: where the field runs high, no trees, and a clearing opens. Filters run against a cached bilinear height lattice (a ~50 ns query), and only the survivors pay for the exact analytic height, so every trunk sits on the true surface, not an approximation of it. Toggle the rules below and watch each one earn its keep — slope off plants cliffs, altitude off drowns trees, clumping off is uniform fuzz.

Aside · Determinism as a feature

Nothing in the scatter calls Math.random(). Every decision — keep or cull, jitter, height, snag-or-tree — is a hash of the candidate's integer cell coordinates and a seed. That means the forest needs no save file, costs no download, and can be regenerated lazily by any system that wants it: the fish AI can ask which shore is shaded, the audio season can ask where wind hits canopy, and both will agree with the renderer down to the last sapling, because they all ran the same arithmetic.

Step 4 · SnagsPerches are level data

Now the payoff for the game. A small fraction of shoreline trees — about a third of a percent, hash-decided like everything else — die standing and become snags: bare weathered spars with a few stub branches, 15% taller than their living neighbors. Each snag's geometry carries metadata: the spar top and every branch tip are recorded as offsets in unit-snag space, so scaling and rotating an instance also places its perch points in the world. The library publishes the full list — every perch in the valley with a position, a kind, and a facing — plus one hand-picked landmark: THE nest snag, a grand dead pine on the north-shore promontory, top spar facing the open water. The landing controller gets targets, the future nest post gets its home, and the hunt gets launch points, all from data that was free once the geometry existed.

Aside · Real ospreys love dead trees

The snag bias is ornithology, not convenience. Ospreys overwhelmingly nest and perch on dead-topped trees near water: a live conifer's crown is too dense to land a 1.6 m wingspan in, while a snag offers clear approach lines, a commanding view of the water, and bare wood for a nest that can grow to two meters across after years of additions. Where snags are scarce, ospreys famously settle for utility poles and channel markers — anything dead, tall, and near fish.

The hero scene up top is the whole system assembled — the full deterministic forest, snags included, plus hash-scattered boulders on the talus and grass tufts in the shore meadow, five instanced draw calls in total, every population swaying to the one wind.
