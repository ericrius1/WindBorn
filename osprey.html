The Osprey

Fish Hawk's protagonist, built from formulas you can read: a body lofted from rings, wings from strips and feather planes, and the field marks painted on face by face. No sculpting app, no imported model — a table of numbers becomes a bird.

Scroll down to read ↓

Fish Hawk is a game about being an osprey, which means the osprey is the one mesh you will stare at for the entire runtime. She perches in the foreground of every screenshot, dives through the center of every splash, and floats in the middle of the photo mode. While The Mirror builds the lake she hunts over, this series builds her — and everything later in the season (the folding wing, the wingbeat, the dive poses, the water landing) will animate exactly the vertices we place today. So today is about placing them well.

The ground rule for this whole project: geometry comes from code, not from a sculpting session we can't reproduce. Every vertex of this bird is the output of a formula with named inputs, which means the bird is tunable — make the beak 2 mm longer, re-run, done — and it means you can read this post and rebuild her from scratch. The single source of truth is a parts list: a table of tapered capsules, one row per body segment, each with a pivot, a far end, and two flesh radii. Real proportions throughout — about 1.5 kg of bird, 1.6 m of wingspan, in meters, +y up. The rows of that table will become joints in the next post; today they tell us how thick the flesh is at every station.

Step 1 · The loftRings on a wire

The oldest trick in hull-building, and still the best one for bodies: draw the spine as a curve, slide cross-sections along it, and skin them. Our spine is a Catmull-Rom curve through eleven stations, from the under-tail coverts up over the back of the skull to the base of the beak. Each station carries two radii — the body is an ellipse in cross-section, wider than it is tall at the keel — plus an optional vertical offset so the belly can hang below the centerline. A ring vertex is one line of math: 

p(θ) = center + x̂·cos(θ)·rx + ŷ·(sin(θ)·ry + yOff)

where x̂ is world +x (the bird never rolls her own spine) and ŷ is the spine tangent crossed with x̂, so rings stay perpendicular to the curve without ever twisting. Between stations, both the centers and the radii interpolate smoothly — the same Catmull-Rom basis works on scalars as well as points. Stitch each ring to the next with two triangles per segment, fan the ends closed, and the torso, neck, and head come out of one loop. Drag the sliders below and watch resolution become a budget knob: 26 rings by 18 segments is the whole body.

Aside · Why the facets stay

After lofting we deliberately un-weld every triangle so each face owns one flat normal and one flat color. Smooth shading would average those normals and make the bird look like a balloon; flat facets read as feathers, hide the low polygon count instead of apologizing for it, and let the painter give every face a tiny brightness jitter — the difference between "brown surface" and "brown plumage" is about six percent of per-face noise.

Step 2 · The wingStrips around an airfoil

A wing is not a tube, but it is still a loft — just with a different ring. Instead of an ellipse, the cross-section is a thin airfoil: a nose point, three stations across the underside, a trailing edge, and three back across the top. Sweep that eight-point ring along the arm — shoulder to elbow to wrist to tip, straight out of the parts table — and quad strips close it into a surface. The chord shrinks as you go out (28 cm of covert at the body, a few centimeters at the tip), and the chord direction always sits perpendicular to the local arm direction, so the planform keeps the crook at the wrist that makes the silhouette.

The membrane only carries the leading half of the wing, though. The trailing edge belongs to feathers, and feathers are gloriously cheap: each one is a tapered plane — a quill line and two vane edges, widest mid-feather, rounded at the tip — built as two layers a millimeter apart, because a feather seen from above is dark chocolate and the same feather seen from below is pale cream. Nine primaries fan from the hand, rotating from straight-back to almost along-the-wing-axis so the outermost ones make the slotted fingertips; ten secondaries hang off the forearm in a parallel band; three broad tertials close the gap to the body. They are separate meshes on purpose — in part three each one gets its own spring and lags the wingbeat individually.

Step 3 · The detailsHeads, tails, feet

Everything else on the bird is the same two tools pointed at smaller problems. The beak is a four-station loft whose spine curve dives at the end — the hook comes from the curve, not from any special-case code. The eyes are riders: a faceted yellow iris ball, a black pupil, a white glint, parented to the head at the anchor the table calls EYE_L. The tail is twelve feather planes sharing one pivot at the tail base; fanning them is one rotation per feather, so "spread the tail" is a single scalar from closed bundle to full fan. Legs are little lofts — white feathered trousers, bare blue-gray tarsus, a squat foot — and each of the four toes is a three-station loft of its own, tipped with a black cone of talon, pitched down into whatever the bird is gripping.

Aside · The reversible toe

Ospreys are one of the few raptors with a reversible outer toe: on a perch they stand three-forward-one-back like any hawk, but at the moment of a strike the outer toe swings around so the foot grips two-and-two, like an owl. Two opposed pairs of talons are a far better cage for a writhing fish — and the soles are covered in spiny scales called spicules for the same reason. In our rig the reversal is one rotation on the outer toe joint, and the strike pose in part four will throw it automatically.

Step 4 · The painterField marks, then the silhouette test

Color goes on during construction, when every vertex still knows where it is: its parameter u along the loft, its angle θ around the ring, its position in the world. The painter is a stack of rules in those coordinates. Torso: dark chocolate above a soft waterline, white below — the smoothstep edge rides slightly higher on the flanks so the white wraps up the sides the way real plumage does. A speckled band across the upper chest (a hash function deciding feather by feather) is the necklace. The head is white with a dark wedge climbing the nape, and the bandit mask is geometry: a band of stripe color around the line segment that runs from the beak gape through the eye toward the neck, applied only on the sides of the head. Underwing: cream, with a dark carpal patch painted by distance to the wrist. And the last quarter of every wing fades to near-black, because an osprey's wingtips are dipped in ink.

The test for all of it is the one birders use: kill the detail and check the shape. Hit the backlight button below. White body, long narrow wings cranked into an M, black wrists, barred tail — if a birder couldn't call "osprey!" from the dark shape alone, the mesh isn't done. She can: the silhouette survives with zero field marks, which is exactly what the game needs when the bird is a speck against a bright dawn sky.

What we have tonight is a statue — a good statue, about nine thousand triangles, cheap enough to draw a dozen of. What it cannot do yet is the thing that makes it an osprey: pull those long wings into a Z against its body, or reach them out to full span between two wingbeats. The parts table already knows everything a skeleton needs — every row has a pivot and a parent. Next post, the rows become joints, the law of cosines becomes a wing, and the statue learns to fold.
